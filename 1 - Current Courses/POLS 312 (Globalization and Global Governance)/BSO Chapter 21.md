# Chapter 21 — The United Nations
### 21.1 Introduction
- UN created in **1945** after WWII, reflecting hope for a just and peaceful global community
- Only global institution with legitimacy from **universal membership**
    - Mandate covers security, economic/social development, human rights, environmental protection
    - Includes central system (New York), specialized agencies (WHO, ILO), funds/programmes (UNICEF, UNDP)
- Fundamental tension: UN was created **by states for states**, but must also protect the needs/interests of people
    - State sovereignty vs. international responsibility
    - How far can the UN intervene in domestic affairs?
- UN activities have expanded beyond inter-state conflict
    - Now addresses non-state threats, political/economic/social conditions within states
    - Greater involvement in development and human rights
- Effectiveness remains contested
    
    - Iraq War (2003): US/UK failed to obtain clear Security Council authorization
        
    - Libya (2011): controversy over implementation of Security Council mandate
        
    - Syria (2011–) and Ukraine (2022): Security Council unable to agree on effective responses
        
- Central question: **Can the UN balance state sovereignty, great power politics, and universal interests?**
    

### 21.2 A brief history of the United Nations and its principal organs

- Established **24 October 1945** by 51 countries
    
    - 193 members by 2022; South Sudan (2011) was the newest
        
    - Members agree to obligations of the **UN Charter**
        
- 4 purposes of the UN
    
    1. Maintain international peace and security
        
    2. Develop friendly relations among nations
        
    3. Cooperate in solving international problems and promoting human rights
        
    4. Harmonize the actions of nations
        
- Charter recognizes both state rights and peoples' needs
    
    - Article 1(2): equal rights and self-determination of peoples
        
    - Article 2(7): limits UN intervention in domestic affairs
        
    - Chapter VI: peaceful settlement of disputes
        
    - Chapter VII: action against threats to peace, breaches of peace, aggression
        
    - Article 42: Security Council may authorize military action
        
    - Article 99: Secretary-General can bring threats to international peace to the Security Council
        

#### 21.2.1 The League of Nations and the creation of the UN

- UN designed to correct weaknesses of its predecessor, the **League of Nations**
    
    - League established after WWI to prevent future wars
        
- League's weaknesses
    
    - No clear division of responsibility between Council and Assembly
        
    - Both could only make recommendations, not binding resolutions
        
    - Decisions required unanimity
        
    - States could reject recommendations
        
    - No mechanism to coordinate military/economic action against aggressors
        
    - Major powers, including the US, were not members
        
- League failed to address several acts of aggression before WWII
    
- UN reforms
    
    - Security Council given primary responsibility for international peace/security
        
    - Security Council decisions can be binding
        
    - Recognized the special role of great powers through permanent membership and veto
        
    - Greater emphasis on economic and social advancement
        

#### 21.2.2 Principal organs of the United Nations

- **6 principal organs** (see Fig. 21.1, p. 327)
    

1. **Security Council**
    
    - Main responsibility: maintaining international peace and security
        
    - 15 members: 5 permanent (P-5) + 10 non-permanent
        
    - P-5: US, UK, France, Russia (formerly USSR), China
        
    - Decisions require 9/15 votes, including agreement of all permanent members
        
    - Permanent members have **veto power**
        
    - Can authorize:
        
        - Peaceful dispute settlement (Chapter VI)
            
        - Ceasefires and peacekeeping
            
        - Economic sanctions and arms embargoes
            
        - Collective military action (Chapter VII)
            
    - Also recommends new Secretary-General and admission of new members
        
2. **General Assembly**
    
    - All UN member states, one vote each
        
    - Two-thirds majority for major issues (peace/security, membership, budget)
        
    - Simple majority for other matters
        
    - Decisions generally recommendations, not binding
        
    - Can consider any matter within the Charter
        
    - Exception: Fifth Committee budget decisions are binding
        
3. **Secretariat**
    
    - Carries out substantive and administrative work
        
    - Led by the **Secretary-General**
        
    - Approximately 37,000 staff worldwide (figure in textbook)
        
    - Primarily bureaucratic, but has diplomatic influence
        
    - Article 99 allows Secretary-General to bring threats to peace/security to the Council
        
    - António Guterres became ninth Secretary-General in January 2017
        
4. **Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)**
    
    - Coordinates UN economic and social work
        
    - Operates under overall authority of General Assembly
        
    - Consults with NGOs
        
    - Includes functional commissions (ex. Commission on Status of Women) and regional commissions (ex. Economic Commission for Africa)
        
5. **Trusteeship Council**
    
    - Supervised 11 trust territories administered by 7 member states
        
    - Goal: prepare territories for self-government or independence
        
    - All territories achieved self-government or independence by 1994
        
    - Last was Palau (formerly administered by US)
        
    - Suspended operations in November 1994
        
6. **International Court of Justice (ICJ)**
    
    - Main judicial organ of the UN
        
    - Judges elected jointly by General Assembly and Security Council
        
    - Decides disputes between states
        
    - State participation is voluntary, but decisions must be followed if a state agrees to participate
        
    - Provides advisory opinions to UN organs and specialized agencies
        

- UN system also includes:
    
    - **Specialized agencies**: WHO, ILO, UNESCO, IMF, World Bank, etc.
        
        - Have their own constitutions, budgets, executive heads, and assemblies
            
        - Independent of central UN management
            
    - **Funds and programmes**: UNDP, UNICEF, UNEP, etc.
        
        - More directly supervised by General Assembly
            
        - Largely funded through voluntary contributions
            

#### 21.2.3 Membership and Security Council reform

- UN membership expanded significantly through **decolonization**
    
    - 51 members (1945) → 144 (1975) → 193 (2022)
        
- Early membership disputes reflected Cold War rivalry
    
    - No new members admitted between 1950–55 due to US–USSR disagreement
        
- China representation dispute
    
    - Republic of China (Taiwan) initially represented China
        
    - People's Republic of China claimed to be the legitimate representative
        
    - General Assembly Resolution 2758 (1971) recognized PRC as China's representative
        
    - Taiwan's 2007 membership application was denied
        
- Two permanent non-member observer states:
    
    - Holy See (1964)
        
    - Palestine (2012)

>

>

>

>

>         
>     - Disagreement over regional representation
>         
>     - EU representation vs. individual European states
>         
>     - P-5 unlikely to surrender veto power
>         
> - Some procedural changes have improved transparency and accountability, but major reform remains unlikely
>     

- **Key tension:** UN combines universal equality of states with unequal power among the great powers
    

### 21.3 The United Nations and the maintenance of international peace and security

- UN effectiveness shaped by the global political environment
    
- During the **Cold War**
    
    - US–USSR rivalry repeatedly paralyzed Security Council
        
    - Both powers used veto when major interests threatened
        
    - Charter's proposed multilateral military force never materialized
        
- UN developed alternative mechanisms for security:
    
    1. Security Council authorizes states/coalitions to act on its behalf
        
        - Ex. Korean conflict (1950), Gulf War (1990)
            
        - Military action principally undertaken by US and allies
            
    2. **Peacekeeping**
        
        - Not explicitly mentioned in UN Charter
            
        - Classical peacekeeping based on Chapter VI
            

#### 21.3.1 Classical peacekeeping

- Traditional peacekeeping characteristics
    
    - UN force deployed between parties after ceasefire
        
    - Under UN command
        
    - Host state consent required
        
    - Weapons used only in self-defence
        
    - Major powers excluded from the force
        
- First used in **1956 Suez Crisis**
    
    - UN force facilitated withdrawal of British/French forces
        
    - Positioned between Egyptian and Israeli forces
        
- Other examples: Cyprus (Green Line), Golan Heights
    
- Classical peacekeeping differed from more robust post-Cold War operations
    
- Exception: **UN Operation in the Congo (1960–64)**, an early robust mission
    

#### 21.3.2 Increased attention to conditions within states

- During Cold War, UN generally avoided domestic affairs to preserve international stability
    
    - State sovereignty prioritized over individual justice
        
    - Ex. Jean Kirkpatrick (1979): tolerate authoritarian anti-communist regimes in Latin America
        
- By the 1990s, growing belief that internal injustice could threaten international peace
    
    - Political/civil rights and basic needs (food, water, health care, accommodation) became security concerns
        
    - Internal justice increasingly seen as part of national and international security
        
- Reasons for the change
    
    1. **End of Cold War**
        
        - Reduced East–West rivalry and ideological constraints
            
        - Greater willingness to question domestic state practices
            
    2. **Decolonization**
        
        - UN prioritized statehood and independence over justice within new states
            
        - Recognition of new states did not require adequate economic performance, defensibility, or justice for citizens
            
        - Local elites could claim popular support even when unrepresentative
            
        - Charles Beitz, Michael Walzer, and Terry Nardin challenged unconditional statehood
            
        - State legitimacy should depend partly on how governments treat their citizens
            
    3. **Changing understanding of security**
        
        - Civil conflict, refugee flows, humanitarian emergencies, human rights violations, poverty, inequality all threaten global peace
            
- Post-Cold War peacekeeping expanded rapidly
    
    - 1994: nearly 80,000 military personnel in UN operations, 7 times the 1990 figure
        
    - Missions increasingly addressed civil wars, not just interstate conflict
        
    - Greater willingness to use force under Chapter VII
        
- 1992: Secretary-General Boutros Boutros-Ghali published **An Agenda for Peace**
    

> **Box 21.4 An Agenda for Peace (1992)**
> 
> Five interconnected UN roles:
> 
> 1. **Preventive diplomacy**: confidence-building, fact-finding, preventive deployment
>     
> 2. **Peacemaking**: bring hostile parties to agreement through peaceful means
>     
> 3. **Peace enforcement**: Chapter VII action, can occur without consent
>     
> 4. **Peacekeeping**: UN presence deployed with consent of all parties
>     
> 5. **Post-conflict peacebuilding**: develop social, political, economic infrastructure to prevent renewed violence
>     

- Problems with expanded peacekeeping
    
    - Peacekeepers increasingly targeted by belligerents
        
    - Ex. Somalia, former Yugoslavia, South Sudan
        
    - Congo: UN support for national army operations contributed to reprisals
        
    - Late 2021: approximately 88,000 personnel in 12 ongoing UN peacekeeping operations
        

#### 21.3.3 Sovereignty and non-intervention

- Increased focus on individual rights created tension with **state sovereignty**
    
- Traditional sovereignty
    
    - States are equal under international law
        
    - No higher authority over the state
        
    - Governments have exclusive jurisdiction within their borders
        
    - Article 2(7) protects domestic jurisdiction
        
- Intervention = deliberate action by an outside agency, without state consent, to change a government's functioning, policies, or goals
    
- Historically, intervention was common
    
    - US claimed intervention rights in its hemisphere until 1933
        
    - Soviet **Brezhnev Doctrine** justified intervention in socialist states to protect socialism
        
    - Britain intervened against slavery, including stopping ships and imposing treaty conditions
        
    - 1878 Berlin Conference agreements imposed minority-rights obligations on some Eastern European states
        
- Debate:
    
    - **For intervention:** statehood should be conditional on protecting citizens' rights
        
    - **Against intervention:** powerful/richer states may impose their interests on weaker states
        
- 1991 General Assembly Resolution A/RES/46/182
    
    - Humanitarian assistance should generally require consent of affected state
        
    - Wording ("in principle") left some room for intervention without consent
        
- **2005 World Summit Outcome Document**
    
    - If national authorities manifestly fail to protect populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, and peaceful means are inadequate, collective action may occur through Security Council under Chapter VII
        
    - Reflects ideas later associated with **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)** (Ch. 33)
        
- Examples:
    
    - **Kosovo (1999):** NATO intervened without Security Council mandate because Russia threatened a veto
        
    - **Libya (2011):** Security Council Resolution 1973 authorized no-fly zone and "all necessary measures" to protect civilians
        
    - **Iraq (2003):** legality and motives of intervention disputed
        
    - **Afghanistan (2001):** Security Council recognized US right of self-defence after 9/11
        
    - **Darfur (2007):** UN waited for Sudanese government consent before deploying peacekeepers
        
    - **Syria (2011–):** Russia and China vetoed proposed sanctions, arguing they could enable foreign military intervention
        
- Increasing UN involvement within states suggests movement toward **global governance**, away from unconditional sovereignty
    
- However, sovereignty and non-intervention remain important principles
    

#### 21.3.4 From peacekeeping to peacebuilding

- 2004: Kofi Annan established High-Level Panel on Threats, Challenges, and Change
    
    - Emphasized interconnectedness of security, development, and human rights
        
    - These issues mutually reinforce one another
        
    - Led partly to creation of Peacebuilding Commission
        
- 2015 peace and security reviews
    
    - UN operations struggling to keep pace with changing causes of violence
        
    - Recommended:
        
        - Better intergovernmental coordination
            
        - Greater focus on political solutions
            
        - Predictable financing
            
        - Gender inclusivity
            
        - More flexible peace operations
            
- 2017: UN Office of Counter-Terrorism (UNOCT) established
    
- 2019: Guterres reorganized peace/security units to improve regional integration and transitions out of peace operations
    
- **UN Peacebuilding Commission**
    
    - Established December 2005
        
    - Advisory subsidiary body of General Assembly and Security Council
        
    - Coordinates post-conflict recovery strategies
        
    - Created because countries such as Liberia, Haiti, and Somalia relapsed into violence after peace agreements/peacekeeping
        
    - 31-member organizational committee
        
    - Peacebuilding Support Fund assists early recovery
        
    - Examples of country-specific work: Burundi, Central African Republic, Sierra Leone
        
- UN peace/security record is **mixed**
    
    - Greater international responsibility for serious abuses
        
    - Failures in Somalia, Rwanda, former Yugoslavia
        
    - Disagreements over Iraq, Syria, Myanmar, Ukraine, Yemen
        
    - Growing use of Security Council veto
        
    - Regional organizations, ad hoc coalitions, and hybrid operations increasingly involved
        
    - No consensus on terrorism and small arms
        
    - Allegations of sexual exploitation and abuse by UN peacekeeping personnel
        

> **Case Study 21.1 The UN and conflict in Syria**
> 
> - Conflict began in 2011 after Syrian government violently suppressed pro-democracy demonstrations against Bashar al-Assad
>     
> - Developed into conflict between government and opposition groups
>     
> - External powers backed different actors:
>     
>     - Russia and Iran supported Syrian government
>         
>     - US and other countries initially supported some opposition groups
>         
>     - Islamic State seized territory from 2013
>         
>     - US-led coalition conducted airstrikes against Islamic State
>         
>     - Turkey conducted operations against Syrian Kurdish groups
>         
> - October 2011: France, Germany, Portugal, UK proposed Security Council resolution condemning human rights abuses and raising possible Chapter VII action
>     
>     - Russia and China vetoed
>         
>     - Russia feared regime change similar to Libya
>         
>     - Russia subsequently vetoed 15 more resolutions, sometimes with China
>         
> - UN did pass resolutions concerning humanitarian access, assistance, and peace talks
>     
> - Human Rights Council established Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Syria (2011)
>     
>     - Investigated human rights violations
>         
>     - Reported 38 instances of chemical weapons use, including 32 attacks by Syrian government (figure cited in textbook)
>         
> - UN brokered Geneva peace talks, but no agreement on key issues, especially Assad remaining in power
>     
> - By the time covered in the textbook:
>     
>     - Over 500,000 killed
>         
>     - More than half of pre-war population of 22 million displaced
>         
>     - Over 6 million refugees
>         
> - **Main lesson:** great power rivalry and Security Council vetoes can prevent effective international action, even during major humanitarian crises
>     
> - Q1: Why has the UN faced such difficulties responding to Syria?
>     
> - Q2: What lessons apply to conflicts such as Yemen and Ukraine?
>     

> **Case Study 21.2 UN peacekeeping in the Congo**
> 
> - Two major missions:
>     
>     - **ONUC (1960–64)**
>         
>     - **MONUC (1999–2010)** → renamed **MONUSCO (2010–present)** in the textbook
>         
> - Congo became independent on 30 June 1960
>     
>     - Belgian colonialism left the country underdeveloped and politically divided despite mineral wealth
>         
>     - Katanga declared independence with Belgian mining interests and troop support
>         
> - Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba appealed to UN for help
>     
>     - Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld invoked Article 99
>         
>     - Security Council authorized ONUC to oversee foreign troop withdrawal and support government restoration of law/order
>         
> - ONUC deployed just under 20,000 troops at its height
>     
>     - Maintained territorial integrity and helped remove foreign troops
>         
>     - However, enormous political, human, and financial costs
>         
>     - Accused of serving US foreign policy during Cold War
>         
>     - Lumumba assassinated in January 1961
>         
>     - Hammarskjöld died in plane crash while negotiating ceasefire (September 1961)
>         
>     - Mission contributed to UN financial crisis
>         
> - MONUC established in 1999 after Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement
>     
>     - Initially monitored ceasefire
>         
>     - Mandate later expanded
>         
>     - Renamed MONUSCO in 2010, authorized to use all necessary means to protect civilians and support stabilization
>         
>     - Just under 18,000 personnel in 2021
>         
> - Accomplishments:
>     
>     - MONUC supported the largest/most complex electoral process ever supported by a UN mission (2006)
>         
>     - Maintained international attention on gender-based violence
>         
> - Criticisms:
>     
>     - Conflict and human rights abuses continue in eastern Congo
>         
>     - Allegations of sexual abuse by UN troops
>         
>     - Tensions with Congolese government
>         
>     - Criticism for supporting Congolese army despite abuses by army personnel
>         
> - **Main lesson:** peacekeeping can achieve important objectives but struggles with long-term state-building and political stability
>     
> - Q1: Was deploying ONUC a mistake?
>     
> - Q2: Should UN peace operations include state-building objectives?
>     

> **Key Points**
> 
> - Cold War and decolonization initially discouraged UN involvement within states
>     
> - By the mid-1990s, UN peace/security work increasingly addressed civil wars, domestic political conditions, and human rights
>     
> - UN does not have a monopoly on peace operations
>     
>     - Regional organizations, ad hoc coalitions, and hybrid UN/non-UN operations also participate
>         
> - Peacebuilding and gender dimensions of security have received greater attention
>     
> - Security Council veto and great power tensions continue to constrain UN effectiveness
>     

### 21.4 The United Nations and economic and social questions

- Promoting **social and economic development** is a central UN goal
    
    - Charter emphasizes social progress, better living standards, and economic/social advancement
        
- UN economic work shaped by:
    
    - **North–South divide**
        
    - Cold War rivalry
        
- Initially, UN economic agenda dominated by Bretton Woods institutions
    
    - Promoted free-market agenda
        
    - Voting and decision-making skewed toward wealthy Western countries
        
    - Initially focused on post-war reconstruction
        

#### 21.4.1 Decolonization and economic development

- Decolonization in late 1950s/1960s expanded UN membership
    
    - Newly independent states pushed attention toward global economic inequality
        
    - Sought alternative approaches to development
        
- UNCTAD (United Nations Conference on Trade and Development)
    
    - Important forum for developed/developing country dialogue
        
    - Helped lead to creation of **Group of 77 (G77)**
        
    - Coalition of developing countries promoting collective economic interests
        
- **UNDP** established in 1965
    
    - Major actor in reducing global poverty
        
    - Promoted concept of human development
        
    - Publishes annual Human Development Report since 1990
        
    - Links development and security concerns
        
- Financial difficulties
    
    - Main contributor states reduced funding despite increasing UN responsibilities
        
    - Mid-1990s: serious financial crisis in regular budget and peacekeeping
        
    - US eventually agreed to repay amounts owed and returned to full funding in December 2002
        

#### 21.4.2 Global conferences and development

- 1990s: UN convened major conferences on emerging global issues
    
    - Rio de Janeiro (1992): environment
        
    - Vienna (1993): human rights
        
    - Cairo (1994): population
        
    - Beijing (1995): women's issues
        
- Conferences helped:
    
    - Recognize growing global interdependence
        
    - Translate broad concerns into specific programmes
        
    - Establish commissions and follow-up processes
        
- UN economic/social reforms
    
    - **Country Strategy Notes**: plans developed through consultation among agencies, programmes, donors, and host countries
        
        - Defined targets, roles, priorities
            
    - Strengthened **Resident Coordinator**, usually a UNDP employee, to coordinate work at country level
        
    - Headquarters reforms strengthened ECOSOC's coordination role
        

#### 21.4.3 Millennium Development Goals and Sustainable Development Goals

- **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)**
    
    - Adopted at 2000 Millennium Summit
        
    - Targets included:
        
        - Halve number of people living on less than $1/day
            
        - Achieve universal primary education
            
        - Reverse spread of HIV/AIDS and malaria
            
    - Deadline: 2015
        
    - Significant achievements, but uneven progress across regions/countries
        
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**
    
    - Adopted September 2015
        
    - 17 goals and 169 targets
        
    - Vision for global development by **2030**
        
    - Includes:
        
        - Eradicating extreme poverty
            
        - Reducing inequality within/between countries
            
        - Empowering women
            
        - Improving energy efficiency
            
    - Unlike MDGs, apply universally to all countries
        
    - More inclusive development process
        
        - Large consultation programme
            
        - Open working group with representatives from 70 countries
            
    - Criticisms:
        
        - Very broad and ambitious
            
        - May be difficult to achieve
            
        - Difficult to prioritize
            
- 2018: Guterres launched institutional reforms to support SDG implementation
    
    - Strengthened Resident Coordinators
        
- 2019: UN launched **Decade of Action**
    
    - Recognition that states were not on track to meet 2030 targets
        
- 2021: focus on sustainable and equitable recovery from COVID-19
    
- High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development meets annually to review progress
    
- Overall: coordination between global goal-setting and local programme implementation has improved, but development progress remains uneven

> **Box 21.7 UN climate change conferences**
> 
> - **Stockholm Conference (1972):** first UN Conference on Human Environment
>     
>     - Encouraged creation of national environment ministries
>         
>     - Led to establishment of UNEP
>         
> - **Rio Earth Summit (1992):**
>     
>     - UNFCCC signed by many governments
>         
>     - Established basis for future climate diplomacy
>         
> - **Kyoto Protocol (1997):**
>     
>     - Added binding greenhouse gas reduction targets to UNFCCC
>         
> - **Common but differentiated responsibility**
>     
>     - All countries have responsibility to protect environment
>         
>     - Highly industrialized countries have greater responsibility
>         
> - Climate negotiations difficult because states have different interests
>     
>     - Small island states demand strong emissions reductions
>         
>     - Oil producers more sceptical of decarbonization
>         
>     - Disagreements over compensation, liability, and verification
>         
> - **Paris Agreement (2015, COP21):**
>     
>     - First legally binding global climate deal
>         
>     - Goal: keep warming well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue 1.5°C
>         
>     - Countries set their own targets
>         
> - **Glasgow COP26 (2021):**
>     
>     - Focused on implementing Paris Agreement
>         
>     - Glasgow Climate Pact asked countries to strengthen climate pledges
>         
>     - Called for a phasedown of coal
>         
>     - Progress made, but insufficient for 1.5°C target
>         
> - **Takeaway:** UN conferences provide an important framework for global agreement, but translating commitments into concrete action remains difficult
>     

> **Key Points**
> 
> - Cold War and North–South divisions shaped disagreements about development
>     
> - UN economic/social institutions expanded significantly
>     
> - Global conferences helped create new programmes and initiatives
>     
> - Late-1990s reforms improved coordination and clarified responsibilities
>     
> - MDGs and SDGs provided measurable targets for international development
>     
> - SDGs are broader and universally applicable, but progress remains uneven
>     

### 21.5 Conclusion

- UN remains an **indispensable part of the global system**
    
    - Established after WWII to promote international order
        
    - Developed extensive rules and institutions governing relations among states
        
- UN membership gives governments:
    
    - Status and legitimacy in the international system
        
    - Recognition of state autonomy
        
    - Opportunities to exercise influence, take initiatives, and shape international norms
        
- UN capacity has expanded since the 1990s
    
    - Economic/social development
        
    - Peacekeeping and peacebuilding
        
    - Humanitarian and human rights work
        
- Persistent problems:
    
    - Great power rivalry and Security Council vetoes
        
    - Disagreement over intervention and sovereignty
        
    - Terrorism and WMD proliferation
        
    - Inability to respond effectively to crises (Central African Republic, DRC, Myanmar, Somalia, South Sudan, Syria, Ukraine, Yemen)
        
    - Global inequality and injustice
        
- Further UN reform is necessary
    
- **Central takeaway:** The UN is essential but imperfect; its effectiveness depends on cooperation among states, especially great powers, and its ability to balance sovereignty, equality, and global responsibility.
