# 1. What was each transcript actually about?

## Transcript 1: Thematic Discussion 1, AI Opportunities and Implications

**Nature of the session:** This was the broadest thematic discussion. It deliberately moved beyond "AI is risky" and asked what AI means socially, economically, culturally, linguistically, ethically, technically, and environmentally.

### Main points

**UNIDO Deputy Director-General**  
The opening argument was that AI is changing faster than governments or international organizations can manage alone. The response proposed was stronger multilateral and multi-stakeholder cooperation, while avoiding a widening divide in access to technology, skills, data, and computing power. UNIDO positioned industrial AI as a development tool that could create jobs and local capability.

**UNEP Chief Digital Officer**  
She added an important dimension that could otherwise get lost: the environmental cost of AI. She argued that AI has benefits for climate monitoring, biodiversity, pollution, and early warning, but also has a full environmental footprint involving energy, water, critical minerals, manufacturing, and e-waste. She called for scientific measurement, transparent metrics, and more environmentally efficient and affordable AI.

**Gabon, Minister Marc-Alexandre Doumba**  
He presented AI as a development opportunity, especially for countries with large amounts of tacit or unstructured knowledge. He emphasized local and regional adaptation rather than simply copying the models and infrastructure of the biggest economies. His proposal was essentially a more distributed model of AI development, including smaller models and different approaches to energy, water, and infrastructure.

**Yellow.ai co-founder Rasheed Khan**  
He framed the problem as a gap between principles and practical mechanisms. His test was that AI should "create more value than you capture", with the benefits distributed across workers, languages, regions, and generations. He also stressed that the discussion needed to cover social, economic, cultural, technical, and environmental dimensions together.

**President Alar Karis of Estonia**  
Karis argued that governments should not try to be first, but should try to be wise users. He emphasized public trust, transparent data use, skills, education, and digital infrastructure. Estonia's experience was presented as evidence that institutional trust and broad digital literacy are conditions for successful AI adoption.

### Concrete proposals or commitments

The session produced proposals around:

- measuring AI's environmental footprint
- developing more efficient and affordable AI
- local and regional adaptation rather than one dominant model
- building AI capacity alongside infrastructure
- using education and skills as a central policy tool
- treating trust and transparency as infrastructure for adoption

There was not a single major disagreement. The recurring tension was **innovation versus concentration and environmental/social cost**.

## Transcript 2: Thematic Discussion 2, Bridging AI Divides

This was probably the most important session for a global governance briefing because it exposed the distributional problem behind AI governance.

### Main points

**UNDP's Robert Opp**  
He distinguished between several AI divides, especially:

- access to compute, capacity, and skills
- the adoption gap, where AI enters countries faster than institutions can govern it

His basic point was that AI is no longer just a technology problem. It is an implementation and institutional-capacity problem.

**UNDRR's Loretta Hébert-Girardet**  
She argued that "bridging the divide" means more than access to products. Countries need the ability to **develop, adapt, evaluate, and govern** AI themselves. AI cannot replace strong institutions, trusted data, local expertise, community participation, or accountable decision-making.

**UNESCO Director-General Khaled El-Anany**  
UNESCO's contribution was heavily focused on implementation and capacity. He pointed to the 2021 UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI and UNESCO's training programmes, AI competency frameworks, teacher training, digital literacy, and work on linguistic diversity.

**El Salvador Vice-President Félix Ulloa**  
El Salvador offered a national case study. He described:

- over 1.2 million devices distributed to students
- Google Classroom in the public education system
- an AI tutor arrangement
- the Doctor SV telemedicine programme
- a national AI agency and four AI-related laws
- problems with rural connectivity, especially outside urban areas

He also described how a UNESCO readiness assessment identified weaknesses, particularly around rural access and participation.

**Digital Futures Lab**  
One of the more important interventions identified an **"oversight gap"** in the Global South. The speaker argued that countries are being encouraged to deploy AI without equivalent investment in evaluation, procurement standards, incident reporting, and continuous monitoring. Civil society and independent media were also described as crucial parts of the accountability system.

**Botswana and Nepal**  
Both pushed the discussion toward international financing. Botswana explicitly supported a global AI fund for developing countries, while Nepal supported the Secretary-General's proposed Global Fund on AI.

**China**  
China advocated capacity building, equitable development, interoperable infrastructure, and the UN as the main channel for global cooperation. It also highlighted its Global AI Governance Initiative and AI Capacity Building Action Plan.

### Concrete proposals

This session generated some of the clearest proposals in the whole event:

- a global AI capacity fund
- stronger access to compute and data
- open models and open standards
- training tied to actual employment and development outcomes
- regional and international networks of AI centres
- capacity to evaluate and govern AI, not just use it
- greater representation of Global South and Indigenous communities

The main disagreement was not about whether AI divides exist. It was about **how to close them and who should pay**.

# 3. Thematic Discussion 3, Safe, Secure and Trustworthy AI

This was the session most directly connected to international governance architecture.

### Main points

**WIPO facilitator**  
The key concept was **interoperability, not harmonization**. Countries can have different laws and regulatory traditions while still developing common interfaces, standards, and ways of recognizing each other's safeguards.

**Costa Rica co-chair Paula Bogantes**  
She argued that the world does not need more principles unless there are ways to show they are actually being implemented. She proposed a **minimum practical compatibility** across:

- terminology
- risk classifications
- documentation
- data protection
- cybersecurity
- testing
- incident reporting

She also raised the problem of unequal computing power and unequal participation in global AI governance.

**Rebecca Finlay, Partnership on AI**  
She proposed three concrete lines of action:

1. strengthen the independent evidence base
2. open up the science and disclosure process
3. create a shared baseline of good practice tied to the public interest and human rights

She also announced the Global AI Progress Hub as a public platform for sharing progress.

**UN Under-Secretary-General Amandeep Singh Gill**  
This was one of the strongest governance interventions. He argued that AI is moving from static model oversight toward **dynamic system governance**, especially with agentic AI. He proposed living risk taxonomies, controlled testing and staged deployment. He also identified regulatory arbitrage, accountability gaps, compliance burdens, and unequal capacity as consequences of fragmentation. One practical proposal was **cross-border regulatory sandboxes**.

**Japan**  
Japan pointed to the OECD AI Principles and Hiroshima AI Process as existing building blocks. It described work with ASEAN, especially Singapore, to cross-map frameworks and identify commonalities.

**Infosys**  
Infosys pushed the problem further: simply having a common taxonomy is not enough. Different systems can use the same word, such as "transparency", but mean different operational requirements. It proposed a deeper control-level mapping and knowledge graph.

**UAE**  
The UAE representative warned against assuming that "high risk" means the same thing everywhere. She also identified the absence of shared incident reporting as a major problem. At the same time, she warned that excessive convergence could create a lowest-common-denominator regime.

**WITNESS**  
WITNESS shifted the focus from abstract catastrophic risk to information integrity. It raised synthetic media, deepfakes, non-consensual imagery, and the "liar's dividend". It proposed content provenance, global detection capabilities, independent third-party oversight, and a multi-stakeholder working group on information integrity.

### The key disagreement

This was the most interesting governance tension in the transcripts:

**Common rules are necessary, but common rules cannot simply mean one universal regulatory model.**

That is essentially the distinction between **interoperability** and **uniformity**.

# 4. Thematic Discussion 4, Respecting, Protecting and Promoting Human Rights

This was the strongest session for your **Ethics and Morality / International Law** topic.

### Main points

**UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk**  
Türk compared AI governance to the lengthy testing and regulatory processes applied to medicines. His argument was that rapid AI deployment is occurring despite surveillance, discrimination, misinformation, child harms, and environmental pressures.

Most importantly for your course, he explicitly said that **international human rights law is already a binding legal framework** and argued for human rights due diligence, impact assessments, privacy protections, and meaningful human oversight.

**Spain**  
Spain's minister argued that the Global South risks becoming a supplier of data and resources for more powerful actors. He connected AI governance to the EU AI Act, digital rights, child protection, transparency, and international cooperation.

**Canada**  
Canadian Permanent Representative David Lametti emphasized the justice system and human responsibility. He argued that the rule of law has to remain meaningful when decisions are increasingly made or assisted by machines. Canada also described proposed child-protection measures requiring AI systems used by children to be proven safe.

**UN Women**  
UN Women argued that without regulation, AI can amplify existing discrimination and violence against women and girls. It emphasized that states remain the primary duty bearers, while companies remain responsible across the AI lifecycle.

**Instituto Alana (Brazil)**  
This speaker focused on children and stressed that children are not one homogeneous group. It advocated proactive accountability, child rights built into systems from the beginning, and caution before deployment where serious harms are possible.

**Netherlands**  
The Dutch minister gave a concrete governance example: an Algorithm Framework, a Fundamental Rights and Algorithm Impact Assessment, bias-testing tools, and an Algorithm Register. She also stressed that young people should participate in decision-making about AI affecting them.

### Key lesson

This session makes a strong argument that the governance problem is not simply "what rules should AI have?"

It is:

**How do existing legal and ethical commitments become enforceable and operational when technology moves faster than institutions?**

### 2. It was large, but not fully universal

### 3. The independent scientific panel became a central part of the governance process

# 10. SWOT analysis

## Strengths

**1. Exceptional convening power.**  
The UN brought states, science, industry, civil society, academia, and technical actors into the same process. That is hard for any individual state to reproduce.

**2. Scientific evidence was explicitly built into the process.**  
The Independent International Scientific Panel was repeatedly treated as an evidence base rather than just another stakeholder.

**3. The dialogue produced practical governance ideas.**  
Cross-border sandboxes, incident reporting, interoperability standards, impact assessments, capacity funds, evaluation networks, and shared baselines are much more concrete than generic statements that AI should be "ethical".

**4. There was significant convergence on basic principles.**  
Human rights, safety, accountability, human oversight, inclusion, and trust appeared repeatedly across very different countries.

## Weaknesses

**1. Participation was still unequal.**  
The co-chair acknowledged that only 107 of 170 represented countries were actually present.

**2. Governance remains fragmented.**  
Participants described dozens of governance models, incompatible regulatory definitions, inconsistent evaluation, and no shared incident-reporting system.

**3. Capacity is deeply unequal.**  
Some states are discussing frontier models and safety institutes while others are still dealing with connectivity, electricity, skills, and basic digital infrastructure.

**4. The process has not yet produced a binding global regime.**  
Its immediate outputs are dialogue, coordination, voluntary mechanisms, and future work.

## Opportunities

**1. Interoperability could become the practical middle ground between fragmentation and one-size-fits-all regulation.**

**2. The UN could connect existing initiatives instead of creating an entirely new bureaucracy.**  
The transcripts repeatedly pointed to networks, standards, sandboxes, and existing organizations.

**3. A global AI capacity fund could address the governance divide, not just the infrastructure divide.**

**4. Human rights could become the common baseline.**  
That would give diverse governance systems a shared normative floor without requiring identical national laws.

**5. Multilingual and locally designed AI could make global governance more representative.**

## Threats

**1. Concentration of compute, models, data, and expertise.**  
The dialogue repeatedly warned that a small number of countries and companies exercise disproportionate influence.

**2. Regulatory arbitrage.**  
Developers could shift activities to jurisdictions with weaker protections. This was explicitly raised by Amandeep Gill.

**3. Rights harms may move faster than international institutions.**  
Participants pointed to surveillance, deepfakes, discrimination, child harms, misinformation, and weaponization.

**4. Environmental pressure.**  
Energy, water, minerals, and e-waste were repeatedly raised as governance issues, not just technical side effects.

**5. Agentic AI could outpace existing accountability structures.**  
The more autonomous a system becomes, the harder it becomes to identify who is legally and morally responsible.

## Revised answer to Question 2: How does it relate to this week's topic and readings?

### The strongest overall connection

The Geneva dialogue is a very good example of **international law and global governance operating before, or alongside, a settled legal regime**.

BSO Chapter 19 says international law is one of the basic **fundamental institutions** through which states cooperate under anarchy. It also distinguishes these from **issue-specific institutions or regimes**, which establish rules and procedures for particular problems.

AI governance is currently somewhere in between. There are already international legal foundations, especially human rights law and the UN system, but there is not yet a single comprehensive AI governance regime. The Geneva dialogue was an attempt to build common understandings, norms, standards, and cooperative mechanisms that might eventually support a more developed regime.

That gives you a very useful line for the presentation:

> **Geneva was not the creation of a global AI law. It was part of the institutional process through which states and other actors are trying to build one.**

# Connection 1: BSO Ch. 19 and the "fundamental institutions" of international society

# Connection 3: Abbott & Snidal, hard law vs. soft law

This is probably your **best reading connection**.

Abbott and Snidal argue that hard and soft law exist on a continuum, and that states deliberately choose different levels of:

- **obligation**
- **precision**
- **delegation**

The Geneva discussion repeatedly showed actors choosing softer forms of legalization.

For example:

**Japan** pointed to the OECD AI Principles and Hiroshima AI Process and described cross-mapping between frameworks as a way to find common ground without requiring identical national systems.

**Indonesia** explicitly argued for interoperability rather than uniformity and called for cooperation, capacity building, and voluntary financing mechanisms.

**Australia** similarly argued for shared principles that could be implemented flexibly across different national contexts.

That looks a lot like Abbott and Snidal's argument that **soft law is often deliberately chosen because it is easier to negotiate, more adaptable, and less costly to sovereignty**.

### Why does that make sense for AI?

The transcripts give you at least three of Abbott and Snidal's conditions.

**Uncertainty:** AI is developing so quickly that governments do not know exactly what problems future rules will need to address. Amandeep Gill described the move toward adaptive governance, living risk taxonomies, staged deployment, and controlled testing.

**Sovereignty costs:** Countries repeatedly resisted a single model. Indonesia explicitly rejected a one-size-fits-all approach, while the UAE warned against convergence for its own sake.

**Divergent preferences:** The meeting included states with radically different levels of infrastructure, regulatory capacity, political systems, and development priorities.

So you can make this argument:

> **The predominance of soft-law mechanisms at Geneva was not necessarily a sign that global governance was weak. According to Abbott and Snidal, soft law can be a rational response to uncertainty, sovereignty costs, and divergent state preferences.**

That's a much more interesting argument than simply saying "there was no treaty."

# Connection 4: But Geneva also shows the demand for hard law

# Connection 7: BSO and the legitimacy problem

BSO emphasizes that international law is not just a set of rules. It also structures arguments about **legitimacy, authority, and what counts as justified behaviour**.

Geneva was full of this kind of legal and political justification.

Consider the repeated arguments:

- AI must be human-centred.
- AI must respect human rights.
- AI must leave no one behind.
- AI must remain under human control.
- AI governance must be inclusive.
- AI cannot be governed by a few powerful states or companies.

These are not purely technical claims. They are arguments about what constitutes **legitimate global governance**.

The most interesting example is the repeated claim that **human rights should serve as the common baseline** rather than merely another policy preference. The Partnership on AI called for a shared baseline of good practice using existing human rights frameworks, while the UN human rights discussion treated human rights law as a binding foundation.

# Connection 8: BSO's "practice turn"

There is also a more subtle connection to the final section of Chapter 19.

The practice turn says legal obligation can develop through **participation in legal practices**, rather than appearing only because someone creates a treaty or imposes coercion.

That describes what Geneva may be trying to accomplish.

States are:

- comparing national frameworks
- cross-mapping regulations
- sharing standards
- developing common terminology
- discussing incident reporting
- creating sandboxes
- testing interoperability
- learning from each other

So the dialogue is not simply "talking about future law."

It is already creating **shared governance practices** that could eventually make stronger legal convergence possible.

That is a very good theoretical point for a global governance course:

> **Institutions can shape behaviour before they create binding law.**
