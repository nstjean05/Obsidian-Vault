# Chapter 9: Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

## 9.1 What Is a Charter of Rights?
- **Purpose:** Prevent democratic majorities from violating fundamental rights, especially minority rights
- **Key principle:** Rights entrenched in constitution = beyond reach of majority/elected representatives
- **Individual rights:** Individuals claim violations against government (not private parties)
- **Protects minorities:** Against tyranny of majority

### Example: **Multani v. Commission scolaire Marguerite-Bourgeoys (2006)**
- Sikh student wanted to wear kirpan (ceremonial dagger) at school
- School had no-weapons policy
- Court balanced religious freedom vs. majority interest
- Allowed kirpan if contained in inaccessible sheath
- Showed Charter protects minority rights even when majority objects

## 9.2 The Adoption of the Charter
- **Not new rights:** Charter was new mechanism for protecting established rights
	- British approach: No entrenched charter; rights protected by tradition, common law, conventions
	- Parliament could technically violate rights, but electoral pressure/opposition prevented it
- **Canada's traditional approach (pre-1982):** British model
	- Rights like freedom of religion, speech part of liberal democratic tradition
	- Parliament was forum for defending rights
	- Believed electoral democracy + rule of law = sufficient safeguard

### Problems With Traditional Approach
- Provinces sometimes violated rights (Aberhart Alberta 1930s, Duplessis Quebec 1950s)
- WWII: Japanese-Canadian internment (major violation)
- Federal government not always trustworthy guardian

### Why Charter Adopted (1982)
- Post-WWII momentum for entrenched rights protections
- U.S. Supreme Court boldly using Bill of Rights for racial equality
- Rights activists wanted American-style constitutional protection
- Canadians already used to judicial review (federalism disputes)

## 9.3 Opposition to the Charter
- **Quebec opposed:** Feared Charter would undermine French language protections, distinct society status
- **General concerns:**
	- Transfers power to unelected judiciary
	- Judges impose policies on elected representatives
	- Undemocratic development
	- Courts decide on abortion, drugs, capital punishment, etc.

### Defense of Charter
- Canada is liberal democracy, not just democracy
	- Majority rule must respect minority rights
	- Democracy depends on freedom of speech/association
	- Japanese-Canadian example shows dangers without entrenched protection
	- Necessary to empower judiciary to correct illiberal excesses

### Counter-Argument
- Most Charter cases involve "grey area" rights, not clear violations
	- Ex. Is 15-year minimum for weapons during sexual assault "cruel/unusual"?
	- Judges not experts on moral-political questions
	- Why should 9 judges impose views on democratic majority?
- Judges also constrained by public opinion (can't stray too far)

## 9.4-9.5 How the Charter Works: Bedford Case

### **Canada (Attorney General) v. Bedford (2013)**
- **Issue:** Three sex workers challenged Criminal Code prostitution laws
- **Laws in question:**
	1. Illegal to live off avails of prostitution (targets pimps)
	2. Bawdy houses (establishments for prostitution) illegal
	3. Public communication for sex trade illegal
- **Argument:** These laws made legal activity (prostitution) dangerous, violated security of person

### **Section 7 Right**
- "Everyone has right to life, liberty and security of person"
- Also: Right not to be deprived thereof except in accordance with principles of fundamental justice
- **Court finding:** All 9 justices agreed
	- Laws don't just impose conditions on prostitution
	- They impose dangerous conditions on legal activity
	- Prevent people from taking protective steps

## 9.6 Section 1 of Charter
- **Reasonable limits:** Charter rights can be limited if reasonable
- Courts must balance rights against government objectives
- If government can't justify limits → violation stands

## 9.7 The Notwithstanding Clause
- **Section 33 of CA 1982:** "Notwithstanding clause"
	- Federal or provincial governments can override certain Charter rights
	- Must be explicit, passed by legislation
	- Must be renewed every 5 years
- **Quebec used it:** To protect French language rights from Charter challenges
- **Controversial:** Defeats purpose of entrenching rights

## 9.8 Political Impact of Charter
- Shifted power to judiciary
- Judges now decide major policy issues
- More Charter litigation means more judicial involvement
- Changed nature of Canadian politics
