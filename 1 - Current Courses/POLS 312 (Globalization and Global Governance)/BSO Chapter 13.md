### 13.1 Introduction
- Constructivism went from nonexistent 30 years ago to a leading IR school
    - Most followed theory among US IR scholars
- Realist and many liberal theories assume states have enduring interests (power, wealth) constrained by _material_ forces (geography, tech, distribution of power)
- Constructivist critique: ideas, knowledge, norms, and rules also shape state identities, interests, and the organization of world politics
- Not the only theory to see international politics as a society rather than a system
    - Ex. Marxism, English School, feminism
    - But constructivists engaged neorealists and neoliberals directly on how identity, norms, and culture shape war and peace
- Branches differ: structure vs agency, stability vs transformation
- Focus on global change, specifically
    1. Convergence of states on similar ways of organizing domestic and international life
    2. How norms become internationalized and institutionalized
    3. Whether norms maintain inequality or reflect new possibilities of progress
### 13.2 The Rise of Constructivism
- **Neorealism**: states are primary actors, in anarchy (no supranational authority), consumed by security, power, survival
    - Distribution of power explains almost everything
- **Neoliberal institutionalism**: states cooperate extensively for interests beyond security
    - Lack of trust is the main obstacle, so states build institutions (monitoring, publicizing cheating)
- Both share **individualism** and **materialism**
    - Individualism: actors have fixed interests; structure constrains
    - Materialism: structure is power distribution, tech, geography
    - Norms and ideas may _constrain_ how states pursue interests, but never _define_ them
- Constructivism's 1990s rise had two causes
    1. 1980s dissidents (drawing on sociological and critical theory) made norms, ideas, identity, and rules visible
        - Is it more counter-intuitive that the world is asocial, or that it's congested with norms?
        - Neorealism had little to say about sovereignty: is it a social capacity, a licence with rights and responsibilities?
        - Norms and institutions don't just constrain, they _constitute_ actors
    2. End of the Cold War
        - Predicted to end with a bang, not a whimper
        - Neorealists and neoliberals had jettisoned the tools to explain the role of ideas
        - Power distribution couldn't say if the US would be a hegemon or work through multilateral institutions
        - Debate over national interest and identity ("who are 'we'?")
        - Raised human security, transnationalism, human rights, all outside neorealism's wheelhouse
- Dissidents became "winners"; some became constructivists, others stayed Marxist, feminist, etc.
    - Constructivists took on neorealism's turf (alliances, intervention, arms races, great power change)
- First generation drew on late 19th century sociology (order and progress through shared norms despite modernization)
    - Imagined a post-Cold War community with shared norms, interests, outlooks
    - Downside: neglected power and domination and obscured inequalities behind "progressive" change
#### Key Points
- Neorealism and neoliberal institutionalism dominated 1980s US IR; both materialist and individualist
- Critics drew on critical and sociological theory to show effects of ideas and norms
- End of the Cold War opened space to challenge existing theories
- First wave stressed shared identities and norms producing stable order and some progress
- Ideas of an egalitarian international community often hid continued power and domination
### 13.3 Constructivism
- Constructivism is a **social theory**, not a _substantive theory_
    - Social theory: how to conceptualize agents and structures
    - Substantive theory: specific claims about patterns (ex. democratic peace)
    - Comparable to rational choice, which says nothing about the _content_ of preferences or constraints
        - Neorealism and neoliberalism both use rational choice but reach rival claims via different assumptions about anarchy and institutions
    - To make substantive claims, you must specify actors, interests, capacities, and the content of normative structures
- "Constructivism is about human consciousness and its role in international life" (Ruggie 1998)
- Core is **idealism** and **holism** (Wendt 1999)
    - Idealism: ideas matter, but they're _social_ (collectively held knowledge, symbols, language, rules)
        - Doesn't reject material reality; meaning of the material depends on interpretation
        - Ex. the balance of power isn't "out there"; states debate what it means
    - Holism: world is irreducibly social, can't be decomposed into pre-existing actors
        - Still allows agency
        - Ex. Cold War leaders creatively transformed the structure
- **Social construction of reality**
    - Actors are produced by their cultural environment (nurture, not nature)
        - Ex. American identity shapes interests and acceptable means
    - Knowledge (symbols, rules, concepts, categories) shapes how we interpret the world
        - Ex. many ways to classify collective violence: civil war, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, genocide
    - **Social facts** vs **brute facts**
        - Brute facts (rocks, gravity, oceans) exist without human agreement
        - Social facts (money, refugees, terrorism, human rights, sovereignty) depend on agreement, though taken for granted
    - **Practices** (Adler and Pouliot 2011): socially meaningful patterns of action
        - Proper way of doing things, routinized, learned in communities
        - Ex. humanitarians: impartiality, independence, neutrality; learned by doing; signal membership and ethical commitment
- **Rules**
    - _Regulative_ rules regulate existing activity (rules of the road, WTO rules)
    - _Constitutive_ rules create the activity (rules of rugby, sovereignty)
    - Rules are revised through practice and argument
    - _Strategic social construction_ (Finnemore and Sikkink 1998)
        - Ex. activists "naming and shaming" and getting states to identify with norms
- Different worlds of anarchy: "anarchy is what states make of it" (Wendt 1992)
    - Ex. a world of Gandhis vs a world of bin Ladens
- **Legitimacy**
    - World orders need consent, not just coercion
    - More legitimacy means cheaper action and easier cooperation; less means costlier
    - Even great powers alter policy to look legitimate
    - Explains why material weak activists can name and shame
- **Logic of consequences** (costs and benefits) vs **logic of appropriateness** (rule-following, legitimacy)
    - Not necessarily competing
    - Ex. US invasion of Iraq (2003) without UNSC blessing: seen as illegitimate, raised costs
- Constructivists as "archaeologists" of the taken for granted
    - Origins of ideas, institutions, motives, and contingency (counterfactuals, roads not taken)
    - Ex. sovereignty didn't always exist; "refugees" as a legal category is only ~100 years old
- Meaning-making (Weber): we're cultural beings who give the world significance
    - Meanings come from culture, not private beliefs
    - Culture _informs_ meaning rather than just constraining
    - Culture is fractured, so meanings are contested
#### Box 13.1: Key concepts
- **Agent-structure problem**: how agents and structures relate
    - Agents first means pre-social actors; structure first means "cultural dupes"
    - Solution: they _constitute each other_
- **Holism** vs **individualism**; **idealism** vs **materialism**
- **Identity**: social understanding of self in relation to an "other"; identities shape interests and can change
- **Normative structure**: collectively held ideas (knowledge, rules, beliefs, norms)
    - Constrain, _construct_ meaning, constitute identities and interests, define appropriate conduct
    - **Norm** is "a standard of appropriate behavior for actors with a given identity" (Finnemore and Sikkink 1998)
    - Actors follow norms for costs and benefits _and_ sense of self
- **Practices**, **rational choice** (maximizing preferences under constraints)
#### Box 13.2: Wendt's three cultures of anarchy
- Anarchy's deep structure is cultural, not material; based on roles of Self and Other
- **Hobbesian** (enemy): war of all against all
    - True self-help, zero-sum security; survival depends on military power
- **Lockean** (rival): expect recognition of sovereignty, life, and liberty
    - No right to be free from violence in disputes
- **Kantian** (friend): two rules
    1. Settle disputes without war or threat of war
    2. Fight as a team if one is threatened by a third party
#### Key Points
- Constructivists focus on consciousness and knowledge; ideas as structural factors
    - Interested in how agents produce structures and structures produce agents
    - Practices sit between agents and structures
- Regulative and constitutive norms shape what actors do, but only _constitutive_ norms shape actors' identity and what counts as legitimate
- Normative structures shape beliefs, practices, sense of right and wrong, legitimacy
- Meanings aren't always fixed; fixing meaning is central to politics
- Social construction denaturalizes what's taken for granted and shows alternative pathways
- Power is also the production of identities, interests, and meanings, not only making someone do what they otherwise wouldn't
#### Opposing Opinions: The Laws of War
- **Do the laws of war still make sense?**
    - For (they're outdated)
        - Non-state actors (ex. Islamic State) cause much mayhem but aren't bound by them
        - Binding states but not non-state combatants gives the latter a battlefield advantage
        - Post-9/11 asymmetrical wars: terrorists don't play by the rules, so shouldn't benefit (ex. no POW rights)
        - IHL used as a weapon: forces in civilian areas as human shields, then win sympathy and publicity when civilians are harmed
1. To what extent are those focused on the growing web of the laws of war too energized about what's "on the books" vs the theatre of war?
2. If the laws of war disappeared, would states still regulate their conduct?
3. Would the world be better or worse off without them?
### 13.4 Constructivism and global change
- Could seem good at explaining stability, useless at change, but it isn't
    - What exists might not have existed, and need not exist
    - Big break: explaining the end of the Cold War
    - Origins of sovereignty and non-interference, and recent conditional sovereignty (_responsibility to protect_)
    - Orders are sustained by changing ideas of legitimacy
        - Ex. empires were legitimate until WWII; now they aren't
- Reputation for tracking change that fuels integration and moral progress
    - Norm internationalization linked to _homogeneity_; many norms are liberal (equality, human rights, liberty, private property)
    - But all orders, even ones built on equality and justice, produce inequality and domination
#### Diffusion
- **Institutional isomorphism**: organizations in the same environment come to resemble each other
    - Ex. nation-state, democratic governance, market economies, multilateral IOs
    - Why: effectiveness, or desire for acceptance, legitimacy, and status
        - Ex. post-Cold War democratization: efficiency, or wanting to look modern and civilized?
- How things diffuse
    - Coercion (ex. colonialism and capitalism)
    - Strategic competition (rivals copy weapons systems)
    - Four more reasons
        1. Resources: adopt institutions to signal "club" membership
            - Ex. Eastern European states adopting reforms for EU entry
        2. Uncertainty: copy models seen as successful (ex. the "Western" model as "gold standard" post-Cold War)
        3. Symbolic standing: ex. Third World states buying expensive weapons of little military value; Iran's nuclear ambitions may also be a status symbol
        4. Professional associations and expert communities spread standard techniques
            - Ex. economists on crises, lawyers on rights violations, humanitarians on refugee camps
#### Norms and the life cycle (Finnemore and Sikkink 1998)
- Norms constrain because of costs _and_ sense of self
    - Ex. "civilized" states avoid violence because it violates how they're expected to act
1. **Norm emergence**: a _norm entrepreneur_ (often NGOs and IOs) names, interprets, and dramatizes an issue, then persuades states
    - Ex. humanitarian intervention and R2P: once objected to as violating non-interference, emerged after tragedies like Rwanda
2. **Norm cascade** after a tipping point
    - Pressure for conformity, international legitimation, leaders' self-esteem
3. **Norm internalization**: taken for granted, no longer debated
    - Ex. women voting, slavery, immunity for medical personnel
- Cautions against reading this as uniformity and harmony
    1. No inevitability; high mortality for norms
        - _Cultural match_ explains receptivity and resistance (ex. liberal human rights norms)
    2. Norms get transmuted in different contexts
        - Ex. "cruel and unusual" punishment and the US death penalty vs Europe
    3. Looking alike isn't acting alike
        - Ex. democratic forms alongside authoritarian and illiberal practice
    4. Established norms can be contested and replaced; world orders rise and fall
    5. Power is always present in a normative order
        - Constructivists are accused of being "utopian," but have the tools to find hidden power
- **Humanity** and power
    - Emerged in the 18th century; not extended to all humans (seen as inhuman or less than human by race, gender, religion)
    - Tied to colonial "civilizing" missions, generating superiority and inferiority
    - Ex. Black Lives Matter shows surface equality masks deep inequality
    - Compassion: pity or compassion? Giving over distance can produce a "stick-figure humanity," reproducing superiority and inferiority
#### Case Study 13.2: "Victims"
- "Victim" is a social construction: first a sacrificed creature (17th c.), then someone who suffered acute loss
- Modern criteria for an "ideal victim"
    1. Seen as part of humanity (ex. enslaved persons rarely registered)
    2. Suffering is grave (culturally determined; ex. domestic violence)
    3. Weak, vulnerable, passive (gender and race shape this)
    4. Innocent, did nothing to cause it
- Ex. gender-based violence (GBV) in war
    - Historically neglected as inevitable; attention after the Cold War from human security, women's rights, evidence of rape as a weapon, media, women's movements
    - DRC called the "rape capital of the world"; Amnesty cited 40,000 reported cases (incomplete count)
- Effects of the "ideal victim" label
	- Women got more international care than ever
    - But some were "complex victims" who were also perpetrators
    - Men overlooked as victims because they were seen as natural perpetrators
    - Tapped into racist colonial narratives ("heart of darkness"; African men as violent, predatory)
    - Unreliable but persuasive statistics reinforced a colonial view
    - Reinforced a modern "white saviour" civilizing mission
1. What distinguishes "deserving" from "undeserving" victims?
2. Why are women and children more often "innocent victims" than men? What if women aren't assumed to be "ideal victims"?
### 13.5 Conclusion
- Constructivism is a _social_ theory, not a substantive one, which reshapes how we see the world "hanging together"
    - A materialist-only view is the strange one, not the idea that politics is social
    - Shows the social alternative in mainstream areas; uncovers the world being made and unmade
- Focus on how normative structure shapes patterns of order
    - Post-Cold War origins stressed order through consensus, not domination
    - Danger: neglecting how power ripples through the normative order and how actors compete, sometimes violently, to redefine norms and community boundaries
- Alternative schools stress power and conflict: international society is in constant combat
- Models fit the times: post-Cold War "denser, more legitimate order" versions may lose ground to those stressing an unmade, disordered society
    - Social construction is an ongoing and sometimes bloody process
