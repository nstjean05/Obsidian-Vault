*1 - Desk jobs tend to be sedentary, having adverse effects on general health, and increasing the probability of heart disease, hypertension, and obesity. Discuss probable effects on the general health of a large increase in the percentage of the population employed at desk jobs. What about if a large percentage of the population is unemployed?*

Each of these has vast negative effects. Sedentary work has been repeatedly shown to increase risks of cancer, diabetes, and general mortality. Personally, I have noticed that during my time working a desk job, my cardio health faded. However, unemployment has even more significant impacts. There is a famous quote from *The Big Short*, a 2015 movie about the Great Financial Crisis, which states that "Every 1% unemployment goes up, 40,000 people die, did you know that?" While this is an out-of-date, innacurate statistic, it serves to illustrate the point. Mortality from unemployed people is 1.6 times higher in Germany than the employed. It has further adverse affects, such as learned helplessness, and elevated rates of alcoholism, heart attacks, and strokes.

*6 - You are the owner of a small snowmobile factory in Quebec that employs about 100 people and is your town’s major employer. These people have nearly all worked for you for more than ten years and are completely dependent on these jobs. You have just learned that your major competitor is about to robotize its factory and will be able to sell snowmobiles for half your retail price. You may either follow suit, laying off 75 employees or see your business become bankrupt in a year, costing everyone’s job. What should you do?*

I would collude with the other company to each robotize our factories, but hold prices artificially high. During this time, workers will continue to be paid as usual, but rather than going to work will pursue further education. After a period of five years, these workers will be laid off, and snowmobile prices will be lowered.

For a more pragmatic (and legal) approach, we can use utilitarianism to come to a logical conclusion. If our two options are either keeping twenty-five jobs or zero jobs, then keeping the twenty-five workers and robotizing is the better approach. Perhaps even more workers can be maintained by transitioning them into sales roles and adjusting branding so customers may accept a higher price (for a more 'quality' product).

*18 - Research the extent to which transportation is now automated. Consider railways and airlines and describe their attempts to automate traffic. Now, propose a way to automate all automobile and truck traffic, or argue convincingly that this task is impractical.*

Both rail and flight are significantly automated, and only growing more so. There are several domains for this automation. First, the actual vehicle control. Both trains and flights are automated for almost all of the transportation process, with interventions in each for stopping/starting or takeoff/landing or other edge cases. Another domain is in the traffic automation. Currently, large audio models are being trained to take the load off of air traffic controllers, who have had a number of recent notable strikes. This is also happening for train routing.

One way for automobile and truck traffic to be organized would be for each vehicle to have a transponder/receiver which can connect to a larger system. This system would give vehicles general route directions. However, all the immediate pathfinding would be done by the vehicle itself, via LiDAR, computer vision, and other modern techniques. I think this could certainly be done practically - we already have part of this equation in apps like Google Maps or Apple Maps, which track vehicle locations and adjust routes automatically.

*23 - A self-driving automobile mistakes a red light for a green light because of dense fog, runs the light, crashes into a car that did have the right-of-way, and several people are killed. Who is liable and to what extent? (1) the car’s manufacturer, (2) the person who programmed the software, (3) the maker of the traffic light for not having its colours distinguishable in foggy conditions, (4) the vehicle’s owner, (5) the vehicle’s “operator” who was asleep at the time, (6) some level of government for insufficient regulation of or control over, cars, software, intersections, or traffic lights?*

This is one of the most important discussions in the current day. Frequently discussed with regard to semi-automated drone technology, it is highly important that the buck stops somewhere, and someone takes responsibility. Currently, two groups take accountability. In many semi-automated vehicles (like a Tesla with Driver Assist), the driver is told they must watch the road and maintain control of the vehicle. In fully-automated vehicles like a WayMo, the company WayMo is responsible for its' vehicles errors.
Generally, it is important to consider these things on a case-by-case basis. At some point in the future, self-driving may be highly reliable, and be far safer than any driver. Crashes could become rare enough that drivers and cars aren't the major issue. Perhaps the city doesn't do proper maintenance on the lights, and that was the cause of the accident. Another case is BC's system of no-fault insurance. This could be a good solution once crashes are less common, as the processes to control a car become so diffused, and the control of the vehicle so out of the hands of an individual, that individuals aren't held accountable for incidents.
