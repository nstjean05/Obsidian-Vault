## The Politics, Power, and Pathologies of International Organizations (Barnett and Finnemore 1999)
### Introduction
- Core question: do IOs really do what their creators intend?
    - Most theories explain why IOs are _created_ (incomplete info, transaction costs, Pareto efficiency), not what they _do_ after
    - IOs often stray from efficiency goals and exercise power autonomously
- Authors’ approach is **constructivist**, rooted in _sociological institutionalism_ and Weber on bureaucracy
    - **Rational-legal authority** gives IOs power independent of states
    - Bureaucracies make rules, but also create **social knowledge**
        - define tasks (ex. “development”)
        - create categories of actors (ex. “refugee”)
        - create new interests (ex. promoting human rights)
        - transfer models of political organization (ex. markets, democracy)
    - Same features that make them powerful can make them _pathological_ (unresponsive, obsessed with rules over mission, self-defeating)
- Focus = intergovernmental orgs only, not NGOs
#### Three debates they weigh in on
1. **Do IOs matter?** (neoliberal institutionalists vs neorealists)
    - Neoliberals only see one facet of IO power (helping states solve market failures and collective action problems)
    - IOs also _create actors, specify authority, define work and give it meaning_
    - Power even without material resources
2. **Are IOs autonomous actors?**
    - Challenges the **statist ontology**
    - Regimes lit treats IOs as structure, not agents (“principles, norms, rules, and decision-making procedures,” Krasner)
    - Weber lets us treat IOs as _agents_, not just structure
3. **Are IOs desirable?**
    - Classical liberalism = IOs are a peaceful alternative to war
    - Neoliberal axiom: if an IO exists it must be Pareto improving, since states would pull the plug otherwise
    - Authors find this unsatisfying, IOs repeatedly produce bad outcomes without being dismantled
- Explanation is **constitutive** (how is bureaucracy put together socially), not law-like “if X then Y”
    - Analogy: DNA structure makes causal claims about genetics possible
    - Makes behaviours _possible or probable_, not determined
- Structure of article
    1. assumptions of org theory
    2. power of IOs (constructivist approach, UN examples)
    3. dysfunction, typology, and **pathologies**
### Theoretical Approaches to Organizations
- Two strands
    1. **Economistic**: instrumental rationality, efficiency
    2. **Sociological**: legitimacy and power
#### Economist approach
- Origin: Coase and Williamson, why do firms exist if markets should be more efficient?
    - Orgs = efficient solutions to contracting problems, incomplete info, market imperfections
- Neoliberals and neorealists (following Waltz) see world politics like a market of utility maximizers
    - Disagree on anarchy, relative vs absolute gains, cheating fears
    - Agree IOs help states further their interests
    - IOs = _instruments_, state power plays out in bargaining on the Pareto frontier
#### Sociological approach
- Two big contrasts with economists
    1. view of org-environment relationship
    2. basis for understanding **org autonomy**
##### IOs and their environment
- Economic view: socially thin environment, only competition, exchange, efficiency pressure
- Sociology: orgs are _social facts_, whether they work efficiently is an empirical question, not an assumption
    - Respond to normative and cultural forces too
    - Can be selected for _legitimacy and normative fit_, not efficiency
    - Created for what they _represent_ symbolically, not what they do
- Some orgs face competition and clear success criteria (firms w/ balance sheets), others don’t (ex. poli sci departments)
- Pick a framework whose assumptions match the empirical conditions of the IO
##### IO autonomy
- Regime theory = IOs are epiphenomena of state interaction (Waltz’s reductionism)
    - “Empty shells” or passive machinery
    - No _ontological independence_
    - Neoliberals give IOs causal status as _intervening variables_ (control of info) but not autonomy or purpose
- Empirically this doesn’t hold (also most IOs are now created by other IOs, i.e. emanation)
    - EU: independent roles for “eurocrats”
    - World Bank: independent culture and agendas
    - UN peacekeeping: agenda often conflicts with member states
    - UNHCR: autonomy and authority grew over time
- **Principal-agent (P-A) analysis** could treat IOs as actors
    - Problem: needs an _a priori_ spec of what IOs want, and IOs are created by their principals
    - Works in economics because lawyers and doctors exist independently of clients
    - Niskanen budget/turf maximizing hypothesis is battered (ex. NATO and OSCE bureaucrats opposed their own expansion)
    - Realism and liberalism are theories about states, no basis for IO utility functions
    - Ethnographies show goals shaped by _professional norms_, varied and in flux
### The Power of IOs
- Two sources of autonomy and authority
    1. legitimacy of **rational-legal authority**
    2. control over **technical expertise and information**
- Autonomy is a _constitutive effect_ (a bureaucracy wouldn’t be one without these)
#### Sources of IO Autonomy and Authority
- Weber was _ambivalent_ about bureaucracy
    - Pros: stable, predictable, nonviolent, brings precision, knowledge, continuity
    - Cons: can be autonomous from creators and dominate the societies they serve
##### Rational-legal authority
- Modern authority invested in _legalities, procedures, rules_ (impersonal), not a leader or tradition
- “Rational” = uses socially recognized knowledge to make rules
- Power comes from “Sorry, those are the rules” and “just doing my job”
##### Control over info and expertise
- Specialized knowledge not available to others
- Gives power _over_ politicians, bureaucracies shape policy and don’t just implement it
##### The irony
- Power comes from the appearance of **depoliticization** (neutral, technocratic, impersonal)
- Weber: behind functional purposes stand “ideas of culture-values,” so bureaucracy always serves _some_ social purpose, not necessarily good
- Also saw the dark side: “iron cage,” “specialists without spirit”
    - impersonal rules dehumanize
    - repressive in the name of general rules
    - rewards narrow careerists
- Critique of IR scholars
    - Realists and neoliberals treat IOs as technical and apolitical
    - Strange (realist) says info is power but IOs are just state agents
    - Neoliberals treat info technocratically
    - Leveling info asymmetries among states _creates new asymmetries between IOs and states_
##### Examples
- **UN peacekeepers**: authority from claiming to be neutral implementers of Security Council resolutions
- **World Bank**: influence far above its budget share because of expertise
    - “best and brightest” development experts, elite credentials
    - neutral, apolitical image
    - “dictated” content, direction, scope of global development for 50 yrs
- **UNHCR**: expert status plus role implementing refugee law
    - makes life and death decisions without consulting refugees
- Expertise doesn’t need to be “scientific”
#### Three types of IO power (all flow from structuring knowledge)
1. **Classification**
    - Bureaucracies classify and organize info, which is bound up with power
    - “To classify is to engage in an act of power”
    - ex. **refugee** category (vs displaced persons, exiles, economic migrants, etc.)
        - shapes who gets help
        - discursively assumes refugees are powerless so no need to consult them
    - ex. World Bank defining “peasant” vs farmer or day laborer
        - only certain classes have knowledge the machinery recognizes (Gran)
2. **Fixing of meanings**
    - Naming the social context sets boundaries of acceptable action
    - ex. **development** (Escobar)
        - created a huge apparatus
        - determines who does the developing (states, IOs) and who is the object (local groups)
    - ex. **security** redefined after the Cold War
        - now includes individuals, economic, environmental, political threats
        - empowers different actors and practices
    - Consequence: legitimates _more IO intervention_ in domestic affairs, esp. Third World states
        - development: advisors running monetary policy, restructuring rural regions, regulating family and reproductive practices
        - security: human rights abuses classified as security threats (apartheid South Africa), “environmental security,” UNDP human security
3. **Diffusion of norms**
    - IOs as **conveyor belts** for norms and models of “good” political behaviour
    - IO officials = the “missionaries” of our time
    - Needs state power too, but they work in tandem
    - ex. **decolonization**
        - UN Charter universalized sovereignty, built apparatus (Trusteeship Council)
        - delegitimized colonialism, empowered bureaucrats to set norms for “stateness”
        - coupled sovereignty with territorial integrity (Congo peacekeeping, 1960s)
    - ex. police training in non-Western states
        - police needs judiciary, needs lawyers, needs code of law, so a package of reforms that turns non-Western societies into Western ones
    - ex. IMF and World Bank teaching states to “be” market economies
    - ex. NATO expansion (Perry), EBRD, OSCE, UN Agenda for Democratization
    - Once democratization and human rights tie to peace and security, the line between international and domestic governance is erased
- Realist and neoliberal reply: classifications and norms just reflect strong states’ preferences
    - Authors: possible, but their theories have no way to _test_ for autonomy since it can’t exist by axiom
    - EU studies haven’t clearly favoured intergovernmentalism
    - Human rights, weapons taboos, environment: NGOs and IOs promote policies strong states didn’t initially back
    - Need intensive empirical study of which mechanisms produce which effects under which conditions
### The Pathologies of IOs
- Folk wisdom: bureaucracies are inefficient and unresponsive
    - IR scholars rarely study IO dysfunction because their theories assume IOs respond to states (axiom, not hypothesis)
    - Policymakers noticed first
- **Dysfunction** = behaviour that undermines the IO’s _stated objectives_ (judged against public mission)
- **Pathology** = dysfunction traced to _bureaucratic culture_ specifically
#### Typology of theories of dysfunction (Figure 1)
- Two dimensions: inside vs outside the org, material vs cultural
1. **Internal / material**: _bureaucratic politics_ (Allison)
    - competition among subunits over turf, budgets, staff
    - “name of the game is politics”
2. **External / material**: realist and neoliberal state preferences
    - IOs forced to choose “between the bad and the awful”
    - Keohane: IOs act not because they’re strong and autonomous but because they’re _weak_
3. **External / cultural**: _world polity model_
    - IOs seek symbolic legitimacy over efficiency (ex. multilateral arms-export control regimes, not because they’re most efficient)
    - World polity has contradictions (market economics vs human equality), IOs mirror them
4. **Internal / cultural**: **pathologies** (their argument)
#### Why bureaucratic culture breeds pathology
- Rules, routines, SOPs make bureaucracies effective but compromise means-ends rationality
    - may obscure missions, create “ritualized behaviour,” parochial normative environment
- Specialization and compartmentalization help but limit field of vision
    - professional training creates distinct worldviews
- Culture shapes _rationality itself_, not just bounded rationality
- Culture isn’t always bad, effects are an empirical question
- Connection to pathology is **probabilistic, not deterministic**
- Three conditions that intensify the tendencies
    1. vague mission
    2. weak feedback from environment
    3. strong professionalism

#### Five mechanisms

1. **Irrationality of rationalization**
    - Rules and procedures become ends in themselves, missions get tailored to the rulebook
    - ex. World Bank formulated goals from existing data-collection procedures
    - ex. UN-mandated elections as the measure of “success” even when premature
        - Bosnia: ratified ethnic cleansing
        - Africa: exacerbated ethnic tensions
    - ex. peacekeepers (trained for interstate conflict, consent-based) sent into domestic conflicts, more aggressive posture
2. **Bureaucratic universalism**
    - Universal rules and categories flatten diversity, inattentive to context
    - ex. IMF applied the same austerity plus high interest rates in Asia as Latin America, made things worse (IMF admitted this later)
    - ex. peacekeepers moved Cambodia to Bosnia or Somalia
        - Akashi: neutrality and no force worked in Cambodia, failed to defend safe havens in Bosnia
3. **Normalization of deviance** (from Vaughan on _Challenger_)
    - Small calculated rule deviations become routine, staff turnover means people forget they were ever risky
    - ex. UNHCR before 1980: repatriation had to be safe and voluntary (_nonrefoulement_)
        - barriers steadily lowered, more involuntary repatriation
        - authors note no one has really studied this in IOs yet
4. **Insulation**
    - Orgs cut off from feedback develop worldviews that ignore info needed for their goals
    - Two causes
        1. **professionalism** (doctors value life, soldiers sacrifice, economists value efficiency)
        2. hard-to-measure performance, no competition to weed out inefficiency
    - ex. World Bank dominated by economists
        - Ferguson’s Lesotho study: Bank “creates” a Lesotho that fits problems it knows how to solve
    - No effective **feedback loops**
        - Bank has a record of “failures,” little interest in evaluating its own projects
        - UN Somalia lessons-learned conferences structured so nothing blemishes the record (Chopra)
        - sometimes new criteria are hoisted so failures count as successes
5. **Cultural contestation**
    - Org coherence is an _accomplishment_, hierarchy is incomplete, subunits develop own cultures
    - Worst in high-profile, vague-mission IOs like the UN
    - ex. **neutrality vs humanitarianism** at the UN, Bosnia
        - “all necessary means” but fear of taking sides
        - contradictory policies
        - Tharoor: “extremely difficult to make war and peace with the same people on the same territory at the same time”
    - ex. UNHCR Protection Division (legalistic, “refugee’s lawyer”) vs regional bureaus (consider state pressures, peace deals)
        - clash when repatriation is contemplated in unstable areas

- Mechanisms interact, and external environment still shapes internal culture
    - state demands can override internal culture or create pathology-prone structures

### Conclusion

- We know little about IOs’ internal workings and effects, largely because of the _economistic lens_
    - Economists ask why firms exist, political scientists ask why IOs exist (cooperation as the anomaly)
    - So research focuses on bargains states strike, not day-to-day operations
- Three implications
    1. IOs can be treated as **purposive actors** (autonomy from state members, maybe ontologically independent)
    2. IOs are **powerful actors** with independent effects, including on the _constitutive basis_ of global politics
    3. Challenges **uncritical optimism** about IOs
        - Bureaucracies can be inefficient, ineffective, repressive, unaccountable
        - Wilsonian liberals = IOs as engines of peace and progress
        - Neoliberals = overcome collective action problems
        - Realists = stabilizing forces
        - Constructivists too often focus on humane, other-regarding features, but social construction doesn’t necessitate “good” outcomes
- Not saying IOs are “bad,” normative evaluation should be _empirical and ethical_, not an analytic assumption

**Do IOs have independent power from their member states?**

- Yes
    - rational-legal authority plus control of expertise
    - classify, fix meanings, diffuse norms
    - EU, World Bank, UNHCR studies show independent agendas
- No (neorealist and neoliberal view)
    - IOs created by states to serve state interests, states would pull the plug otherwise
    - classifications and norms mostly reflect strong state preferences, so power is epiphenomenal
    - Authors’ reply: this can’t be tested under their own axioms, needs empirical work
