### 13. International Ethics (Richard Shapcott)

#### Framing Questions

- Do states and their citizens have significant moral duties to members of other countries?
- Should states and militaries be morally constrained in war?
- Who is morally responsible for alleviating global poverty?
- What principles should guide states, NGOs, corporations, and individuals in their relations with everybody else?

#### Introduction

- **Ethics** is the study of what actors _ought_ to do, not what they've done
- International ethics is about **transboundary duties**: how members of political communities (mostly states) should treat those beyond their borders
- Two core questions
    1. Should outsiders be treated by the same principles as insiders, as moral equals?
    2. What does treating outsiders as equals mean in substance?
- Quandaries
    - Should we go without to help outsiders, and how much?
    - Duties of wealth redistribution, or merely charity?
    - Forgo free trade gains if they harm others?
    - Balancing compatriots vs others affected by our actions
- Globalization raises the stakes
    - More ways to harm each other, intentionally or not (ex. driving a car, buying clothes)
    - Fairness issues in global economic governance
    - Greater awareness of **distant strangers'** suffering
    - Westphalian ethics (only minor moral weight for outsiders) seems less adequate
    - No single standard of fairness among states, so whose principles apply?
- Core challenge: can we define principles _everyone_ could agree on?
    - Other questions are about consequences (ex. does aid help or hinder?) and assume we should treat all as equal

#### Historical and intellectual contexts

- Terrain set in the 18th century by legal scholars and philosophers
- Three developments in the 18th/19th centuries
    1. Enlightenment and French Revolution: human equality via rights doctrine (Declaration of the Rights of Man, US Declaration of Independence), but in national contexts
    2. European empires and colonialism: moral _inequality_, justified by supposed inferiority
    3. Nationalism: chauvinism and disregard for outsiders
- Enduring tension: what we owe each other as **humans** vs as **fellow nationals**
- More recent: the Holocaust and racial inequality; decolonization (ended formal racial and political hierarchy, endorsed self-determination); 1945 UN human rights declaration as a (contested) universal moral vocabulary
- Much recent thought asks whether these two values can be reconciled

#### The study of ethics: methods

- Most academic debate draws on analytical philosophy
    - **Deontological**: rules right in themselves, not by consequences
        - Kantian: rules everyone could agree on (_universalizability_)
    - **Consequentialist**: judged by outcomes
        - Realism: right if it serves the state's interests
        - **Utilitarianism**: greatest good of the greatest number
    - Religion arguably provides most of the world's moral guidance; most everyday ethics mixes both
- **Continental philosophy** is the alternative
    - Analytical: abstract, decontextualized, universalistic, tied to liberal ethics
    - Continental: contextual, ethics from relationships, sceptical of abstract universalism
        - Hegelian, communitarian, poststructuralist
- **Poststructuralists**: sceptical of universalism, liberalism, cosmopolitanism, even "humanity" and the state
    - "Universal" community reflects Western Enlightenment ideas of what a human is, so it justifies excluding those who don't fit
    - "Humanity" and "humanitarianism" justify war and unequal treatment of non-Western "others"
    - Not necessarily anti-cosmopolitan, but they challenge taken-for-granted meanings
- European and Enlightenment thought (and Christianity, esp. just war) dominates; non-Western traditions are largely absent except the 1990s "Asian values" debate
    - Postcolonial challenge: how to engage ethical thought outside the canon

#### The ethical significance of boundaries: cosmopolitanism and statism

- **Cosmopolitans**: morality is universal; what defines us morally is our humanity
- **Communitarians**: morality comes from particular communities, so it's particular
- Is humanity one moral community, or separate communities with no common morality?
- Most ethical thought on IR sits within a **cosmopolitan horizon**: some duties to all humans
    - Usually expressed as human rights, i.e. equal moral standing regardless of race, gender, belief
    - National identity and well-being shouldn't come at outsiders' expense
- Box 13.1 key quotes
    - Nussbaum: give humanity's ingredients (reason, moral capacity) our first allegiance
    - Pogge: liberal cosmopolitanism = **individualism, universality, generality**
    - Linklater: wrong to export suffering or benefit from exploiting the vulnerable
    - Miller (statism): a world of diversity with different citizenship rights and social justice schemes
- History: Diogenes ("citizen of the world"), ancient Rome; Kant is the most comprehensive modern defence
    - **Categorical imperative** (Box 13.2): treat humanity always as an end, never simply as a means
        - Ex. violations: slavery; interstate war (reduces people to means)
    - Treating people as ends requires thinking universally, so borders are morally irrelevant
- Forms: many religious ethics are cosmopolitan (Christianity, Islam), but mostly _liberal deontological_
    - Based on rights (Shue, Caney), capabilities (Nussbaum), social contract, utilitarianism (Singer), democracy (Held)
    - **Moral** (individuals' acts) vs **institutional** cosmopolitanism (rules governing societies)
- **Positive duties** (to act): create a just order, assistance, humanitarianism, responsibility to protect
- **Negative duties** (to avoid harm): non-intervention, don't harm
    - Problem: needs clear causation; diffuse harms (ex. global warming) complicate it
    - Some add a positive duty to prevent harms, plus compensation or redress
- Linklater's three relationships
    1. Bilateral (what "we" do to "them")
        - Ex. laws punishing "sex tourism" abroad
    2. Third-party (what they do to each other)
        - Ex. genocide
    3. Global (what we all do to each other)
        - Ex. global warming

##### Thick and thin cosmopolitanism

- Many now prefer thick vs thin to cosmopolitan vs statist, since basic human rights are widely accepted
    - Real difference: how _demanding_ cross-border obligations are
- **Thick**: institutions should guarantee global equality of rights and goods (**global egalitarianism**)
    - Extensive positive and negative duties; radically transformed global order
    - Dominates academic debate
- **Thin / statist**: only minimal duties (don't harm, emergency aid, minimal human rights)
    - Better describes state practice
    - Defends the state for national and communal self-determination
    - Draws on communitarianism: _associational_ duties from membership in a bounded community
    - Compatriots have priority, but with a "basic moral minimum" (famine relief, don't inflict unnecessary suffering)

##### Opposing Opinions 13.1: Who should pay for climate change?

- **Is it fair for China to contribute as much as the US to the costs?**
    - For (current emitters pay)
        - _Polluter pays_: US, China, India, Europe emit disproportionately; China and the US are top emitters
        - Negative duty: cease harmful activity and switch to renewables
        - Positive duty to aid those harmed most (Africa, small island states)
        - Historical emissions are unfair to count: past generations acted in ignorance; descendants shouldn't pay for honest mistakes
    - Against (historical emissions count)
        - Warming comes from two centuries of emissions, so those with the highest _historical_ totals pay (US, Europe, OECD)
        - China and India have been high emitters only ~2 decades
        - OECD emissions are mostly "luxury"; poorer countries' are "survival" emissions for development
        - High emitters should help the renewable transition

1. Should we calculate costs by fairness principles?
2. Is it fair for China to pay as much as the US?
3. What principles should decide how to handle the costs of global warming?

##### Ethics of migration (Case Study 13.1)

- **Is there a right to exclude?**
    - For (Walzer)
        - Membership is the primary good we distribute; it structures all other choices
        - States must choose who enters to survive as independent political communities
        - Rights of belonging override free movement
    - Against (some liberals)
        - Freedom of movement is a basic right; restrictions violate it
        - Refusing entry just redirects claims to other states, so it carries moral responsibility
- **Refugees and asylum seekers**
    - Most ethicists accept a right of asylum and a duty to accept that overrides exclusion
    - Cosmopolitan view: weigh insiders' and outsiders' interests impartially; survival outweighs the state's quality of life

1. How should we set rules on migration and entry?
2. Do asylum seekers' rights outweigh political communities' rights?

- Poststructuralists try to disrupt the cosmopolitan/statist binary
    - Both use strategies of exclusion and domination (D. Campbell 1994)
    - Some reframe cosmopolitanism away from abstract individualism (Burke, Dallmayr)

#### Realist ethics

- Anarchy and sovereignty mean the only viable ethics are **self-interest and survival**
    - Called Machiavellian at worst, amoral at best, but realists claim a more pragmatic _morality_
- Morgenthau: appeals to universal morals just reflect a nation's own preconceptions
- Statesperson's duty is state survival, so **self-help is a moral duty**, not just a necessity
    - Ex. Kissinger: bombing neutral Laos if it serves defeating North Vietnam
    - Ex. backing rights-violating governments (Pinochet's Chile, Pakistan) against foes (USSR, ISIS)
- Carr: scepticism toward claims of universal morality (cynical mask or delusion)
- Critiques
    - Can slip into opportunism and justify anything
    - Not every choice is survival vs destruction; it's often advantage vs disadvantage
    - Favours the national interest, the status quo, and nationalism, so realism is **prescriptive and normative**, not only descriptive
- Beardsworth (2015): under globalization, Morgenthau's responsibility ethic supports cosmopolitan policy (ex. climate)

##### Key Points

- Globalization supports cosmopolitan theory (universal human community, everyone equal)
- Cosmopolitans stress both positive (assistance, hospitality) and negative (don't harm) duties
- Thick: primacy of obligations to humanity; thin: primacy of duties to fellow nationals
- Realists: necessity demands statist ethics and prudence over "moralism"
- Some realists: globalization adds cosmopolitan responsibilities for statespeople

#### Global justice, poverty, and starvation

- Globalization has produced large inequality and more absolute poverty and starvation
    - Pogge: there's now enough wealth to end poverty quickly and cheaply
- Whose responsibility is it?
- Three lines of argument
    
    1. **Utilitarian**: individual positive duties of assistance
    2. **Global egalitarian**: globally just distributive system
    3. **Sufficientarian**: minimal positive duties to aid, not global equality
    
    - Cutting across: Pogge's _negative duty_ argument

##### The Singer solution

- Impartial, utilitarian morality: those who can help ought to, regardless of causal link
- Principle: if we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to
- **Drowning child** analogy (from the 1972 Bangladeshi famine): muddy clothes vs a child's life
    - So we should donate a significant percentage of discretionary income (Box 13.4)
- Criticisms
    - Likely ineffective: relies on individuals' sense of obligation; needs state action
    - Ignores the wealthy's role in _causing_ poverty

##### Global egalitarianism and liberal institutional cosmopolitanism

- Beitz, Moellendorf, Pogge: interdependence creates a duty to build a globally just institutional scheme
    - Equal basic rights and duties and equal chance at a full life
    - Goes beyond charity: overhaul global and domestic institutions
- **Rawls** (Box 13.5)
    - Justice begins with the **basic structure** of society
    - **Original position** and **veil of ignorance**: designers don't know where they'll end up (rich, poor, race, gender, talent)
    - Would choose equal basic liberties plus the **difference principle**: inequality is unjust unless it improves the worst-off
    - Internationally, a second contracting session among representatives of _peoples_
- Rawls rejected global distributive justice, but most Rawlsians say his own premises support it
    - Pogge: apply the difference principle globally
    - Beitz: compensate for uneven natural resources, past injustices, and redistribute a share of the product of _global_ cooperation
- **Thin / statist counter**
    - No global state or demos
    - International order is a _modus vivendi_, not mutual advantage
    - No shared community or destiny to ground distributive norms
    - Distributive justice applies only within states
    - So only duties of assistance, not permanent redistribution (ex. a tax system)
- Rawls's **The Law of Peoples** (Box 13.7): independence, treaties, non-intervention, self-defence only, human rights, limits in war, mutual aid

##### Pogge's solution

- Unlike Singer, stresses the **causal link** between rich countries' wealth and the poor's poverty
    - Rules of international order disadvantage the poor
    - Richest countries collectively responsible for ~18 million poverty deaths a year (his figure)
- Wealthy states have a negative duty to stop imposing this order
    - Which gives rise to positive duties to design a just order that helps the neediest
    - Statist objections don't cancel it, independent of any "communal bond"
- Critiques both Singer and statism
- **Explanatory nationalism**: causes of poverty are mostly domestic (culture, corruption, lack of democracy), so no global responsibility (Miller 2007)
    - Pogge and Wenar: powerful states are complicit in corrupt, undemocratic states
        - Bribery
        - **International resource privilege** (Box 13.8): any ruler who takes power can sell the country's resources and be recognized, so outsiders and insiders have incentives for coups, civil war, corruption
        - Pharmaceutical industry practices (Pogge 2009; Wenar 2008)
    - Counter (Risse 2009): there's less poverty than before, and the order has improved the poor's plight

##### Key Points

- Debate dominated by utilitarian and Rawlsian theories (individual vs institutional responsibility)
- Cosmopolitans: the rich must help, from positive and negative duties
- Thick: globally egalitarian distribution; thin: only humanitarian duties of assistance

#### Just war tradition (JWT)

- Guidelines for when a state may go to war and how it may fight (a _tradition_, not a "theory")
- Revival from two developments
    - Nuclear weapons (theological reflection on non-discriminatory weapons)
    - Vietnam War: Walzer's _Just and Unjust Wars_ (1977)
- Applies moral limits to resort to war and to harms against states, forces, civilians
- Three parts
    1. **Jus ad bellum** (resort to war)
    2. **Jus in bello** (conduct in war)
    3. **Jus post bellum** (after war; proposed)
- Both cosmopolitan and statist
    - Christian theology since Augustine, plus Walzer's _legalist_ tradition (rules for what states owe each other)
    - Justifications go to other states: defence of sovereignty, arguably the society of states
    - Jus in bello is more cosmopolitan (civilians, harm minimization), codified in the Geneva Conventions and weapons treaties

##### Boxes: criteria

- **Jus ad bellum**
    - Just cause (self-defence or defence of a third party)
    - Right authority (only states)
    - Right intention (address injustice or aggression, not glory or loot)
    - Last resort
    - Reasonable hope of success
    - Restoration of peace
    - Proportionality of means and ends
- **Jus in bello**
    - Proportionality of means (ex. don't use a nuke if a conventional weapon will do)
    - **Non-combatant immunity** (central; Coates: how can a theory of war as an instrument of justice countenance suppressing non-combatants' rights?)
    - **Law of double effect**: unintended but foreseeable civilian losses can be permissible
        - Real issue: can foreseeable deaths be unintended, and is responsibility the same?
- **Jus post bellum** (proposed)
    - Proportionality and publicity; vindication of rights; discrimination (no sweeping socio-economic sanctions on civilians)
    - Punishment: proportionate for aggressors; fair public trials for leaders; soldiers on all sides accountable
    - Compensation; rehabilitation of institutions (proportionate to the regime's depravity; Orend)
- **Islam and war**
    - War is central to Islamic ethics; at limited times it's incumbent on Muslims, if only defensively
    - Debate: no pacifist tradition vs Islam compatible with pacifism
    - Some authorities have argued for spreading Islam's realm; most say the Koran sanctions war only in self-defence
    - Most reject Al Qaeda's reading of defence and attacks on civilians
    - Islamic equivalents of right authority, right intent, civilian immunity
- Realist objection: JWT unjustifiably limits statecraft; politics is the realm of necessity, so any means serve the state's ends
- Pacifist objection: JWT gives war a veneer of legitimacy and expands the realm of warfare

##### Case Study 13.2: Targeting civilians and non-combatant immunity

- WWII: all sides routinely violated it
    - Allied _area bombing_ of Germany and Japan; Axis attacks on civilians
    - Ex. **Dresden**: no military significance, firestorm killed 100,000+
    - Same logic: US bombing of Japanese cities, Hiroshima and Nagasaki
- Defence: consequentialist (ends the war sooner, saves lives), but _survival can justify anything_
- Double effect allows unintended civilian deaths, but foreseen deaths complicate it
    - Ex. Israel in Gaza (Dec 2008): dense population plus Hamas firing from there, so civilian casualties were highly foreseeable
    - Belligerents exploit immunity and double effect by placing military sites in civilian areas
    - Critics: only drawing the line at _intended_ deaths lets planners anticipate as many as they like (Sjoberg 2006)

1. Can any circumstances justify violating civilian immunity?
2. Should planners also _minimize_ civilian deaths?

- The biggest problem: when is the usual ban on killing suspended in war?
    - Walzer: **moral equality of soldiers** (mutual vulnerability); laying down arms ends permission to kill
    - McMahan: just cause must link to jus in bello; unjust combatants have no legitimate targets
        - Both non-combatants and _just_ combatants are impermissible targets for unjust combatants
- Just war thinking is generally justified on consequentialist grounds (better a world where most observe limits)
- Pacifists tend to argue deontologically, which consequentialists reject

##### Key Points

- Three components: ad bellum, in bello, post bellum
- Permits war but restricts how it's fought
- Both cosmopolitan and statist arguments
- Double effect is the most controversial part
- Justifying war means asking when killing is permissible

#### Conclusion

- Ethical issues confront all international actors, especially states (greater capacity to aid or harm)
- With interdependence, the question for most writers is _how_ to be ethical, not whether
- Cosmopolitanism appears in the international order, but most state practice and most people prioritize compatriots
    - Especially on global warming and immigration
- Real agreement on three points
    1. Basic human rights should be observed
    2. Freedom from poverty and starvation is universally desirable
    3. National boundaries shouldn't stop us from treating others with respect
