## Chapter 21 — The United Nations
### 21.1 Introduction
- The **United Nations (UN)** was established in 1945 after the Second World War to create a more peaceful and cooperative international system.
- It is made up of:
- A central system headquartered in New York.
	- Specialized agencies, such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Labour Organization (ILO).
    - Funds and programmes, such as UNICEF and the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP).
- The UN is the only global institution with near-universal membership and a mandate covering security, development, human rights, and environmental protection.
- Its central tension is the relationship between **state sovereignty** and the protection of people.
    - The UN was created by states and traditionally respects their authority within their borders.
    - However, it increasingly addresses problems within states, including human rights violations, civil wars, and humanitarian crises.
- The UN's responsibilities have expanded, but its effectiveness remains contested.
    - The Iraq War (2003), intervention in Libya (2011), the Syrian conflict, and the war in Ukraine have raised questions about its authority and ability to respond to crises.
### 21.2 A Brief History of the UN and Its Principal Organs
- The UN was officially established on **24 October 1945** by 51 countries. By 2022, membership had grown to 193 states.
- The UN Charter established four main purposes:
  - Maintain international peace and security.
  - Develop friendly relations among nations.
  - Cooperate in solving international problems and promoting human rights.
  - Provide a centre for coordinating the actions of nations.
- **The League of Nations**
  - The UN's predecessor was established after the First World War to prevent future conflicts.
  - It was weakened by its reliance on unanimous decisions, lack of enforcement powers, and the absence of important states such as the United States.
  - Its decisions were recommendations rather than binding resolutions.
- **How the UN addressed these weaknesses**
  - The Security Council was given primary responsibility for international peace and security.
  - Its decisions can be binding on member states.
  - The system recognizes the influence of major powers through permanent membership and veto authority.
- **Principal organs of the UN:**
  - **Security Council:** Responsible for maintaining international peace and security. It has 15 members, including five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, and China). The permanent members can veto substantive resolutions. The Council can authorize sanctions, arms embargoes, peacekeeping, and military action.
  - **General Assembly:** Includes every UN member state, each with one vote. It discusses international issues and makes recommendations, but most resolutions are not legally binding.
  - **Secretariat:** Carries out the UN's administrative and operational work. It is led by the Secretary-General, who can bring threats to international peace to the Security Council's attention under Article 99.
  - **Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC):** Coordinates the UN's economic and social work and consults with NGOs.
  - **International Court of Justice (ICJ):** The UN's principal judicial organ. It settles legal disputes between states when they accept its jurisdiction and provides advisory opinions.
  - **Trusteeship Council:** Originally supervised territories preparing for independence. It suspended operations in 1994 after all trust territories achieved self-government or independence.
- **The Security Council veto**
  - The five permanent members were given special authority because they were considered the major powers when the UN was founded.
  - A substantive resolution requires at least nine votes and no veto from a permanent member.
  - This creates tension between the principle of equality among states and the reality of unequal political power.
- **Security Council reform**
  - Many countries argue that the Council no longer reflects today's distribution of economic and political power.
  - Countries such as Germany, Japan, India, Brazil, and South Africa have been discussed as potential candidates for greater representation.
  - Reform is difficult because states disagree over representation, and existing permanent members are unlikely to surrender their veto power.
- **The UN system**
  - Specialized agencies have their own constitutions, budgets, and leadership, but coordinate with the wider UN system.
  - Funds and programmes, such as UNICEF and UNDP, are more directly supervised by the General Assembly and rely heavily on voluntary contributions.
### 21.3 The UN and the Maintenance of International Peace and Security
- **The Cold War**
  - Rivalry between the United States and Soviet Union frequently prevented the Security Council from acting.
  - Both powers could use their vetoes to block decisions that threatened their interests.
  - The UN's ability to intervene in conflicts was also restricted by competing political interests and the principle of state sovereignty.
- **Changes after the Cold War**
  - The UN became more involved in civil wars, humanitarian emergencies, and conflicts within states.
  - International security increasingly came to include human rights, poverty, inequality, and refugee movements, rather than only aggression between countries.
  - Peacekeeping operations expanded rapidly during the 1990s.
- **Different approaches to maintaining peace**
  - **Preventive diplomacy:** Attempts to stop disputes from developing into violent conflicts through negotiation, fact-finding, and confidence-building.
  - **Peacemaking:** Uses peaceful negotiations and mediation to bring hostile parties to an agreement.
  - **Peacekeeping:** Deploys UN personnel to monitor agreements and maintain stability, traditionally with the consent of the parties.
  - **Peace enforcement:** Uses or authorizes force under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, potentially without the consent of the parties.
  - **Post-conflict peacebuilding:** Rebuilds political, economic, and social institutions to prevent renewed conflict.
- **Sovereignty and intervention**
  - **Sovereignty** means that states have authority over their own territory and are generally protected from outside interference.
  - Article 2(7) of the UN Charter protects states from UN intervention in matters within their domestic jurisdiction.
  - However, the UN increasingly recognizes that governments have responsibilities toward their populations.
  - This creates a conflict between respecting state sovereignty and protecting people from serious human rights abuses.
- **The Responsibility to Protect (R2P)**
  - The 2005 World Summit recognized that states have a responsibility to protect their populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity.
  - If a state is manifestly failing to protect its population and peaceful measures are inadequate, collective action may be taken through the Security Council under Chapter VII.
  - The principle does not mean that individual countries can automatically intervene whenever they claim humanitarian concerns.
- **Examples of intervention**
  - **Kosovo (1999):** NATO intervened without Security Council authorization because Russia was expected to veto action. The intervention was justified by NATO states as necessary to stop ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity.
  - **Libya (2011):** The Security Council authorized a no-fly zone and necessary measures to protect civilians. A coalition intervened, and NATO later assumed command.
  - **Darfur (2007):** The UN faced difficulties deploying peacekeepers without Sudanese government consent. A mission was eventually established after negotiations.
  - These examples demonstrate the difficulty of balancing humanitarian protection, state sovereignty, and international law.
- **From peacekeeping to peacebuilding**
  - The UN increasingly recognized that security, development, and human rights are interconnected.
  - The **Peacebuilding Commission**, established in 2005, coordinates strategies for countries recovering from conflict.
  - Its purpose is to reduce the likelihood that countries will return to violence after peace agreements or peacekeeping missions.
- **Limitations of UN peace operations**
  - Member states disagree over the use of force and the appropriate role of international intervention.
  - Peacekeepers can become targets themselves, and some missions have struggled to protect civilians.
  - Allegations of sexual exploitation and abuse by UN personnel have damaged the organization's credibility.
  - Regional organizations and coalitions sometimes conduct operations instead of, or alongside, the UN.
**Case Study 21.1 — The UN and Conflict in Syria**
- The Syrian conflict began in 2011 when the government used deadly force against pro-democracy demonstrations.
- The conflict became increasingly complicated as foreign powers supported different groups:
    - Russia and Iran supported the Syrian government.
    - The United States and other countries initially supported some opposition groups.
    - The Islamic State gained territory beginning in 2013, leading to further international military involvement.
- The UN struggled to respond because Russia and China vetoed proposed Security Council action against the Syrian government.
- The UN did achieve some progress:
    - Security Council resolutions addressed humanitarian assistance and peace negotiations.
    - A Commission of Inquiry investigated alleged human rights violations and chemical weapons attacks.
    - The UN organized negotiations in Geneva between the government and opposition groups.
- However, the negotiations failed to resolve major disagreements, including whether President Assad would remain in power.
- **Main lesson:** The Syrian conflict demonstrates how great power rivalry and the Security Council veto can prevent international action, even during major humanitarian crises.
### 21.4 The UN and Economic and Social Questions
- The UN Charter identifies economic and social development as important goals, including improving living standards and promoting social progress.
- The UN's economic work has been shaped by two major divisions:
    - **Cold War rivalry:** The US and USSR disagreed over the best approaches to economic development.
    - **North–South divide:** Developed and developing countries disagreed over global economic inequality and the distribution of resources.
- Initially, the Bretton Woods institutions played a major role in international economic development. Their voting structures and policies gave wealthy Western countries significant influence.
- **Decolonization and developing countries**
    - As newly independent states joined the UN, developing countries gained greater influence in the General Assembly.
    - They pushed for greater attention to global inequality and more equitable economic arrangements.
    - **UNCTAD** became an important forum for discussing trade and development.
    - The **Group of 77** formed as a coalition of developing countries seeking to advance their shared economic interests.
- **The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)**
    - Established in 1965 to support development and reduce poverty.
    - Helped promote the concept of **human development**, which considers people's well-being and opportunities rather than economic growth alone.
    - Since 1990, it has published the annual Human Development Report.
- **Global conferences and new priorities**
    - During the 1990s, the UN organized major conferences on environmental protection, human rights, population, and women's rights.
    - These conferences helped turn broad international concerns into specific programmes and policy goals.
    - They also demonstrated the growing recognition that economic, social, and environmental problems are interconnected.
- **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)**
    - Established in 2000 with targets for reducing poverty, improving education, and combating diseases such as HIV/AIDS and malaria.
    - They produced significant progress, but results varied between countries and regions.
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**
    - Adopted in 2015, with 17 goals intended to guide global development through 2030.
    - They cover poverty, hunger, health, education, gender equality, environmental protection, and other development priorities.
- **Climate change cooperation**
    - The UN provides a forum for international climate negotiations.
    - The 1992 Earth Summit helped establish the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change.
    - The 1997 Kyoto Protocol introduced binding emissions targets for certain countries.
    - The 2015 Paris Agreement established a global framework for limiting temperature increases, with countries setting their own emissions targets.
    - Climate negotiations remain difficult because countries disagree over responsibility, costs, and how emissions reductions should be achieved.
- **Financial limitations**
    - The UN depends on assessed contributions from member states for its regular budget.
    - Many funds and programmes rely on voluntary contributions.
    - Late or unpaid contributions can create serious financial difficulties and restrict the organization's ability to operate.
### 21.5 Conclusion
- The UN has developed from an organization primarily concerned with preventing war between states into one that also addresses internal conflict, human rights, development, and environmental issues.
    - Its universal membership gives it an important degree of international legitimacy.
    - However, its effectiveness depends heavily on the willingness of member states to cooperate.
- Its main challenges include:
	- Great power rivalry and the Security Council veto.
    - Disagreements over state sovereignty and humanitarian intervention.
    - Limited financial resources.
    - Unequal global development and competing national interests.
- **Overall argument:** The UN is essential to international cooperation, but it is not an independent world government. Its ability to act is ultimately limited by the political interests and cooperation of its member states.
### Opposing Opinions 21.1 — Has the United Nations Outlived Its Usefulness?
**For:**
- The Security Council reflects the distribution of power in 1945 rather than today's international system.
- The veto allows powerful states to block action, even during serious conflicts.
- The UN has struggled to prevent wars, protect civilians, and address global inequality.
- Its reliance on member-state funding and cooperation limits its independence.
**Against:**
- The UN provides a global forum where nearly every country can participate.
- It creates opportunities for diplomacy and cooperation that would otherwise be difficult to achieve.
- It coordinates peacekeeping, humanitarian assistance, development, and international agreements.
- Despite its weaknesses, it provides legitimacy and a framework for collective action.
