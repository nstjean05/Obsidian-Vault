### 6.3 The US order under challenge
- Late 1990s: stable US-dominated order starts to be challenged
    - 9/11 showed the darker side of globalization
    - War on terror (Iraq, Afghanistan) exposed the limits of military power
    - Human rights rhetoric vs Guantanamo, Abu Ghraib, rendition undercut moral claims
    - Bush unilateralism undercut the legitimacy of US leadership
- Increased diplomatic activism from large developing countries
    - **Trade G20** at WTO Cancun (2003): blocked the Doha Round, ended in deadlock
        - Led by Brazil, China, India, South Africa
    - **IBSA** (India, Brazil, South Africa), Brasilia Declaration 2003
    - **BASICs** (Brazil, South Africa, India, China) sidelined Europe at Copenhagen climate talks (2009)
- **BRICS** (Brazil, Russia, India, China, plus South Africa from 2010) are the five largest economies outside the OECD
    - ~50% of global FX reserves; major aid donors (China, India, Brazil)
    - China overtook the US as Brazil's top trading partner
    - South-South trade went from marginal to 17.5% of global merchandise exports by 2010
    - Language of "rising powers" took off ~2003, and the debate was about where order is _going_, not where it is
- 2007 financial crisis hit the Western core
    - Undermined the technical and moral authority of the centre of global capitalism
    - Pushed for institutional reform
        - WTO "new Quad" (US, EU, Brazil, India)
        - G20 leaders' summit (2008), replaced the G7 as primary grouping (2009)
    - Western govts told emerging powers to stop using poverty and colonial history as "excuses" to dodge their "responsibilities"
#### Key Points
- 1990s: near universal agreement the system was led by the US and its institutions
- Global South saw the US as rarely a status quo power, since it moulded the system in its own image
- In the 1990s there seemed little alternative to accommodating Western power
#### Case Study 6.1: The BRICS
- Coined by Jim O'Neill (Goldman Sachs, 2001); 2003 report predicted BRICs would be larger than the G7 in under 40 years
    - China/India as suppliers of manufactures and services; Brazil/Russia as raw materials
- Also a diplomatic grouping: FMs met 2006, first summit 2009 in Russia, South Africa joined 2010
    - Views: bargaining coalition or proto-alliance, caucus of marginalized states, or embryo of alternative institutions
        - Ex. New Development Bank, AIIB, Belt and Road Initiative
1. How do investors and IR analysts view the emerging world differently?
2. Is the BRICS grouping an alliance?
#### Case Study 6.2: Brazil
- Cardoso (a dependency theorist): stabilization, liberalization, cautious foreign policy
- Lula: autonomy, coalitions with developing states, multipolar order
    - Little hard power, so relies on soft power ("diplomatic GNP"), legitimacy as spokesperson for the developing world, and cutting inequality and hunger at home
- Later: structural economic problems, violence, polarization
    - Bolsonaro elected 2018 (populist nationalism); Lula jailed, then returned
    - Corruption scandal, mass protests, hit hard by China's slowdown
    - Went from epitome of the rising power to a country in crisis with few options
1. Can soft power substitute for hard power?
2. Will foreign policy activism always be hostage to domestic politics?
### 6.4 Three questions about the power of rising powers
1. **Where is power shifting to?**
    - To major emerging states
        - Ex. "superpower China", "India rising", BRICS/BASICs
    - Or a _general diffusion of power_ ("rise of the rest", Khanna 2009)
        - Ex. MINTs (Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey), private actors, transnational groups
        - Diffusion of power, preferences, and ideas/values
        - Tech plus mobilization: Arab Spring, Brazil protests 2013 (1.5M), Islamic State
        - Effective power gets harder for _everyone_
            - Ex. Ukraine: Putin's bid to control the "near-abroad" showed the difficulty of force against mobilized populations
        - Need to study emerging powers' relations with weaker actors, not just the dominant ones
        - Large fast-developing states struggle to keep coherent foreign policy
2. **What is power?**
    - An _essentially contested concept_; no overarching theory
    - Levels (Barnett and Duvall 2005)
        - Relational: impose your will, resist others
        - Institutional: control the agenda, exclude threatening issues
        - Structural: material and discursive conditions for action
    - **Hard power** is coercive; **soft power** is attraction
        - Arguments against US decline combine all levels (Nye)
    - Ask: influential over what actors, in what period, on what matters?
        - Ex. regional power as a stepping stone; Brazil's "diplomatic GNP"
    - Power can't be separated from motives and values (constructivists)
        - Revisionism comes from ideas more than hard power
            - China: historical humiliation
            - India, Brazil: denied recognition
            - Russia: NATO expansion and a claimed right to regional predominance
3. **Power for what?** (most important)
    - BRICS mattered to Goldman Sachs as markets, which says nothing about political weight
    - Analysis must connect to theory, not just lists of power resources
#### Key Points
- Realists: power is the common currency of IR, but no agreed definition
- Power diffusion is either a shift between states or a broader mobilization of groups
- Liberals and constructivists: power is tied to values, purposes, identities
- Power is relational and best understood in context
#### Opposing Opinions 6.1: Are rising powers powerful enough to affect order?
- **Do rising powers have real influence on order?**
    - For
        - Long-term erosion of Western dominance; US limited in reasserting hegemony
        - More embedded in the global economy than 1970s Third World; South-South ties deeper
        - Needed in functional institutions (economy, climate, pandemics, nukes) and for legitimate governance
    - Against
        - Military power still matters most, and the US has no challenger
        - US keeps agenda-setting power and builds alternatives (mega-regional trade blocs)
        - BRICS are divided: China, India, Russia are competitors
1. Does military power help countries achieve political goals?
2. Does economic development success underpin diplomatic influence?
3. Can you assess rising powers' influence without a clear view of global order?
### 6.5 Debating the impact of rising powers on international relations
#### a. Realists
- IR (international relations) is the rise and fall of great powers; names change, logic doesn't
- Power transitions are dangerous
    - Rising states revise norms; established powers may block them by force
    - Consensus: new powers "count" only through the balance of power (Mearsheimer 2001)
- Focus on measuring material power; coercive power is the "entry card" to great power politics
- Kennedy (1988): economic change drives military balances; wars are won by greater material resources
- **Prestige** (Gilpin 1981): conflict comes from the gap between material capabilities and the prestige hierarchy
    - Ex. India's 1998 nuclear test
    - _Recognition games_, ex. Brazil's troops in Haiti to bolster its UNSC case (Suzuki 2008)
#### b. International society theorists
- Accommodate rising powers to avoid conflict ("Haves" and "Have Nots")
- Great powers are a social category based on legitimacy and recognition
- Return to a pluralist, power-centred order; reform institutions or use informal clubs
    - Ex. G2, G8 + 5, G20 as _concert diplomacy_
#### c. Liberal institutionalists
- Power shifts plus diverse interests worsen collective action problems
    - Ex. WTO deadlock
- Emerging world gained voice and veto power, but alternatives (New Development Bank) are limited
- US built new deals (TPP), then withdrew
- Result: paralysis or fragmentation, with parts of the emerging world left behind
#### d. Critical political economists (neo-Marxist)
- Wrong to focus on nation-states of the Global South
- Real story is the transformation of capitalism into a transnationalized, _deterritorialized_ order
    - Marked by inequality, instability, stratification
- Study the _transnational whole_ and class conflict
#### Key Points
- Realists: rising powers disrupt the balance, causing conflict (ex. US-China "inevitable")
- Material approaches don't explain the path from diffusion to multipolarity, or status and recognition
- International society: stability depends on accommodation
- Marxists: look at global capitalism, not states
### 6.6 Beyond the BRICS: emerging powers in the twenty-first century
- BRICS focus was a moment in time; except for China, rising powers are back to secondary roles
- Why
    1. Return of geopolitics
    2. Instabilities and inequalities of global capitalism
    3. Covid-19
    4. New patterns of mobilization
- Major power conflict is back (US vs Russia and China; Ukraine, Asia-Pacific, Middle East)
    - Realists: globalization feeds back into the _Westphalian_ state system
    - States are resilient in controlling flows and borders
        - Ex. investment screening, cyberspace, resources, vaccines
- **Backlash politics** and nationalism
    - Challenges to the liberal order now come from the _centre_ (US, Europe), not the emerging world
    - Global governance in gridlock, fragmentation, backlash
- Winners and losers (Milanovic 2016)
    - Winners: Asian poor and middle classes; losers: rich-world lower middle classes
    - Mobilized populations plus less capable governments gives an _expectations gap_
- Covid-19 pulled two ways: need for global governance vs walls, travel bans, blame on outsiders
#### Three implications
1. Limits to "emergence"
    - Slower growth, capital flight, debt, 80M more in extreme poverty (2020)
    - O'Neill (2021): BRICS have disappointed; "BRICS fallacy" (Pant 2013)
    - Political crises in Brazil and South Africa
2. "Pathways to power" undermined
    - Institutions were the path, but international law and governance are under challenge
    - Realists: power is hard power
3. More differentiation, harder collaboration
    - Uneven growth; BRICS act together less in the G20; soft-balancing against the US has faded
    - Ex. India: Indo-Pacific focus, Hindu nationalism and hard power under Modi, break from the Nehruvian identity
#### Pushback: why the Global South still matters
- **Is the emerging world still central to global order?**
    - Yes
        - Indispensable for global challenges: objective (forests, emissions, migration), political (veto power), legitimacy
        - Cooperation is about _distributional effects_, ex. weak health systems in the pandemic
        - Historically distinct: still poor per capita, incompletely integrated into a West-set economy
        - Foreign policy shaped by legacies of marginalization
            - Ex. India refusing to take sides on Ukraine; democracy vs autocracy doesn't map onto their interests
    - No
        - Can't overhaul institutions; fragmentation and rivalry keep them secondary
- _Justice shifts_ alongside _power shifts_, ex. BLM, reparations, decolonizing narratives
- **Post-Western order**: BRICS are one stage in a longer revolt against Western dominance (Bull and Watson 1985)
    - Liberal order was never global in the cold war
    - 1989 to early 2000s unipolarity is the historical anomaly
#### Key Points
- Emerging powers (except China) are back to secondary roles
- Many face economic frailty and instability
- Westphalian state system more durable than expected
- Biggest threats to order are backlash and US-China rivalry, not emerging powers
### 6.7 Conclusion
- History hasn't ended; ideological cleavages are back
    - China's illiberal success is the biggest challenge to Western liberal assumptions
- International society writers focused on non-West "membership" and missed how the emerging world navigates a post-Western system
    - Marked by recognition battles, resentment, ambivalence
- West vs non-West and liberal vs illiberal are unhelpful binaries
    - Emerging countries won't fit a democracy vs authoritarianism split (migration, health, climate; alliances don't track democracy)
- What is "global" is the **diffusion of agency and political consciousness**, i.e. more actors able to shape global politics
    - This is why the emerging world still matters
