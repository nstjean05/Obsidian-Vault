## Chapter 33 — Humanitarian Intervention in World Politics
### 33.1 Introduction
- **Humanitarian intervention** raises a fundamental tension between state sovereignty and the protection of human rights.
	- International society is traditionally built on sovereignty, non-intervention, and restrictions on the use of force.
	- However, states sometimes commit genocide or mass atrocities against their own populations or fail to prevent them.
	- This raises the question of whether other states have a responsibility to intervene.
- After the Holocaust, international society established laws prohibiting genocide and protecting basic human rights.
	- These principles can conflict with sovereignty and non-intervention.
	- Should governments that systematically abuse their populations continue to receive the protections of sovereignty?
- During the Cold War, armed humanitarian intervention was generally not considered legitimate because states prioritized sovereignty and international order.
- Attitudes shifted during the 1990s, when some states increasingly accepted intervention to protect civilians.
	- However, the emerging norm remained weak and controversial.
	- The UN Security Council's 2011 authorization of intervention in Libya was the first time it authorized force against a fully functioning sovereign state for humanitarian protection purposes.
- The **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)** emerged as an attempt to establish international agreement on protecting populations from mass atrocities.
	- It recognizes that states have the primary responsibility to protect their own populations.
	- The international community has a responsibility to assist and respond when states fail.
### 33.2 The Case for Humanitarian Intervention
#### 33.2.1 Human Security
- Traditional security approaches focus primarily on the security of states.
	- **Westphalian sovereignty** emphasizes each state's right to govern its own territory without outside interference.
	- Sovereignty and non-intervention are considered essential foundations of international order.
	- This approach assumes that states are the best protectors of their citizens.
- However, states can also become major sources of insecurity.
	- Governments have committed genocide, mass murder, and other forms of systematic violence against their own populations.
	- One study estimates that governments killed approximately 262 million people during the twentieth century.
- The **human security approach**, which emerged in the 1990s, shifts attention from states to individuals and communities.
	- It broadens the meaning of security beyond military threats.
	- Poverty, human rights abuses, gender violence, civil war, and climate change can all threaten human security.
	- States are understood as both potential protectors and potential perpetrators of insecurity.
- This creates a moral and political question: should governments retain sovereign rights when they systematically abuse their populations?
#### 33.2.2 The Legal Argument
- **Counter-restrictionists** argue that international law permits humanitarian intervention under certain circumstances.
	- First, the UN Charter commits states to protecting fundamental human rights.
	- Second, customary international law may recognize a right of humanitarian intervention.
- The UN Charter identifies human rights as a central purpose of the international system.
	- The preamble and Articles 1(3), 55, and 56 emphasize human rights protection.
	- Article 1(3) identifies promoting and encouraging respect for human rights as a principal purpose of the UN.
- Some counter-restrictionists argue that these commitments create a humanitarian exception to Article 2(4), which prohibits the use of force.
	- Michael Reisman argued that the Security Council should have acted against states committing genocide and mass murder during the Cold War.
	- Some lawyers argue that humanitarian intervention does not violate Article 2(4) because it does not seek to undermine a state's political independence or territorial integrity.
- Other counter-restrictionists argue that a right to humanitarian intervention exists under **customary international law**.
	- Customary law requires both consistent state practice and the belief that the practice is legally justified (**opinio juris**).
	- Historical examples include British, French, and Russian intervention in Greece in 1827 and US intervention in Cuba in 1898.
	- Supporters also cite the creation of safe havens in Iraq in 1991 and later arguments for intervention in Rwanda and Syria.
- Critics reject these interpretations.
	- They argue that the UN Charter does not create a unilateral right to humanitarian intervention.
	- Most international legal opinion supports a restrictive interpretation of the prohibition on force.
#### 33.2.3 The Moral Case
- Many scholars argue that there is a moral duty to intervene when populations face genocide or mass killing.
- Main moral arguments:
	- **Common humanity:** All individuals possess basic human rights, creating obligations to protect others from extreme harm.
	- **State responsibility:** Sovereignty is based on a government's responsibility to protect its population. A state that abandons this responsibility weakens its claim to sovereign protection.
	- **Global interconnectedness:** Globalization creates moral relationships between people across borders, making distant suffering relevant to the international community.
	- **Religious and ethical principles:** Major religious and ethical traditions contain principles supporting the duty to assist people in need.
- Moral arguments also create difficult questions:
	- How severe must a humanitarian crisis become before force is justified?
	- Who determines whether intervention is necessary?
	- Should intervention occur before a humanitarian emergency develops?
	- Could humanitarian arguments be used to justify wars that primarily serve national interests?
### 33.3 The Case Against Humanitarian Intervention
- Critics advance seven major objections to humanitarian intervention.
	- These objections appear across realist, liberal, feminist, and postcolonial perspectives.
#### 33.3.1 No Basis for Humanitarian Intervention in International Law
- **Restrictionists** argue that international law does not recognize a general right to humanitarian intervention.
	- Article 2(4) of the UN Charter prohibits the use of force.
	- Article 51 recognizes individual and collective self-defence, while the Security Council can authorize enforcement action.
	- Beyond these exceptions, unilateral military intervention is unlawful.
- States have generally avoided claiming a broad legal right to humanitarian intervention.
	- India justified its 1971 intervention in East Pakistan through self-defence.
	- Vietnam justified its 1978 intervention in Cambodia through self-defence.
	- NATO's 1999 intervention in Kosovo relied partly on arguments about implied Security Council authorization.
#### 33.3.2 States Do Not Intervene for Primarily Humanitarian Reasons
- States usually have mixed motives when intervening abroad.
	- Governments are unlikely to risk soldiers and resources without some national interest.
	- Realists argue that genuine humanitarian intervention is unlikely because it does not necessarily serve national interests.
- Powerful states may intervene only when it is politically or strategically convenient.
	- Decisions may reflect national interests rather than the needs of the population being protected.
#### 33.3.3 States Should Not Risk the Lives of Their Soldiers to Save Strangers
- Realists argue that governments have a responsibility to protect their own citizens first.
	- Political leaders do not necessarily have the moral authority to sacrifice their soldiers to protect foreign populations.
	- Humanitarian intervention may expose soldiers to unnecessary risks when national interests are not directly threatened.
- From this perspective, domestic political struggles should primarily be resolved by the people and leaders of the affected country.
#### 33.3.4 The Problem of Abuse
- Humanitarian intervention can become a justification for pursuing national interests.
	- Without an impartial authority deciding when intervention is permissible, powerful states can use humanitarian language to justify interference in weaker countries.
	- Hitler's claim that Germany needed to protect ethnic Germans in Czechoslovakia illustrates how humanitarian arguments can be manipulated.
- Critics argue that creating a legal right to intervene could make international politics more dangerous.
	- States would have another justification for using military force.
	- The result could be more intervention rather than more genuine humanitarian protection.
#### 33.3.5 Selectivity of Response
- **Selectivity of response** occurs when states apply humanitarian principles inconsistently.
	- Governments intervene when doing so serves their interests but may ignore comparable or more severe crises elsewhere.
	- NATO's intervention in Kosovo is contrasted with the limited international response to humanitarian disasters in Darfur and the Democratic Republic of Congo.
- Selectivity undermines the credibility of humanitarian intervention.
	- It suggests that political interests, rather than consistent moral principles, determine which populations receive protection.
#### 33.3.6 Disagreement About Moral Principles
- International society lacks universal agreement about which moral principles should justify intervention.
	- Different societies and political traditions disagree about human rights, sovereignty, and legitimate government.
- **Pluralist international society theory** emphasizes the importance of respecting these differences.
	- Intervention without international agreement could allow powerful states to impose their own cultural and political values on weaker countries.
	- Sovereignty helps protect international order in a world where states disagree about fundamental principles.
#### 33.3.7 Intervention Does Not Work
- Some critics argue that outsiders cannot successfully impose human rights or democracy through military force.
	- John Stuart Mill argued that political liberty must emerge through a domestic struggle rather than being imposed externally.
	- Foreign intervention may weaken local political movements and prevent societies from developing their own institutions.
- Humanitarian intervention can also produce unintended consequences.
	- Armed groups may provoke governments into committing atrocities in the hope of triggering foreign intervention.
	- Military action can escalate violence rather than protect civilians.
- Critics therefore question whether military force can produce lasting humanitarian improvements.
#### Case Study 33.1 — Myanmar: Barriers to Intervention
- In 2008, Cyclone Nargis struck Myanmar's Irrawaddy Delta.
	- More than 100,000 people died, and millions needed food, shelter, and medical assistance.
	- Myanmar's military government initially restricted international humanitarian aid because it feared foreign influence.
- French Foreign Minister Bernard Kouchner argued that the international community had a moral obligation to deliver aid forcibly.
- The proposal received little international support for several reasons:
	- **Military difficulties:** Myanmar was geographically distant from Western countries and possessed significant military resources.
	- **Risk of escalation:** Military intervention could provoke violence and lead the government to restrict humanitarian access further.
	- **Political consequences:** Intervention could undermine hopes for democratic transition and peace agreements with ethnic armed groups.
	- **International opposition:** China and Russia opposed intervention and regarded the crisis as an internal matter.
	- **Limited political will:** Western militaries were already committed to Afghanistan and Iraq, and domestic support for another intervention was weak.
- The case demonstrates that even extreme humanitarian need does not automatically create international agreement on intervention.
### 33.4 The 1990s: A Golden Era of Humanitarian Activism?
- The period immediately following the Cold War is often described as a **golden era of humanitarian activism**.
	- States became more willing to consider military intervention to protect populations abroad.
	- However, the decade also included failures to prevent genocide and mass atrocities, particularly in Rwanda and Srebrenica.
#### 33.4.1 The Role of Humanitarian Sentiments in Decisions to Intervene
- **Northern Iraq (1991)**
	- Following the Gulf War, Saddam Hussein's government violently suppressed Kurdish populations.
	- The resulting refugee crisis prompted Western military forces to establish protected safe havens.
	- Public pressure in the United States contributed to the decision to intervene.
- **Somalia (1992)**
	- The United States intervened in response to famine and widespread suffering.
	- American public opinion was initially sympathetic to the humanitarian mission.
	- Support declined as US soldiers began suffering casualties.
	- This demonstrated how quickly humanitarian public support can disappear when military costs increase.
- **Rwanda (1994)**
	- Approximately 800,000 people were killed during the genocide in around 100 days.
	- The international community failed to intervene effectively when the genocide began.
	- France eventually launched Operation Turquoise, describing it as a humanitarian intervention.
	- However, France had previously supported Rwanda's Hutu government and was concerned about losing influence in the region.
	- The intervention came late, did not stop the genocide, and provided safe passage for some perpetrators into neighbouring Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of Congo).
	- The case illustrates how humanitarian language can conceal political interests and how a lack of political will can allow mass atrocities to continue.
- **Kosovo (1999)**
	- NATO intervened against Serbian forces, arguing that military action was necessary to prevent a humanitarian catastrophe.
	- NATO had several motives:
		- Preventing a repetition of the violence experienced in Bosnia.
		- Avoiding a large refugee crisis in Europe.
		- Preventing the conflict from spreading.
	- The intervention reflected both humanitarian concern and strategic interests.
	- Mixed motives are not necessarily incompatible with humanitarian objectives, but they become problematic when national interests undermine the protection of civilians.
#### 33.4.2 How Legal and Legitimate Were the Interventions?
- The interventions in northern Iraq, Somalia, Rwanda, and Kosovo were publicly justified in humanitarian terms.
- However, states disagreed about whether humanitarian intervention was legally permissible.
	- China, Russia, and members of the Non-Aligned Movement defended traditional interpretations of sovereignty.
	- Western states increasingly supported the idea that the Security Council could authorize force to protect populations.
- The UN Security Council gradually expanded its interpretation of what constituted a threat to international peace and security.
	- Human suffering, state failure, refugee movements, and ethnic cleansing increasingly became relevant to Security Council decisions.
	- Chapter VII of the UN Charter allowed the Council to authorize enforcement action when it determined that international peace and security were threatened.
- **Kosovo raised a particularly difficult legal question.**
	- NATO intervened without explicit Security Council authorization.
	- Russia attempted to pass a resolution condemning NATO's bombing, but it was defeated.
	- The intervention was later described by an international commission as **“illegal but legitimate.”**
- By the end of the 1990s, many states accepted that the Security Council could authorize intervention in cases of genocide and mass killing.
	- However, unilateral intervention without Security Council authorization remained controversial.
	- The emerging norm was limited because the Council had not yet authorized force against a fully functioning sovereign state.
#### 33.4.3 Were the Interventions Successful?
- Humanitarian intervention should be evaluated through both short-term and long-term outcomes.
	- **Short-term outcomes:** Stopping mass killing, reducing immediate suffering, and delivering humanitarian aid.
	- **Long-term outcomes:** Addressing the causes of violence, facilitating reconciliation, and building stable political institutions.
- **Northern Iraq**
	- Operation Provide Comfort successfully addressed the immediate refugee crisis and saved lives.
	- Long-term stability remained difficult because of hostility towards the Kurdish population.
	- Nevertheless, the Kurds eventually achieved a significant degree of autonomy.
- **Somalia**
	- The initial US intervention may have saved thousands from starvation, although the extent of its success is disputed.
	- The mission deteriorated when UNOSOM II expanded beyond famine relief into disarmament and the enforcement of law and order.
	- The intervention ultimately ended in disaster.
- **Kosovo**
	- NATO helped return Kosovar Albanian refugees to their homes.
	- Initially, it failed to protect the Serbian community from reprisal attacks.
	- Ethnic violence declined over time, and relations between Kosovo and Serbia gradually improved.
	- However, rebuilding required sustained political commitment and financial support.
- **Overall assessment**
	- Military intervention can sometimes stop immediate atrocities.
	- It is much less effective at addressing the underlying political causes of violence.
	- The International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS) argued that intervention should be accompanied by two other responsibilities: **prevention and rebuilding**.
### 33.5 The Responsibility to Protect (R2P)
- The 1999 Kosovo intervention intensified debate about the relationship between sovereignty and humanitarian protection.
	- NATO's actions were described by an international commission as “illegal but legitimate.”
	- The debate raised questions about the responsibilities of states and the international community when governments commit atrocities.
- In 2001, the **International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS)** introduced the concept of the **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)**.
	- It shifted attention away from the rights of intervening states and towards the protection needs of victims.
	- It originally identified three responsibilities:
		- **Responsibility to prevent:** Addressing the underlying causes of mass atrocities before they occur.
		- **Responsibility to react:** Responding to situations where populations face serious harm, potentially including military intervention.
		- **Responsibility to rebuild:** Supporting recovery, reconciliation, and political reconstruction after intervention.
- World leaders unanimously endorsed R2P at the 2005 UN World Summit.
- In 2009, the UN Secretary-General organized R2P around three pillars of equal importance.
#### The Three Pillars of R2P
- **Pillar 1 — State responsibility**
	- Every state has the primary responsibility to protect its population from:
		- Genocide.
		- War crimes.
		- Ethnic cleansing.
		- Crimes against humanity.
	- This responsibility also includes preventing the incitement of these crimes.
- **Pillar 2 — International assistance**
	- The international community should assist and encourage states to fulfil their protective responsibilities.
	- Assistance can include:
		- Addressing the underlying causes of mass atrocities.
		- Developing state capacity to prevent atrocities.
		- Supporting governments before crises escalate.
- **Pillar 3 — International response**
	- The international community must respond when national authorities are manifestly failing to protect their populations.
	- Responses should initially prioritize diplomatic, humanitarian, and other peaceful measures.
	- If peaceful measures are inadequate, the Security Council may authorize more forceful action under Chapter VII of the UN Charter.
- The three pillars are not sequential stages; they are equally important and can operate simultaneously.
#### Five Important Features of R2P
- **Narrow but universal scope**
	- R2P applies to every state, at all times.
	- It is limited to four crimes: genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity.
- **Protection of all populations**
	- States are responsible for protecting everyone under their care, not only their citizens.
- **Grounded in existing international law**
	- R2P is based on established international legal principles and must be implemented consistently with the UN Charter.
- **Prevention is central**
	- R2P includes preventing the four crimes and their incitement, not merely responding after they occur.
- **Security Council authorization**
	- The use of force under R2P requires authorization from the UN Security Council.
#### 33.5.1 R2P in Action
- **Darfur**
	- R2P initially struggled to move from principle to practice.
	- The Security Council referred to R2P in Resolution 1706 (2006), concerning Darfur.
	- Many observers viewed Darfur as a major test of R2P.
	- The international community's failure to respond effectively led many to conclude that R2P had failed this test.
- **Libya (2011)**
	- The Security Council adopted Resolution 1970 in response to violence and crimes against humanity by the Gaddafi government.
	- The resolution demanded an end to violence, established a political process, imposed targeted sanctions, and referred the situation to the International Criminal Court.
	- When the government failed to comply, Resolution 1973 authorized military force to protect civilians and enforce a no-fly zone and arms embargo.
	- This was the first time the Security Council authorized force against a functioning sovereign state for humanitarian protection purposes.
	- The intervention became controversial because critics argued that NATO exceeded its mandate and contributed to regime change.
	- Russia, India, and China argued that humanitarian protection should not be used as a justification for overthrowing governments.
- **Côte d'Ivoire (2011)**
	- Following post-election violence, the Security Council adopted Resolution 1975.
	- The Council recognized Alassane Ouattara as president and authorized force to protect civilians.
	- The case demonstrated that R2P could be used to justify international action beyond Libya.
- **Other applications**
	- The Security Council later referred to R2P in relation to the Democratic Republic of Congo, Mali, South Sudan, the Central African Republic, and Syria.
	- Resolution 2165 (2014) authorized humanitarian aid deliveries into Syria without the Syrian government's consent.
	- This was the first time the Security Council had authorized such action.
- **Responsibility while protecting**
	- Brazil introduced the concept of **responsibility while protecting** in response to concerns about how states used military force under Security Council mandates.
	- Its four main elements were:
		- Prioritize prevention and peaceful measures before force is used.
		- Establish mechanisms to hold intervening states accountable to the Security Council.
		- Develop criteria to guide decisions about the use of force.
		- Carefully assess the likely consequences of military action.
	- Supporters viewed this as a way to prevent states from exceeding their mandates.
	- Critics worried that additional restrictions could delay necessary action to stop mass atrocities.
- **Challenges after 2012**
	- Security Council agreement became more difficult as Russia and China increasingly challenged humanitarian intervention.
	- Libya descended into renewed civil war in 2014 after the initial intervention was not followed by sufficient peacebuilding and stabilization efforts.
	- The deterioration of Libya strengthened doubts about the long-term effectiveness of humanitarian intervention.
	- China increasingly promoted an alternative vision of international order focused on strong states, social stability, and development.
		- This differs from the UN's human-centred approach, which emphasizes human rights, development, and peace and security.
		- China is more reluctant to accept international involvement in the protection of populations from their own governments.
	- The future of R2P therefore depends partly on whether major powers can agree on the responsibilities of the international community.
- **Intervention against the so-called Islamic State**
	- In 2014, the rise of the so-called Islamic State (ISIS) created renewed questions about humanitarian intervention.
	- US and allied forces supported Iraqi government and Kurdish forces fighting ISIS.
	- Air power helped rescue Yazidis trapped on Mount Sinjar and prevent further attacks against them.
	- The UN identified credible grounds for believing that ISIS had committed genocide against Yazidis.
	- The United States later extended air operations into Syria, citing collective self-defence rather than humanitarian intervention.
	- Western states also attacked Syrian government targets in response to the Assad regime's use of chemical weapons.
	- In April 2018, the UK, US, and France launched strikes against Syrian chemical weapons facilities.
	- The UK defended the action through a claimed exceptional right of humanitarian intervention, while Russia condemned it as a violation of international law.
#### Case Study 33.2 — The Role of Middle Eastern Governments in Operation Unified Protector (Libya, 2011)
- Humanitarian intervention is not exclusively a Western activity.
	- During the Cold War, India intervened in East Pakistan, Vietnam in Cambodia, and Tanzania in Uganda to stop mass killing.
	- After the Cold War, the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) intervened in Liberia in 1990.
- Middle Eastern organizations played an important role in supporting the 2011 Libya intervention.
	- The **League of Arab States (LAS)** suspended Libya's participation following violence against civilians.
	- The **Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)** called on the Security Council to take measures to protect civilians, including a no-fly zone.
	- The **Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC)** supported a no-fly zone while opposing foreign ground operations.
	- On 12 March 2011, the LAS formally called on the Security Council to establish a no-fly zone and safe areas.
- Middle Eastern states also contributed to the operation.
	- Qatar supplied aircraft and special forces.
	- The United Arab Emirates contributed 12 aircraft.
	- Jordan provided six aircraft, primarily for non-combat support and humanitarian relief.
- The case demonstrates that regional organizations and non-Western states can play significant roles in facilitating humanitarian intervention.
#### Opposing Opinions 33.1 — The West Should Have Intervened in Syria to Protect People from the So-Called Islamic State
- **For**
	- **Moral argument:** ISIS committed widespread human rights violations, including genocide against the Yazidis, mass executions, rape, and sexual enslavement.
	- **Prudential argument:** Military action was arguably the only realistic way to protect populations from ISIS. Air power helped local forces reverse ISIS advances in Iraq and defend Kobane.
	- **Legal argument:** UN Security Council Resolution 2249 (2015) called on states to use all necessary measures against ISIS. Supporters interpreted this as implied authorization for military force.
	- **Self-interest argument:** ISIS threatened international security through terrorism and attacks planned or inspired abroad.
- **Against**
	- **Legal argument:** Resolution 2249 did not explicitly authorize military force under Chapter VII. Syria also had not consented to US and allied operations on its territory.
	- **Prudential argument:** Air strikes alone could not defeat ISIS, and previous Western interventions in the Middle East had often produced destabilizing consequences.
	- **Counterproductive effects:** Western military action could strengthen ISIS propaganda, attract foreign fighters, and increase terrorist threats.
	- **Civilian casualties:** Air strikes risked killing civilians and worsening the humanitarian crisis they were intended to address.
### 33.6 Conclusion
- Globalization has strengthened ideas of cosmopolitan moral responsibility and interconnectedness.
	- People increasingly recognize the suffering of populations beyond their own national borders.
	- However, this has not created international agreement on the legitimacy of forcible humanitarian intervention.
- Humanitarian concern is often selective.
	- States may respond to some humanitarian emergencies while ignoring others.
	- Interventions may begin with humanitarian objectives but develop into activities that undermine those objectives.
- **R2P** has changed the language and framework of the debate.
	- It emphasizes the responsibility of states to protect their populations.
	- It also recognizes an international responsibility to assist states and respond when they fail.
	- Its adoption at the 2005 UN World Summit was an important achievement.
- However, R2P remains constrained by national interests and competition among states.
	- Major powers may disagree about when intervention is justified.
	- The Security Council can be unable to act because of geopolitical divisions.
	- Military intervention may stop immediate atrocities without resolving the political conditions that caused them.
- The central challenge remains balancing sovereignty, international order, and the protection of individuals from mass atrocities.
