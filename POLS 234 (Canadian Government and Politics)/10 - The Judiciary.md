## 10.1 The Role of the Judiciary
- Four main tasks (expanded from original modest role):
	1. **Adjudicate private law disputes** (contracts, property, torts)
	2. **Adjudicate public law cases** (criminal law, administrative law)
	3. **Judicial review of Constitution** (most politically significant)
	4. Oversee inquiries and commissions (rare)

### 1. Adjudicating Private Disputes
- Courts settle disputes between private parties fairly
- Private mechanisms exist: negotiation, mediation, arbitration
- But all depend on courts as final recourse
- **Private law jurisdiction:**
	- Provinces have "property and civil rights" (Section 92(13))
	- Quebec uses civil code (comprehensive statute)
	- Other provinces use common law (judge-made law based on precedent)

### 2. Adjudicating Public Law
- **Public law:** Government vs. individual or government regulation
- **Two main areas:**
	1. **Criminal law:** Federal jurisdiction (Section 91(27))
		- Criminal Code sets crimes and punishment ranges
		- Judges determine specific sentences (substantial power)
	2. **Administrative law:** Regulatory legislation without criminal penalties
		- Administered by tribunals or courts
		- Wide range: traffic regulations to pensions

### 3. Judicial Review of Constitution
- Courts interpret Constitution
- Most politically significant role (expanded far beyond founders' expectations)
- Judges decide important questions:
	- Federal/provincial power limits
	- Abortion access
	- Medical assistance in dying (MAID)
	- Other Charter issues
- **Triggered by cases:** Judicial review not initiated by courts, but by cases requiring constitutional ruling

## 10.2 Fundamental Principles of Canadian Judiciary

### 1. Rule of Law
- Everyone, including government officials, subject to law
- Government acts must follow legal procedures
- No arbitrary detention/punishment
- Established in Section 7 of Charter: "principles of fundamental justice"

### 2. Judicial Independence
- Judges must be free from political pressure to decide cases
- **Three conditions for independence (Valente v. The Queen, 1985):**
	1. **Security of tenure:** Can't be fired for unpopular rulings
		- Section 99 of CA 1867: Superior court judges until age 75 "during good behaviour"
		- Can only be removed for incapacity or misconduct
	2. **Fixed salaries:** Salaries set by law, not manipulated by executive
		- Section 100 of CA 1867: Remuneration fixed by law
	3. **Control over court administration:** Freedom from pressure about case scheduling, decisions
		- Not constitutionally entrenched but largely respected in practice

### 3. Equality Before Law
- Everyone treated equally by courts
- Section 15(1) of Charter: Explicit protection
- **Challenges:**
	- Judge bias/mistakes
	- Laws themselves may discriminate
	- Legal costs: Poor can't afford justice
	- Quality of legal representation varies by affordability
	- Legal aid spotty in non-criminal cases

## 10.3 Canada's Court System
- **Principle 1: Hierarchy**
	- Trial courts → Appeal courts → Supreme Court
	- Based on case importance and need for appeals

- **Principle 2: Federalism**
	- Dual system like U.S. (unlike unitary systems)
	- Federal courts for federal law
	- Provincial courts for provincial law
	- Supreme Court of Canada hears appeals from both

### Court Structure
1. **Inferior courts:** Handle minor cases (traffic, small claims)
2. **Superior trial courts:** Murder, assault, serious criminal cases
3. **Provincial appeals courts:** Hear appeals from trial courts
4. **Federal court:** Federal law matters
5. **Supreme Court of Canada:** Final court of appeal (9 justices)

## 10.4 Supreme Court of Canada
- **Final court of appeal:** Highest authority
- **Jurisdiction:** 
	- Hears appeals from provincial courts of appeal
	- Hears federal court appeals
	- Can refuse cases (leave to appeal required)
- **Justices:** 9 total
	- Chief Justice + 8 associate justices
	- Selected from across country
- **Power:** Interprets Constitution (most significant political role)

## 10.5 Politics of Judicial Appointments
- **Who appoints?** Prime Minister recommends to Governor General
- **Selection process:** Currently more transparent
	- Advisory committees
	- Consultations
	- Public criteria
- **Impact:** Political because judges interpret Constitution, make policy-relevant decisions
- **Representation:** Increasingly considering gender, ethnic diversity, Indigenous representation

## 10.6 Judicial Power and the Charter
- Charter expanded judicial power dramatically
- Courts now regularly overturn legislation
- Judges have become major policy makers
- Debate: Is this appropriate for unelected officials?
- **Reality:** Judges still constrained by
	- Need to respect public opinion
	- Necessity of voluntary compliance with judgments
	- Can't stray too far from prevailing values
