### 19.2 Institutions
- Three levels of international institutions (Box 19.1)
    1. **Constitutional institutions**: primary rules and norms of international society, without which society among sovereign states couldn't exist
        - Ex. **sovereignty** (centralized, hierarchical power inside the state, no higher authority outside), plus self-determination and non-intervention
    2. **Fundamental institutions**: basic norms and practices states use to coexist and cooperate under anarchy
        - Ex. international law, multilateralism, bilateralism, diplomacy, great power management (Bull 1977)
        - Since the mid-19th century, law and multilateralism have been the basic framework for cooperation and order
    3. **Issue-specific institutions** (_regimes_): rules, norms, and procedures defining legitimate actors and action in a domain
        - Ex. NPT (1968), Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, UNFCCC, Ottawa landmines convention, ICCPR
        - Concrete enactments of fundamental practices (ex. NPT = law plus multilateralism in arms control)
- Chapter focuses on the middle stratum
#### Key Points

- States have strong incentives to escape the insecurity of anarchy
- Coordination and collaboration problems exist, but cooperation is hard under anarchy
- So states build institutions at the three levels
- International law is one of the most important fundamental institutions
### 19.3 The modern institution of international law
#### 19.3.1 Historical roots
- Contemporary system is a _historical artefact_
    - Grotius (1583-1645) and Vattel (1714-67) are the "fathers"; Augsburg (1555), Westphalia (1648), Utrecht (1713) are landmarks
    - But the system got many of its distinctive features as late as the 19th century
- **Age of Absolutism**
    - Monarchs ruled by _divine right_ (Bodin)
    - Law = command of a legitimate superior (God, natural law)
    - International law derived from natural law: divinely ordained principles accessible through right reason
    - Monarchs obeyed out of **fealty to God**, not contract (Grotius)
- Liberalism and nationalism challenged absolutism in the late 18th and early 19th centuries
    - Constitutionalism and popular sovereignty weakened monarchs and widened the franchise
    - New conception: **law as reciprocal accord**, legitimate if authored by those subject to it and applied equally
    - Filtered into interstate relations as **contractual (positive) international law**
        - Product of negotiation among states; binding via reciprocal agreement ("mutual will of the nations concerned," von Martens)
##### Box 19.2: Key constitutive legal treaties
- **Westphalia (1648)**: ended the Thirty Years' War; gave monarchs rights to standing armies, fortifications, taxes
- **Utrecht (1713)**: consolidated _territorial_ sovereignty; fixed boundaries, not family ties, define sovereign reach
- **Paris (1814)**: ended the Napoleonic Wars; led to Vienna and the **Concert of Europe**
    - Limited great power war, but also upheld monarchy and fought liberal and nationalist movements
- **Versailles (1919)**: ended WWI; League of Nations, reparations on Germany, **Mandates** system (tutelage over colonial peoples)
- **UN Charter (1945)**: created the UN; limits force to self-defence and collective peace enforcement approved by the UNSC
- **Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (1960)**: GA Res. 1514, non-binding, delegitimized colonialism and fed decolonization via self-determination
- Result: four distinctive characteristics (Box 19.3)
    1. Multilateral legislation
    2. Consent-based legal obligation
    3. A peculiar language of justification
    4. A strong discourse of institutional autonomy
##### Case Study 19.1: Is international law an expression of Western dominance?
- **Is it a Western, imperial institution?**
    - Yes
        - Roots in 16th/17th century European thought
        - Distinguished law among Christian peoples (equality) from law toward Muslim, American, and later Asian peoples (Christian superiority)
        - 19th century **standard of civilization** (Gong 1984): sovereignty only if you met Western political and diplomatic standards
            - Divided peoples into "civilized," "barbarian," "savage" to justify degrees of Western rule
        - Persistent bias: Anglo-European dominance of peak legal institutions and human rights law
        - Humanitarian intervention seen as Western powers using UNSC position and human rights norms to intervene in developing states
    - No (more complex)
        - Customary norms uphold legal equality of states, self-determination, and non-intervention
        - Non-Western states are the most vigorous defenders of these
        - Non-Western peoples were central to building the human rights regime (Case Study 19.2)

##### Case Study 19.2: Non-Western agency in international human rights law
- Standard account: the West drove civil and political rights (UDHR 1948, ICCPR 1966); non-Western actors pushed cultural, economic, and social rights (ICESCR 1966)
    - Also pushed by non-Western critics who call rights cultural/legal imperialism
- Contradicted by the record
    - UDHR drafted by a committee meant to represent the world's cultural traditions (Glendon 2002); small Third World states helped it pass the UN
    - Postcolonial states insisted on the _primacy_ of civil and political rights in the ICCPR
    - They defeated Western attempts to insert "federal" and "colonial" state exceptions
    - First Optional Protocol (individual petition to the UN) was a non-Western initiative (Reus-Smit 2013)

#### 19.3.2 Multilateral legislation
- **Legislation** = formulating and enacting binding rules; it's formal _and_ informal
    - Informal: arguments, social learning, repeated practice
        - Ex. **R2P** debate (whether a new norm is evolving to qualify sovereignty; many deny it)
    - Informal processes are how **customary norms** evolve
        - Binding on all states regardless of consent (territorial jurisdiction, freedom of the seas, diplomatic immunity)
- Formal: **multilateralism** (cooperation among three or more states based on reciprocally binding rules)
    - Before the Napoleonic Wars it was marginal; often aggregated bilateral deals (ex. Westphalia, Utrecht) (Ruggie 1993)
    - 19th century liberalism made it the preferred mode: law is legitimate only if authored by its subjects and applied equally
#### 19.3.3 Consent and legal obligation
- Grotius: states obey the law of nations "even though they have made no promise"; fealty to God was the root, consent secondary
- Today **consent** is the _primary_ source of obligation (Henkin 1995)
    - Leaders and critics both invoke it
- Two complications
    1. States are bound by customary law without formal consent
        - Test: general observance plus **opinio juris** (states observe it _because_ it's law)
        - This is only _tacit_ consent, which isn't actual consent
    2. Philosophically circular (Hart)
        - Consent obligates only if a prior rule says promises are binding, so consent can't be the source of that rule's force
#### 19.3.4 Language and practice of justification
- Law is more than neutral rules applied to clear cases; it structures political debates about right and wrong, legitimacy, authority, membership
- Legal argument is
    - **Rhetorical**: interpretation decides which rules apply, their meaning, and the case at hand
        - Kratochwil: acceptability, not "truth"; logic plays a minor role
    - **Analogical**: establishing similarities across different cases (Kratochwil)
        1. Interpreting a rule by analogy to another rule
        2. Claiming one class of action is rule-governed because another was
        3. Establishing a rule's status (ex. customary) from another rule's levels of assent and dissent
#### 19.3.5 The discourse of institutional autonomy
- Actors act as if at some point action moves from the _political_ realm to the _legal_ realm
    - Political: self-interest and barely veiled coercion are legitimate (if distasteful)
    - Legal: legal reasoning is the legitimate form
    - Ex. US on Iraq in 2003: constrained by legal justifications in the UNSC, more self-interested and coercive outside it
- Two points
    1. Separating politics and law is _modern_; absolutist monarchs fused them in the sovereign (cf. **separation of powers**)
    2. It's politically functional: brings discipline, structure, and predictability to IR
##### Key Points
- Modern international law is a historical artefact of the post-French Revolution transformation of European governance
- Absolutism: law as command (of God); modern: law contracted among subjects, i.e. mutual will of nations
- Distinctive features reflect liberal values: multilateral legislation, consent, justification, institutional autonomy
### 19.4 From international to supranational law?
- Law that served order (protecting states' negative liberties) was circumscribed in four ways
    1. States were the primary **subjects** (Montevideo Convention 1933: "state as a person of international law")
    2. States were the primary **agents** (only ones to formulate, enact, enforce)
    3. Regulated _inter-state_ relations only (self-determination and non-intervention kept domestic affairs out)
    4. Scope limited to **order, not justice**
- Quest for **global governance** is changing this
    1. Individuals and groups are becoming subjects
        - Human rights law; minority groups and indigenous peoples; individual criminal responsibility (ICTY, ICTR, ICC)
    2. Non-state actors are becoming agents (they can't formally enact law or create custom)
        - Shape the normative environment, supply info, draft treaties
        - Ex. ICRC drafted the 1864 Geneva Convention; multinationals shaped investor protections in the 2018 CPTPP
    3. Law is increasingly **global**, not just international
        - Breaches the domestic boundary: trade law, environmental law, human rights law
        - Helped by national courts that draw on international law
    4. Beyond order, toward **global justice**
        - Humanitarian law; UNSC decisions treating gross rights violations as threats to peace (ex. Libya) under Chapter 7
        - Links international order with minimum standards of global justice

#### Key Points

- Law for order was circumscribed (states as subjects and agents, inter-state focus, order only)
- Global governance raises the question of a shift to _supranational_ law
- Individuals and collectivities gain rights and responsibilities
- Non-governmental actors matter in codifying norms
- Domestic regimes are affected, and scope extends to justice _(text cuts off mid-sentence here)_

### 19.5 The laws of war

- Law on use of force is the core of the international legal system
- Two types
    - **Jus ad bellum**: when force is permitted
    - **Jus in bello**: conduct in war
    - Always entwined; content has changed, sometimes reversing cardinal norms
- **Jus ad bellum has changed most**
    - Early: _just cause_ (response to an unwarranted attack, reparations)
        - Complicated by norms like conquest securing sovereignty
    - 19th century: war justified by a state's vital national interests, which it alone defined
        - War as a fundamental sovereign right; consequences clear in WWI and WWII
    - After 1945: **UN Charter** allows only
        1. Self-defence (Art. 51), an unqualified sovereign right
        2. UNSC-sanctioned peace enforcement (Art. 42)
- **Jus in bello**: gradual expansion of constraints, three areas
    1. **Weaponry**
        - Hague Conferences (1899, 1907): expanding bullets, bombs from balloons, gas projectiles
        - Later: landmines, chemical weapons; campaigns now on lethal autonomous weapons ("killer robots")
    2. **Treatment of combatants**: four Geneva Conventions (1864, 1906, 1929, 1949) plus additional protocols (1977 x2, 2005)
    3. **Non-combatants**: Geneva Conventions again; deliberate targeting long prohibited
        - Disturbing trend: more non-combatants killed than combatants
        - Rape in war being codified as an international crime (Case Study 13.2)
- Clearest example of the **international to supranational** shift: ICTY, ICTR, then the **ICC**
    - Prosecutes genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression
- Challenges since 2001
    - War on terror: Afghanistan seen as legitimate self-defence (Taliban harboured Al Qaeda)
    - Iraq: criticized as illegal
        - Failed to establish _preventive_ self-defence; couldn't convince the UNSC
    - Jus in bello abuses: Guantanamo (no Geneva protections or normal judicial process), **extraordinary rendition**
    - Fears that law is crumbling under a unilateralist superpower (Steiner, Alston, Goodman 2008)
    - Obama: order to close Guantanamo (later retreated), banned rendition for torture, Red Cross access, re-engaged multilateral processes
        - Still, perception of crisis intensified (Clark et al. 2018): drones, extrajudicial killing
    - Trump: open disdain for legal limits, ended the 1987 INF Treaty
    - Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine: violated ad bellum, and targeting civilians violated in bello
    - Islamic State: deliberate, choreographed provocation of laws of war

#### Key Points

- Limiting force is a key challenge; laws of war evolved to meet it
- Ad bellum (when) vs in bello (how)
- Biggest shift: 19th century sovereign right to wage war vs post-1945 self-defence or UN-mandated enforcement
- In bello covers weaponry, combatants, non-combatants
- Since 2001 both have been challenged by US conduct in the war on terror, insurgents, and Russia undermining neighbours' territorial integrity

### 19.6 Theoretical approaches to international law

#### 19.6.1 Realism

- Sceptical; hostile to "peace through law"
- Kennan: it transposes Anglo-Saxon individual law onto governments
- No central authority to legislate, adjudicate, or enforce, so doubts it's really law
    - Morgenthau: a "primitive law," like preliterate societies
- Obligation is weak: few sanctions, rudimentary enforcement

#### 19.6.2 Neoliberal institutionalism

- Avoided talking about law at first ("regimes" were close; inspired by economics, less provocative in Cold War realist field)
- After the Cold War, pushed for IR-law dialogue, but with rationalist commitments
    - States as rational egoists
    - Law as an _intervening variable_ and a _regulatory_, not constitutive, institution (Goldstein et al. 2000)

#### 19.6.3 Constructivism

- Normative and ideational structures matter as much as material ones; identities shape interests; structures sustained by routinized practices
- Common ground with legal theorists
    - Politics includes identity and purpose, not just strategy
    - Rules and norms are _constitutive_, not just constraining
    - Discourse, communication, socialization (Reus-Smit 2004; Brunnée and Toope 2010)

#### 19.6.4 Critical legal studies ("new stream", 1980s)

- Challenges the inherent liberalism of international law
    - Liberalism pushes theory between **apology** (rationalizing the sovereign order) and **utopia** (naive faith that law civilizes states) (Koskenniemi 1989)
- Four propositions (Purvis 1991)
    1. Liberalism's logic is **incoherent**: denies objective values beyond state particularism, yet imagines neutral rules resolving conflicts
    2. Legal thought works in a **confined intellectual structure**: liberal ideology (naturalizes sovereignty) plus constraining public international legal argument
        - A "self-referential search for origins, authority, and coherence"
    3. Rules aren't **determinate**: any doctrine can justify multiple, competing outcomes (against positivism's "finding the law")
    4. Authority is **self-validating**: legitimacy comes only through internal rituals

#### 19.6.5 The practice turn

- Knowledgeable social practices (Pouliot 2010; Adler and Pouliot)
- Brunnée and Toope: where does legal obligation come from?
    - Realists: coercion; liberal positivists: consent; others: legitimacy or fairness
    - Their answer: engagement in legal practices
        - Obligation is an _internalized commitment_, a feeling about the legal order's legitimacy
        - Socially constructed through participation
    - Practices must meet **criteria of legality**: general, officially promulgated, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, realistic, constant, congruent

### 19.7 Conclusion

- Paradox: scholars downplay law's value, yet states invest heavily in elaborate legal regimes
- International law is functional (needed in a complex system) _and_ grounded in ideas of legitimate rule from political liberalism
- Trend toward supranational or transnational law
- Each theory offers a different view on the paradox

#### Key Points

- Realists: law matters only when it serves powerful states' interests
- Neoliberals: explain how self-interested states build dense legal regimes
- Constructivists: law is part of normative structures that constitute identities, interests, and strategies
- Critical legal studies: law's inherent liberalism curtails its radical potential
- Practice theorists: obligation comes from participating in legal practice, not coercion, consent, or legitimacy

#### Opposing Opinions 19.1: International law has no real effect on IR

- **Does international law matter?**
    - For (no real effect)
        - Not "real" law: no central authority or enforcement backed by force
        - Exists because it serves the powerful (codifies interests, legitimacy gets weaker states to comply)
        - Complexity and fragmentation let states pick from a menu to justify almost anything
        - Can't keep pace with change (ex. laws of war assume sovereign state combatants, distinguishable civilians, particular tech)
    - Against (it matters)
        - Creates states as primary actors; sovereignty's rights are embedded in international norms and law
        - Legitimacy is a key resource, and law is a main source
        - Compliance is high, even by domestic standards
            - Some laws limit power for the wider good (ex. UN Charter limits on force)
        - Law-breakers almost always _reference_ the law (deny breaking it, say it doesn't apply, or claim they defend it)
