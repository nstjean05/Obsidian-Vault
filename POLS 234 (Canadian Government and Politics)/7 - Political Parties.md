# Chapter 7: Political Parties

## 7.1 Political Parties in the Canadian Regime
- **Political parties:** Publicly organized groups motivated by common political ideas, goal is to have members win public office to put ideas into practice
- Canadian political life dominated by political parties
	- Every Parliament divided along party lines
	- Every cabinet (except WWI Union Government) restricted to members of dominant party
- **Not mandated by Constitution:**
	- CA 1867 and CA 1982 don't mention political parties
	- Not a constitutional convention (no hard rule requiring MPs to be party members)
	- We have parties because they're useful and no law prohibits them

### Why Parties Exist
- **Parliamentary government organized into government and opposition** → promotes existence of parties
- **Useful to leaders:** Need cohesive group of legislators to support/oppose programs
- **Useful to voters:** Parties gather policy positions into coherent package based on general vision
- **Evidence of necessity:** Canadians normally don't elect "independent" candidates
	- Lone MP exercises little political power
	- Can speak without toeing party line, but unlikely to accomplish much

### Parties vs. Factions
- **Frank Underhill:** "Acid test of freedom = whether power can pass peaceably from one party to another. Her Majesty's Opposition just as necessary as Her Majesty's Government."
- **Parties accept legitimacy of other parties:**
	- Agree different parties have right to exist
	- Winner has legitimate right to govern until next election
- **Factions don't accept legitimacy of other group** (form sides in civil wars)
- **Party politics is non-revolutionary:** Every party agrees on fundamental principles of regime

### Exceptions to Party Politics
- **Northwest Territories and Nunavut:** "Consensus government" without parties
	- 19 independent MLAs (NWT)
	- After election: Elect Speaker, premier, 6 government members
	- "Regular members" not in cabinet hold government to account
	- Works because of political culture and small assembly size
		- Unclear if would work in large assembly with diverse populations
- **Indigenous governments:** Operate on non-partisan, consensus basis
- **Municipal level:** Generally non-partisan (changing in some cities)

## 7.2 The Five Functions of Political Parties
1. **Recruitment:** Recruit people into party as members and candidates
	- Crucial for combating apathy and lack of civic participation
	- Tocqueville's "individualism" = people withdraw into private life, ignore politics
	- Democracy endangered when people lose taste for politics
	- Parties supply political leaders via national leadership conventions
	- Candidates via constituency nominations
	- Campaign workers for election aspects
2. **Fundraising:** Raise money for organizations and election campaigns
	- Still largely private activity, regulated by election finance laws
	- Alternative: Costs borne by taxpayer
3. **Interest Aggregation:** Identify, represent, balance diverse interests of Canadians
	- Crucial to healthy liberal democracy functioning
	- Policy advantageous for one group often comes at expense of others
		- Ex. Fiscal transfers to have-not provinces paid by prosperous provinces
		- Environmental policy conflicts with energy policy (fossil fuels)
	- **Without parties:** MPs free to vote for constituents' best interests
		- Most powerful regions (Ontario, Quebec) would have greater influence
	- **With parties:** Major national parties seek votes in all groups
		- Forced to hammer out compromise positions offering relatively fair deal to all
4. **Policy Development:** Formulate and influence public policy
	- Design policies at policy conventions (gatherings of rank-and-file party members)
	- Debate policy resolutions
	- Elected members and party leaders guide policy development
	- Once party in power: Policies become law
5. **Political Education:** Educate public about policies and issues
	- Campaigns, debates, policy announcements all educate voters
	- Help citizens make informed decisions

## 7.3 Parties and Ideology
- **Ideology:** Set of beliefs about nature of society, role of government, freedom, equality
- **High partisanship:** Strong commitment to party's ideology/principles
	- More common in ideological parties
- **Low partisanship:** Flexible, pragmatic approach
	- More common in brokerage parties
- **Left-Right spectrum in Canada:**
	- **Left (NDP):** Activist government, social programs, redistribution of wealth, environmental protection
	- **Center (Liberals):** Balance between government intervention and free market
	- **Right (Conservatives):** Limited government, free markets, individual responsibility, traditional values

## 7.4 Canada's Major Parties
### 1. Conservative Party
- **Origins in Confederation:** John A. Macdonald's vision
	- Strong central government
	- National economic development (transcontinental railway)
	- Close ties to Britain
	- Support from business, wealthy, Anglican/Protestant establishment
- **Early 20th century challenges:**
	- Western alienation → Progressive movement
	- 1940s: Coalition with Progressives → Progressive Conservative Party
		- Inclined toward free trade, smaller government, more hospitable to U.S.
- **Mulroney era (1980s):** Perceived as beholden to central Canada
	- Western right-leaning voters broke off → **Reform Party** (Preston Manning)
		- Market-oriented, populist
		- Decried western Canada's lack of influence
		- Proposed Senate reform to give west more voice
- **2000:** Reform became **Canadian Alliance**
	- Split right-of-centre vote with PCs
	- Allowed Liberals to dominate 1990s
- **2003:** Stephen Harper unified conservatives → **Conservative Party of Canada**
	- Free-market economic policy
	- Moral traditionalism
	- Strong national defence
	- Law and order criminal justice
	- Resuscitation of symbolic connections to UK
	- High-water mark: Majority government 2011
	- Decisive defeat 2015 by Trudeau Liberals
- **Andrew Scheer:** Limited Liberals to minority 2019
	- Resigned after criticism that tepid demeanor/moral traditionalism prevented Conservative majority

### 2. Liberal Party
- **Origins:** Wilfred Laurier (PM 1896)
	- Quebec-born, French-speaking Catholic
	- Understood Canada as arrangement between French and English
	- Supported provincial rights
	- Urged English provinces to support francophone Catholic education funding
	- Supported separation of church and state
	- Free trader, open attitude to U.S. and democratic customs
- **Increasing distance from UK:**
	- Mackenzie King discontinued appeals to British Judicial Committee
	- Pearson introduced new flag (1965)
	- Trudeau achieved Constitution patriation (1982)
	- New immigration policies from all over world (changed British face of country)
	- Trudeau's 1971 multiculturalism policy moved away from British connection
	- Charter of Rights borrowed from American experience
- **"Natural governing party":**
	- In office more than any other party since 1867
	- Success factors:
		1. **Exactly kind of party SMP encourages:** Flexible, pragmatic, excels at interest aggregation
		2. **Pragmatic policies occupy broad middle** of Canadian electoral preference
		3. **Policies shift with time/circumstance:**
			- Major social policies (medicare, unemployment insurance, social assistance transfers)
			- Also cut transfers to combat deficits
			- Supported emission reduction + oil pipelines
			- Supported protectionism (1970s), free trade (1990s+)
			- Care not to alienate business while shielding Canadians from market's harshest effects
- **Ideological identity rooted in Charter:**
	- Guarantee of individual rights
	- Right not to be discriminated against
	- Aligns with "progressive" forces (women, LGBTQ, visible minorities, disadvantaged groups)
	- Policies: Gun control, cannabis legalization, medical assistance in dying, therapeutic approach to drug addiction, choice in abortion
	- Trudeau's leadership: Special attention to women and Indigenous peoples
- **Foreign affairs:** Favour diplomacy over force, prize UN peacekeeping
	- Lester Pearson: Nobel Peace Prize for peacekeeping leadership (Suez Crisis 1957)

### 3. New Democratic Party (NDP)
- **Origins:** Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF)
	- Regional protest politics
	- Reaction to prairie economy collapse (1930s)
	- Capitalist interests (central Canada) held responsible
	- Reaction against free-market capitalism and liberalism
	- Associated with concern Canada run by central Canada for central Canada
	- Formed to represent desperate farmers and working class
- **1961:** NDP formed by combining CCF + Canadian Labour Congress (CLC)
	- CLC strength in Ontario
	- Until 2011: Modest success nationally
	- Did well provincially (formed governments in 6 provinces)
	- Traditionally drew strength from Ontario and western Canada
- **2011 breakthrough:**
	- Spectacular gains in Quebec and Maritimes
	- Became Official Opposition for first time
	- 2015: Returned to historically typical support levels
- **Influence despite never forming federal government:**
	- Many progressive ideas adopted by other parties
	- Provincial NDP governments demonstrated social democratic policies viable

### 4. Bloc Québécois (BQ)
- **Formation (1990):** Group of Conservative and Liberal MPs quit parties in protest
	- Focused on promoting Quebec sovereignty
	- Advancing Quebec interests from inside House of Commons
	- Runs candidates only in Quebec
	- Cannot/has no interest in forming national government
	- Primary interests:
		1. Extract concessions for Quebec from minority governments
		2. Give voice to complaints about Ottawa's treatment of Quebec
- **1993 election:** 13.5% of vote nationally, but 54 seats (geographically concentrated)
	- Good enough for second place, became Official Opposition (ironic)
	- Averaged 47 seats over next 5 elections (third largest party)
- **2011 decline:** Support for Quebec sovereignty in long-term decline
	- Hard feelings from Meech Lake, Charlottetown, Clarity Act fading
	- Elected only 4 MPs, significant internal disarray
- **2019 comeback (32 seats):**
	- New leader: Yves-François Blanchet (telegenic, articulate)
	- Gap between Quebec and rest of Canada on accommodating religious minorities
		- Quebec's Bill 21: Prohibits public sector employees from wearing religious garb/symbols
		- Overwhelming majority of Quebec voters support
		- Criticized in rest of Canada
	- Dropped sovereignty emphasis
	- Campaigned on leaving it to Quebecers to determine approach to secularism/religious accommodation
	- Framed as need to send "people like us" to defend Quebec's distinct identity
	- Dominated ridings outside Montreal
	- Effectively displaced NDP in Quebec
- **Question:** Whether recent success can be sustained

### 5. Green Party
- **Formation (1983):** Canadian version of Green parties in Germany, New Zealand
- **Ideology rooted in Charter of the Global Greens:**
	- Six principles: Ecological wisdom, social justice, participatory democracy, non-violence, sustainability, respect for diversity
- **Appeals to younger voters**

### Three Challenges for Greens
1. **Single-issue perception:**
	- Name suggests focus on environment only
	- Implies nothing distinctive on non-environmental issues
	- David Suzuki argues: Better strategy for environmentalists = make existing parties "greener"
		- Green Party diverts efforts away from parties that exercise power
	- Difficult to distinguish from Liberal/NDP on many issues
2. **Economic growth approach:**
	- Western societies measure health by wealth and economic growth indicators
	- Politicians punished for shrinking economies
	- But economic growth = key driver of environmental destruction
	- **Dilemma:** Zero-growth economy (prophetic but momentous changes) vs. sustainable/clean growth?
		- Greens take latter approach
		- Vulnerable to criticism that contemporary growth inherently unsustainable
3. **High electoral threshold under SMP:**
	- Need large numbers in particular ridings, not just broad support
	- **2008:** ~7% of vote, didn't win single seat
	- **2011:** 4% of vote but elected member from BC (strongest regional base)
	- **2015:** Poured resources into leader Elizabeth May's riding to secure victory
		- Other candidates left to own devices
	- **2019:** Important step toward viability = won 3 seats (including one in New Brunswick)
- Difficult (but not impossible) for Greens to become major national party under SMP

## 7.5 The Canadian Party System
- **Party system:** Number and types of parties regime likely to have given various factors
	- Electoral system, party finance rules, federalism, political culture
- **Canada = multi-party system** (vs. single-party system like China)
	- Democratic political culture
	- Liberal democracy protects diversity of opinion
	- 21 parties legally registered by Elections Canada (2019)
		- Beyond 5 major parties, rest not competitive
		- Single-issue and rigidly ideological parties normally not successful

### Four Categories of Parties
1. **Electoralist/Brokerage Parties:**
	- Purpose: Employ professional organization to manage campaigns and win elections
	- Less focused on large, stable mass membership
	- Flexible approach to policy/ideology (adjust to attract voters)
	- Emphasize leader appeal
	- Large, pragmatic, middle-of-the-road policy positions
	- Appeal to every region, ethnic group, social class
	- **Examples:** Liberals and Conservatives
2. **Ideological Parties:**
	- Espouse ideological views outside mainstream
	- More concerned with promoting views than winning seats
	- **Examples:** Marxist-Leninists, Christian Heritage Party
	- **Note:** NDP has been on "long march" from ideological to pragmatic party
		- Moderating platform, adopting mainstream techniques to broaden appeal
		- Not as mainstream as Liberal/Conservative, but not ideological party
3. **Single-Issue Parties:**
	- Resemble ideological parties (promote point of view over electoral success)
	- Focus on single issue rather than ideology
	- **Example:** Marijuana Party
4. **Protest Parties:**
	- Emerge among people who believe dominant forces ignore them
	- Angry enough to use vote to express protest
	- In Canada: Typically regional in character (profound regional divisions)
	- **Examples:** Reform Party (western-based), BQ and Créditistes (Quebec-based)

### Why Brokerage Parties Dominate Canada
1. **Responsible government structure:**
	- Places premium on being on government side of House
	- In separation of powers regime: Even small parties have influence via Congressional committees
	- In parliamentary government: Legislation controlled by party in power
		- Opposition has influence only if government in minority
	- Electoral system + parliamentary form → encourages small number of large brokerage parties
2. **SMP electoral system:**
	- Systematically promotes majority governments
	- Over-rewards large national brokerage parties
	- Thwarts new national parties (Green), single-issue parties, small ideological parties
	- **Exception:** Encourages regional protest parties when conditions ripe
		- Rewards parties concentrating votes in small number of ridings vs. dispersing across country

### Historical Evolution
- **Early stage:** Liberals and Conservatives only (two-party phase)
- **Post-WWI:** At least one "third party" in every election
	- **1921:** Progressive Party won more seats than Conservatives
		- Regional protest party = short-term phenomenon
		- Can't win majority (merely regional)
		- Supporters realize not useful to sit constantly in opposition
		- Support declined, merged with Conservatives
- **1935:** CCF emerged (third party)
	- Regional protest + distinct ideology (democratic socialism)
	- Late 1950s: Radicalism kept it from success
	- 1961: Merged with labour → NDP (more moderate, reform vs. destruction of capitalism)
	- Leading third party for last 50 years
	- Non-mainstream ideology = not close contender for national power
	- Limited by SMP (smaller proportion of seats than votes)
- **1993 significant change:**
	- Liberals won solid majority
	- PCs handed stunning defeat (2 seats, finished fifth)
	- Two new regional parties bolted ahead:
		- BQ: 54 seats (second place, Official Opposition)
		- Reform: 52 seats (third place)
	- PCs finished fifth in 1997 and 2000
	- **At time:** Predicted end of "two-and-a-half party system"
	- **Reality:** Almost inevitable PCs and Reform would resolve differences
		- Responsible government + SMP place premium on victory
		- Competition between two dominant national parties remains key feature
- **2006-2011:** Liberal support declined, lost government 2006
	- Conservative Party (Harper): Two minorities, then majority 2011
	- NDP: Stunning Quebec foray (59 of 75 seats), became Official Opposition
	- BQ reduced to 4 MPs
	- Liberal Party: Difficulty defining itself (middle between right-Conservatives and left-NDP)
		- Succession of ineffective leaders
		- Standing seemed in terminal decline
	- **Harper strategy:** Polarize system (clear choice between right and left)
		- Marginalize Liberals
- **2015:** Liberals (Justin Trudeau) vaulted from third to majority
	- First time ever third-place party formed majority
	- **Voter volatility:**
		- NDP lost 1/3 of 2011 vote, over half seats
		- Conservatives lost 1/4 of support, government status
		- Liberals: 19% (2011) → 39% (2015); 34 seats → 184 seats
	- **Perfect conditions:** Desire for change, remove Harper, new young leader exceeded low expectations
	- Benefited from SMP system (as every majority government has)
- **2019:**
	- Liberals won more seats, stayed in power with minority (fewer votes than Conservatives)
	- Greens concentrated support into 3 ridings (elected 3 candidates)
	- NDP's 2011 Quebec breakthrough almost entirely reversed (reduced to 1 seat)
- **Importance of leadership:** Trudeau (2015), Singh (2019), Blanchet (2019) credited with party success
	- Scheer blamed for Conservative failure to win majority, resigned

## 7.6 The Organization of Political Parties
- **Canadian national election = 338 separate simultaneous elections**
	- Each person casts one vote for MP (unlike U.S. with multiple votes)
	- Parties organized at constituency level
	- Each major party has 338 local constituency organizations

### Constituency Organization Purposes
1. Attract members into party
2. Raise money for operations and campaigns
3. Choose candidate to run in riding
4. Send members to provincial/national conventions (debate policy, elect officials, help choose leader)

### Constituency Association
- Has own constitution and executive
- Full membership may meet 1-2 times/year between elections
- More active when:
	- Selecting delegates to national convention
	- Election approaches
- **Candidate selection:** Most important function
	- Increasingly: Central party officers (including leader) take interest
	- Parties vet candidates to prevent embarrassment
	- May encourage selection of diverse candidates
	- In some cases: Leader appoints desired candidate or refuses to sign nomination papers
- **"Green-light" vetting:** Sophisticated, detailed vetting of prospective candidates
	- Ensure suitable, reliable representatives
	- Prevent disreputable past or inappropriate social media from emerging
	- **Effect:** Limits autonomy of local riding associations

### Party Leadership
- **Leader's role in elections:**
	- Provides campaign platform
	- Chooses campaign strategy
	- Sets party's course
	- Usually gets credit for success, pays price for defeat
	- Major defeat often followed by reorganization, leadership convention
	- Leaders often resign after electoral defeat (Harper resigned night of 2015 election)

### Leadership Selection Evolution
1. **Early 20th century:** Caucus meeting of MPs and senators
	- Left MPs with some power over leader
	- Leader more directly accountable to them
2. **Leadership convention (1919 for Liberals):**
	- Party sent fixed number of delegates to convention
	- Delegates elected leader
	- Most delegates = constituency association representatives
	- Others = party association representatives, MPs, senators, provincial assembly members
	- 4,000-5,000 delegates total
	- Secret ballot, successive votes dropping lowest until majority
	- **Effect:** Greatly increased power of party leader over caucus
		- Leader owed position to party organization as whole, not caucus
		- Could speak for party over/against caucus members
3. **All-member vote (late 20th century onward):**
	- Resembles American "primary"
	- Each member votes directly for leadership candidate (vs. delegates)
	- **Liberal Party 2012 innovation:** Category of "supporter"
		- Encourage people to associate with party and vote for leader
		- Without becoming formal dues-paying members
		- 2013: Boasted ~300,000 members and supporters
		- Opened doors to people drawn to candidate, not party
	- **Effect:** Makes leadership selection more democratic (greater participation)
		- Produces more powerful leaders
		- Leader owes position to entire membership, not party elite
		- Whether more powerful leaders = more democratic is open question

### Federal and Provincial Organization
- Parties organized at both levels
- Some parties: Strong links between levels
- Others: Federal and provincial parties completely independent
- Common: Party politically successful provincially but not federally (or vice versa)
	- Voters distinguish between provincial and federal wings
	- **Example:** 2000 federal election: PCs didn't elect single Ontario MP even though Ontario PCs controlled provincial legislature

### Parliamentary vs. Extra-Parliamentary Wing
- **Parliamentary wing:** All party members with seat in Parliament
- **Extra-parliamentary wing:** Party executive at constituency, provincial, national levels

| Parliamentary Wing | Extra-Parliamentary Wing |
|-------------------|-------------------------|
| Parliamentary leader | Party president |
| Caucus | National executive |
|  | Constituency associations |

- **Parliamentary wing wields disproportionate power:**
	- Elected MPs view policies/positions in terms of re-election
	- Must respond to ongoing House events
	- Forced to formulate policies before next policy convention
	- **Reality:** Parties are electoral machines designed to win elections
	- Parliamentary wing best positioned to guide toward electoral success
	- Leader continues to dominate parliamentary wing

## 7.7 Financing Political Parties
- Substantial money necessary for national party
	- Offices, staffing, advertising, campaigns
	- Three main parties each spent ~$20 million in 2011 campaign

### Historical Evolution
- **Early Canadian democracy:** Relied on donations from wealthy individuals, large corporations
	- **Problem:** Bribery and corruption
	- John A. Macdonald and Mackenzie King defeated after soliciting large donations for government favours
	- **Solution:** "Bagmen" (low-profile, well-connected individuals) solicited donations discreetly
- **1970 legislation:** Parties required to register with chief electoral officer
	- Names appeared on ballots for first time
	- Age of regulation began
- **1974 laws:** Encourage individual Canadians to donate
	- Reduce influence of large corporate/union donors
	- Individual donors could claim generous tax credit on income tax return
- **2004 major change:**
	- Corporate/union donations sharply limited
	- **Per-vote public subsidy:** ~$2.00/year for every vote in previous election
		- Added to revenues
		- Assisted parties failing to generate individual donations
		- Meant money based on votes, not seats
		- But encouraged parties to look to state vs. supporters for funding
- **Stephen Harper changes:**
	- Terminated subsidy
	- Banned corporate/union donations altogether
	- Increased individual donation amounts
	- Increased tax incentives for donors

### Current Regulatory Framework (as of 2019)
1. **Contribution Limits:**
	- Only Canadian citizens and permanent residents can donate
	- Per year: Up to $1,600 to each registered party
	- Up to $1,600 total to constituency associations, nomination contests, candidates of each party
	- Up to $1,600 total for contestants in party leadership contest
	- Candidates can commit higher amounts to own campaigns
2. **Spending Limits:**
	- **Nomination candidates:** ~20% of candidate election campaign limit
	- **Candidates (2019):** ~$100,000 per campaign
		- Tied to number of electors per riding
		- Larger electorate = higher limit
		- Tied to campaign length
		- **Examples:** Surrey Centre BC: $106,000; Charlottetown PEI: $86,500
	- **Not counted as expense:** Polls, fundraising
	- **Parties:** Maximum depends on number of candidates and campaign length
		- 2019: Parties running all 338 candidates allowed ~$29 million (~$86,000/riding)
	- **Election expense limits:** Cover only campaign period
		- With fixed dates: Parties can spend on unofficial campaign before writs
	- **2018 legislation:** Regulated pre-writ spending
		- 2019: $2 million limit in 2-month period before election call
3. **Reimbursements:**
	- **Candidates:** 60% of expenses if:
		- Win at least 10% of vote
		- Submit all reports to Elections Canada
		- (Reimbursements go to electoral district association, not candidate)
	- **Parties:** 50% of national expenses if:
		- Get at least 2% of national vote, OR
		- 5% of vote in ridings where ran candidates
4. **Tax Credits for Political Contributions:**
	- Up to $650 generous tax credit
	- **Example:** Donate $400 → receive $300 credit
		- Government actually giving party/candidate $300, donor giving $100
5. **Reporting Requirements:**
	- Parties and candidates submit detailed reports of donations and expenses
	- Public documents
	- Subject to fines, criminal charges for failures/misrepresentations
	- Forfeit reimbursements if fail to comply
6. **Cash for Access:**
	- Historically: Parties raised funds via dinners with elected officials
	- Dubbed "cash for access": Pay fee, get ear of official in government
	- **Now regulated:** If attendance fee >$200 and prominent official present:
		- Party must publicly advertise event
		- Report to Elections Canada
7. **False Statements:**
	- **2018 legislation:** Increased regulation of campaign statements
	- Prohibits false statements about candidate's:
		- Biography
		- Whether violated law or under investigation
		- Citizenship, qualifications, related information
		- (Also for leaders and party officials)

### Provincial Variation
- Provinces regulate party politics per own priorities
- Many rules similar to federal
- Several provide annual public subsidies based on vote counts

## 7.8 Party Government and Party Politics
- **Parliamentary government = party government**
- **Canadian politics = party politics**
- **Benefits:**
	- Clear division into parties allows easy determination of election winner
	- Identifies who will form next government
	- Governor General's obligation easy to discharge (ensure workable government)
	- Parties provide clear outcomes and parliamentary leader who becomes PM
	- **Without parties:** Public wouldn't know who PM would be
		- Without party discipline: Governments would come/go with problematic frequency
		- Party leadership + discipline = stable parliamentary government
- **Conclusion:** Only thing worse than party politics = parliamentary politics without parties

### Criticisms
- **Troubling instances:** Party interests appear to take priority over national interests
	- Government (party leadership) always looking out for future electoral interests
- **Power of party leaders:**
	- In government: Determines cabinet positions
	- In opposition: Assigns "shadow cabinet" positions (critics for government departments)
		- High-profile positions with frequent national media exposure
		- Opportunity to build specialized expertise
		- Well-placed for ministerial position if party wins
	- **Question:** Have leaders become too powerful?
		- Dominate Parliament and public view of politics
		- Impression politics = struggle between 3-4 individuals (and advisors)
- **Important reminder:** Leaders' political power ultimately depends on public opinion
	- Can leader lead party to success in next election?
	- Power declines when party senses defeat
	- Liberal democratic regime not direct democracy
	- But political power ultimately rests on votes and opinions of people
