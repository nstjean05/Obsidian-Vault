### Hard and Soft Law in International Governance (Abbott and Snidal, _International Organization_, 2000)

#### Intro

- International relations are heavily legalized, but with huge variety; few arrangements approach hard law, most is "soft"
- Argument: actors choose legal forms to solve specific substantive and political problems, and choose **soft** forms when they're the superior solution
- **Hard law**: legally binding obligations that are precise (or can be made precise) and delegate authority to interpret and implement
- **Soft law**: weakened along one or more of **obligation, precision, delegation**
    - Varying degrees and combinations; a _continuum_, not a binary, running to purely political arrangements
- Notation {O,P,D}: capital = high, lowercase = moderate, dash = low
    - Ex. {O,P,D} is hard law; {-,P,-} is precise but nonbinding with minimal delegation
- Critics of soft law
    - Realists: no independent judiciary or enforcement, so all intl law is window dressing
        - Downs et al. (1996): agreements just reflect what states would do anyway
    - Lawyers (Weil): soft law could destabilize the normative system
    - Others: only a way station to harder law
- Authors: soft law is often _deliberately_ chosen
    - Gets many hard law benefits at lower cost, plus its own advantages
    - Easier to achieve, esp. for states jealous of autonomy
    - Better for uncertainty (learning over time)
    - Helps compromise across interests, values, time horizons, power
- Key variables: transaction costs, uncertainty, sovereignty implications, divergence of preferences, power differentials
- Frames it around states first, nonstate actors in the final section

### Contracts and Covenants: Rationales for Hard Law

- **Contract** (interests; rationalist) vs **covenant** (normative commitments; constructivist)
- Authors reject the sharp split: law is both
    
    1. Actors use law to pursue interests _and_ values, which are intertwined
    2. They use both normative and interest-based strategies to build it
    3. Rules work by changing incentives _and_ by changing understandings and identities
    
    - Hammer and wrench: use both tools, no need to declare one better
- Use contracting theory to organize the analysis

#### Credible commitments

- Hard to credibly commit under anarchy; matters when one side performs first or makes relation-specific investments (assurance games)
- Disaggregate the state: political investments, binding successors or other branches, shaping citizens' incentives
    - Ex. Mexico legalized NAFTA to reassure foreign investors, accepting binding arbitration
- No central enforcement, but legalization still raises credibility
    - Limits self-serving _auto-interpretation_ (precision, coherence with principles, courts)
    - Raises the cost of reneging: reputational damage generalizes across all intl law
    - Alternatives (bonding, escrow) cost more; intl law underpins statehood itself
    - Judicial review (EU, European human rights, WTO) tests allegations under accepted standards
        - Ex. EU's losses over hormone-treated beef and bananas
    - Law of state responsibility allows proportional _countermeasures_
    - Some centralized enforcement (UNSC, IFIs)
    - Domestic incorporation raises delegation
        - Ex. Whaling Convention and CITES violations carry US criminal penalties
        - Ex. Pinochet: Spanish magistrate enforced torture conventions; Lords held him liable only for acts after the convention entered British law
    - Mobilizes legal groups and bureaucracies; raises audience costs
    - Normative channels: _pacta sunt servanda_, good faith, legitimacy's "compliance pull" (Franck: determinacy, coherence, procedure)
    - Legal discourse requires justification via text, precedent, analogy; disqualifies pure interest arguments
- **Hypotheses**: states use hard law when
    1. Cooperation benefits are high and opportunism is costly (trade, investment; reciprocal, nonsimultaneous). Less so in coordination situations
    2. Noncompliance is hard to detect (arms control)
    3. Forming "clubs" of sincerely committed states (EU, NATO), as an ex ante sorting device. Not needed for looser groups like APEC
    4. Executives want to bind other agencies or legislatures and have room to act with little interference
    5. (Secondary) the state is in other legal regimes and has strong domestic legal institutions

#### Reducing transactions costs

- Two interactions: _managerial_ (interpret, apply, elaborate) and _adversarial_ (enforce)
- Hard law sets bounds on negotiation, forces consistency with existing norms and specialized procedures
    - States bargain "in the shadow" of legal decisions; unauthorized coercion looks illegitimate
    - Ex. WTO required members to use dispute settlement, not unilateral action (aimed at US Section 301)
- **Demandeurs** (those seeking commitments) prefer hard law; resisters want flexibility
    - Demandeurs seek hard law when opportunism is high and detection hard, to limit membership to the committed, and when other states' executives agree but elites diverge

#### Modifying political strategies

- Legal institutions open new tactics
    - Ex. ICTY: states lobby for officials, gather evidence, push indictments (ex. Milosevic), arrange arrests
- Domestic litigation becomes part of the toolkit; states support private litigants
    - Supranational judges encourage private litigants and national courts (Alter on the EU)
- States favour hard law when its strategies suit them
    - Ex. US has big legal staffs; states confident agreements track their preferences; powerful states have an _overlooked_ stake
    - Sublimates political conflict into legal argument

#### Incomplete contracting

- Precision can be wasteful, counterproductive, rigid, or block agreement
- Complete contracts are impossible (bounded rationality, uncertainty)
- **Delegation** is the best fix
    - Ex. ECJ on competition law ("concerted practices"); ECHR on "inhuman and degrading treatment"
    - Softer regimes use nonjudicial procedures that need state consent
- States delegate when gains are large, consensus on principles exists, but applications are hard to anticipate

### The Advantages of Soft Legalization

#### Contracting costs

- Hard law cuts post-agreement costs but raises _contracting_ costs
    - Legal specialists, lengthy reviews, differing legal traditions, ratification
- Ex. **ILO**: low ratification rates led directors-general to favour recommendations and codes of conduct over conventions (labour resisted)
- Ex. **1997 OECD anti-bribery convention**
    - US wanted a binding treaty; states that had resisted flipped to _support_ binding to exploit high costs
    - US backed a nonbinding recommendation; compromise was a short treaty deadline, else a recommendation
- Intl politics magnifies costs
    - Sovereignty jealousy, security and distributional concerns, multilateral talks, ill-defined scope (ex. magazines: economic or cultural?), thin institutional context
- Soft devices: escape clauses {o,P,d}, imprecise commitments {O,p,d}, political delegation {O,p,-}
- Ex. **Nuclear regime**: NPT binding; sensitive technical issues via IAEA recommendations {-,P,d}, some later hardened (spent fuel and waste)
- Ex. **Trade**: ITO charter too hard to negotiate and resisted in the US; **GATT 1947** was "provisional" with a lenient withdrawal clause and skeletal institutions {o,p,-}
    - Became the WTO as states learned harder legalization pays
- Hypothesis: softer forms attract more as contracting costs rise

#### Sovereignty costs

- Binding obligations, especially delegation, are costly
- Krasner's four meanings: domestic, interdependence, international legal, Westphalian
- Scale of costs
    - Low: limits on behaviour in particular circumstances (though can still limit border control or domestic policy)
    - Greater: external authority over significant decisions
        - Ex. "democratic deficit"; "faceless bureaucrats" at the WTO
    - Highest: state-citizen or territory relations
        - Ex. human rights regimes, ICC jurisdiction over US soldiers, Law of the Sea
- Withdrawal is possible, but enmeshment makes it costly; can even diminish _legal_ sovereignty
- Delegation is the biggest source of unanticipated costs ("authority always becomes to a degree uncontrollable," Lindblom)
    - Ex. ECJ turned Article 177 into a tool for private litigants to challenge national policies
    - Ex. France and Canada's cultural policies trimmed by WTO and NAFTA rulings
    - Domestic delegation is cheaper (states control courts)
- Even powerful states resist
    - Ex. US on Law of the Sea's Enterprise, ICC, UN; NAFTA delegation limited to Chapter 19 and not self-executing; Congress's WTO review with fast-track withdrawal
- **Negative** sovereignty costs: groups that gain (free-trade coalitions, environmentalists), governments uncertain of staying in power binding successors, and arrangements that boost standing (Montevideo, UN Charter art. 2, OAS Charter)
- Positive costs remain the standard case
- Limiting costs: nonbinding, imprecise, limited delegation
    - Ex. Council of Europe minorities convention {O,p,d}; special protocols or party consent before jurisdiction; arms control consultation
- Ex. **Money laundering**: FATF (OECD, 1989) uses recommendations, peer review, mild sanctions
    - Flexible, still legitimizes outside participation, produced real convergence
- **Issue type**
    - Security: highest costs; even NATO is {O,p,d}; SALT precise and binding but minimal institutions
    - Political economy: technical matters (transport, food standards, ISO) highly legalized; investment, money laundering, export controls, tax much less
    - Trade: in between, benefits often outweigh costs (varies by state and over time, ex. agriculture)
    - EU: most institutionalized, from strong commitment or a long legalized process
    - WTO is now stronger than the ITO would have been: GATT success shifted domestic politics, and states learned

#### Uncertainty

- New or complex issues make delegation tempting but costly in sovereignty
- Soft options
    1. **Reduce precision** {O,p,d}
        - Can't be precise about unknown contingencies
        - _Risk_ (known probabilities) vs _uncertainty_ (Knight); _ambiguity-averse_ actors prefer imprecise deals
        - Ex. climate framework agreements
    2. **Precise but nonbinding** {-,P,-}, ex. Agenda 21 and Forest Principles (Rio 1992), or escape clauses
    3. **Moderate delegation**: UN specialized agencies {-,p,d}; arms control uses forums for bargaining, not third-party decisions
- Learning dynamic: Vienna Ozone Convention {O,p,-}; hortatory rules; Helsinki Final Act with review sessions; technical standard-setting bodies
- Ex. **FAO-UNEP prior informed consent** on hazardous chemicals
    - Code of conduct (1985), guidelines (1987), PIC added (1989) as {-,P,d}
    - Hardening at Rio 1992 failed; later a 1998 treaty tracked the system almost exactly
- Soft law = rational adaptation: capture the easy gains, adapt as things change, cheaper than renegotiation
- **2x2 hypotheses**
    - Low sovereignty cost, low uncertainty: hard {O,P,D}
    - High sovereignty cost, low uncertainty: precise and binding, little delegation {O,P,-}
    - Low sovereignty cost, high uncertainty: binding, moderate delegation, imprecise {O,p,d}
    - Both high: flexible or hortatory {o,p,-} or {-,p,-}

#### Soft law as a tool of compromise

- **At a point in time**
    - Heterogeneous states: set general goals, let each adapt implementation
    - Defuse sticky issues in package deals (ex. NAFTA labour and environment side agreements)
    - Different readiness: exceptions, reservations, phase-ins for developing countries
    - Ex. **Wassenaar Arrangement** (1996) {-,p,-}
        - Successor to CoCom; faced more nations, no common enemy, uneven costs, uneven technical readiness
        - Core is info exchange on past exports; consensus-based; US got conventional and dual-use goods, lists, target nations, transparency
    - Cost: harder to detect shirking, weaker tool for committing domestic actors; fix with mixed hardness, reporting, domestic implementation
- **Over time**
    - A patient state trades a smaller present payoff for a soft deal that _enmeshes_ an impatient state
    - Ex. **Helsinki**: USSR needed immediate stabilization; West wouldn't legally recognize Soviet dominance but accepted nonbinding terms in return for a human rights framework (Basket III) with long-run bite
    - Not an inevitable life cycle from soft to hard
        - Hard law more likely evolves from soft than from "utopian" full-blown plans
        - Sometimes soft is the best attainable
    - States learn that even soft law has effects (Helsinki, ECJ backlash), so impatient states may demand a higher price (ex. China on human rights)
- **Between weak and strong**
    - Legal view: law shields the weak (Weil, Reisman, constitutionalism)
        - Ex. Latin America vs the US; African support for postcolonial boundaries
        - Small states pay lower sovereignty costs, even negative
    - IR view: law reflects power (realists: epiphenomenal; institutionalists: power matters but institutions have real effects)
    - Reconciliation: rules _regularize_ asymmetric relations
        - Strong embed advantage in settled rules, cheaper than constant coercion
        - Weak get certainty and credibility, like an insurance premium
        - Strong still must make terms attractive enough for broad participation
    - Powerful states care most about delegation, so choose limited delegation {O,p,-} or {O,p,d}, administrative over judicial bodies
    - Self-binding helps strong states get weak states to take risky actions
        - Ex. US ran the Gulf War through the UNSC, winning bases in Saudi Arabia and Japanese funding; others could monitor its scope
    - _Laundering_: legalized arrangements spare weak-state governments from looking like they follow a powerful state's orders
    - Ex. **Law of the Sea**: more territorial sovereignty for littoral states, a role for LDCs in resources, military passage protected; delegation limited
    - Ex. **NPT**: weak states accepted the nuclear oligopoly, powerful states agreed to restraint and technology transfer; IAEA controlled by major powers
    - Process: powerful states advantaged. Outcome: even they accept legal constraints, but shape substance and institutions

### The Role of Private Actors

- Interest groups, epistemic communities (Haas), NGOs, advocacy coalitions, and transgovernmental units are key to new agreements
- Three accounts: **pluralist**, **public choice**, **statist**

#### Pluralist (Moravcsik's liberal account)

- Groups within and across states are the fundamental actors; govts ratify private bargains
- Domestic variation means more divergent preferences, higher costs, so soft law is _more_ valuable
    - Ex. Agenda 21, minorities convention
- Private demandeurs push for hard law
    - Ex. Mexican business (NAFTA), high tech firms (TRIPs), ILO workers resisting softening
    - Activists use the "boomerang effect" (Keck and Sikkink)
- Hard law gives new strategies: access to dispute settlement, invoking rules in national courts, urging govts to espouse claims
    - Ex. WTO Appellate Body accepts friend-of-the-court briefs from NGOs
- Compromise forms
    - Demandeurs good at litigation: binding and precise, yield on delegation {O,P,-}
    - Activists: precise normative statements {-,P,-}, which also suits business (no concrete enforcement)
        - Ex. Rio Declaration, Agenda 21, Forest Principles
- Soft instruments fuel "accountability politics" by exposing gaps between commitments and conduct (ex. Beijing Declaration, Helsinki)

#### Public choice

- Officials pursue private rewards (reelection, contributions)
- May back hard law for credible commitments (ex. TRIPs)
- But soft law appeals
    - Supply present benefits while keeping future rents
    - Avoid taking sides in distributional conflicts (study, vague principles, pass the buck)
    - Transgovernmental bodies (Basle Committee, IOSCO) lack treaty authority and avoid scrutiny, so soft law fits

#### Statism

- Govts keep some autonomy; act as "transmission belts" unless national autonomy is at stake
    - Soft law answers private demands while limiting sovereignty costs, so {-,P,-} is common
    - Ex. Rio and Beijing declarations adopted by nearly every nation _because_ nonbinding
- Complex version: state as a broker between private actors
    - Ex. NAFTA labour side agreement: binding and precise on enforcing national labour laws {O,P,d}, but vague on the _content_ of those laws {-,p,d}
    - Environmental side agreement let private parties initiate reviews
- Divergent discount rates (Helsinki: impatient activists, patient Western govts)
- Offensive uses: binding deals to forestall domestic groups; nonbinding or imprecise ones to introduce unpopular rules
    - Ex. Vienna Ozone Convention, WTO services framework (change discourse, leave costly regulation for later)

### Conclusion

- Legalization is diverse because it helps diverse actors solve diverse problems
- Tradeoffs
    - States: hard law's benefits (commitment, incomplete contracting) vs sovereignty costs
    - Private actors: want hard law matching their interests, but conflict with others
    - Soft law balances competing considerations and handles uncertainty and power gaps
- Combines **contract** (rational incentives) and **covenant** (norms)
    - Legalization embeds actors in a partially autonomous process and discourse that constrains them
- International politics and law are deeply intertwined, not separate realms
    - Politics limits law's autonomy; politics is rooted in legal foundations (sovereignty, diplomacy, war, commerce)
- Delegation shows the link: states rarely give independent courts general, mandatory jurisdiction, mostly delegating to IOs and administrative bodies under controls
- Future: neither "Pollyanna nor Chicken Little"
    - Post-WWII expansion largely caught up with globalization; no guarantee of continued hardening
- Final claim: **soft law is valuable in its own right, not just a stepping-stone to hard law**
