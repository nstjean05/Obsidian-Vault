# Chapter 8: Federalism

## 8.1 What Is Federalism?
- **Federalism:** Political system dividing power between central government and regional governments
- Each level of government has own area of jurisdiction
- Both levels sovereign in their respective areas
- **Why federalism?**
	- Union of different territories (British colonies wanted to preserve separate identities)
	- Diversity requires decentralized power
	- Regional differences need regional solutions

## 8.2 Why a Federal Union?
- **Confederation rationale:**
	- British colonies (Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick) wanted union for:
		- Common defence
		- Economic benefits (common market)
		- But wanted to preserve regional identities/powers
	- Feared centralized American system
- **Result:** Hybrid system
	- Strong federal government (John A. Macdonald's vision)
	- But provinces retain significant powers
	- Designed so federal power would dominate

## 8.3 Original Design of Federal Union
- **Federal powers (Section 91 of CA 1867):**
	- Peace, order, good government
	- Criminal law
	- Trade and commerce
	- Taxation (any method)
	- Banking, currency, military
- **Provincial powers (Section 92 of CA 1867):**
	- Education
	- Health/hospitals
	- Property and civil rights
	- Direct taxation only
	- Liquor licensing
- **Federal advantages by design:**
	- Broader tax powers (could raise indirect taxes; provinces only direct)
	- Spending power in provincial areas (create leverage)
	- Powers of reservation/disallowance (federal government could disallow provincial laws)
		- **Note:** Unused for decades; now considered unconstitutional convention

## 8.4 Historical Development of Federalism in Canada
### Five Periods of Federalism Evolution

1. **Quasi-Federalism (1867-1896)**
	- Macdonald era, federal government much stronger
	- Relationship like Britain-Canada colonial arrangement
	- Federal government dominated major initiatives
	- Provincial legislation occasionally reserved/disallowed

2. **Classical Federalism (1896-1914)**
	- Laurier elected, Liberals championed provincial rights
	- Balance shifted toward provinces
	- Courts eroded federal powers (peace/order/good govt, trade/commerce)
	- Provincial jurisdictions expanded (property/civil rights)
	- **Maritime Bank case (1892):** Courts rejected notion that provinces subordinate to federal
	- Each level sovereign in assigned jurisdictions

3. **Emergency Federalism (1914-1960)**
	- Balance swung back to federal government
	- WWI and WWII required strong national leadership
	- Great Depression bankrupted provinces
	- Only federal government could afford welfare programs
	- Centralization increased

4. **Cooperative Federalism (1960-1995)**
	- Post-war economic growth strengthened provinces
	- Provinces raised more revenue, more powerful
	- Federal government sought relevance through federal-provincial cooperation
	- **Executive federalism:** Regular meetings of first ministers and cabinet ministers
	- Constant bargaining and coordination

5. **Collaborative/Open Federalism (1995-present)**
	- 1995 budget: Reduced federal transfers, simplified conditions
	- Gave provinces more spending discretion
	- Less cooperation required
	- But sectoral agreements on mutual standards (environment, childcare, workers)
	- **Different PM approaches:**
		- Chrétien: Avoided formal engagement, bilateral relations
		- Martin: More engaged, formal agreements
		- Harper: Less collaboration, "open federalism" (classical orientation)
		- Trudeau: Collaborative, consultative approach

## 8.5 Financing Government and Federal-Provincial Relations
### Three Branches of Fiscal Federalism

1. **Taxation**
	- Federal: "Any mode of taxation" (indirect + direct)
	- Provinces: "Direct taxation within province" only
	- Problem: Both now occupy same fields (income tax, sales tax)
	- Federal dominated these fields historically (political difficulty for provinces to add their own)
	- **Tax room/tax points:** Federal can relinquish tax room to provinces

2. **Federal Spending Power**
	- Federal government involved in provincial jurisdictions through financing
	- **Conditional grants:** Federal money with conditions on spending
		- Ex. Healthcare: Billions transferred but provinces must respect principles (no user fees)
		- Effective leverage on provincial policy
	- **Unconditional grants:** Block funding with broad national standards
		- Allows provincial discretion
	- **Current programs:**
		- Canada Health Transfer (CHT)
		- Canada Social Transfer (CST)
		- 2019-20: ~$55 billion total

3. **Equalization Payments**
	- Federal responsibility to provide money to provinces with below-average tax revenues
	- **Purpose:** Ensure rough equality in government services and taxation across provinces
	- **Entrenched in CA 1982, Section 36**
	- **How it works:**
		- Formula determines provincial fiscal capacity
		- Provinces below national average receive transfers
		- Transfers unconditional (provinces spend as they wish)
		- Currently ~$17 billion/year
	- **Controversy:**
		- Wealthier provinces (like Alberta) resent subsidizing others
		- Generally misunderstood (richer provinces don't directly subsidize; federal coffers)
		- Vital for smaller provinces (20%+ of revenues in NB, PEI)

## 8.6 Other Orders of Government

### Territorial Governments
- Three territories: Yukon, Northwest Territories, Nunavut
- Not independent from federal government like provinces
- Powers more limited, delegated by federal government
- Receive significant federal funding

### Municipal Governments
- Created by provinces ("creatures of the province")
- No independent constitutional status
- Powers delegated by provincial governments
- Can be abolished or altered by provinces
- Handle local issues: policing, roads, sewers, local planning

## 8.7 Federalism and Indigenous Self-Government
- **Ongoing issue:** Recognition of Indigenous peoples' right to self-government
- **Current status:** Limited self-government agreements in some areas
- **Challenges:**
	- Fit Indigenous governance into federal/provincial division of powers
	- Resource and financing issues
	- Reconciliation with colonial past

## 8.8-8.9 Challenges and Current Pressures

### Pressures Affecting Federalism
1. **Decentralizing forces:**
	- Regional economic patterns (Alberta oil, Quebec distinctiveness)
	- Weak Canadian identity, strong local attachments
	- Quebec nationalist demands for strong provincial government

2. **Centralizing forces:**
	- Need for national standards (social services, human rights)
	- Protection from American domination (economy, culture)
	- Global issues (climate change) require national response

### Major Issues
- **Climate change:** Federal carbon pricing vs. provincial resistance
- **Healthcare:** Federal transfers with conditions vs. provincial autonomy
- **Indigenous reconciliation:** Overlapping jurisdictions
- **Quebec sovereignty:** Ongoing constitutional tension
