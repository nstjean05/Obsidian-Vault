6. Suppose that statecraft does to some extent become globalized. What degree of power and authority ought to be transferred from the current national level to a global one? How can the details of universal involvement in decision-making be worked out practically?

I think a significant amount of authority can be transferred to a global government. One of the major issues regarding this is that of compliance. Obligation, precision, and delegation form OPD notation, a framework developed by Abbott and Snidal to describe how legalized a commitment is, meaning how binding, precise and enforceable it is. If any one of them is missing, then authority will not be followed. Without obligation, states are not legally bound to their commitments. A loose precision means actors are able to skirt around the limits of the law. Finally, delegation indicates who is given authority to interpret and enforce the rules. By balancing OPD, a more globalized statecraft can adjust the extent to which states exhibit sovereignty. A completely centralized global government, with high OPD, would have complete authority and control to do what it deems.

On the other hand, you have a system like the UN, where states choose to take part, and face little real accountability. For a functioning global government, the world would likely still need to be subdivided into state-like entities, allowing for some level of regional autonomy. A UN-like system would likely be the clearest realistic path to a globalized government, if there is some set of global circumstances that incentivized the states of the world to cede some of their autonomy, in exchange for greater cohesion. This is what has happened in the EU, and is theoretically possible at a much larger scale.

  

16. Argue for or against the suggestion that global nuclear disarmament is either (a) impossible, or (b) inevitable.

Given the most recent global events, it seems to be the case that global nuclear disarmament is impossible. Championed in the post-Cold-War era, nuclear disarmament was the aspiration for all nuclear weapons to be disassembled, given the potentially civilization-ending results of their use. However, rather than getting rid of all nuclear weapons, an easier goal was to simply concentrate the weapons amongst a smaller number of powers. This could be done through mutual assurance agreements, and countries like Ukraine, Belarus, Lithuania, Kazakhstan,  and South Africa gave up or dismantled their weapons. The current situation in Ukraine, however, has likely undone the possibility of these arrangements. The U.S. promised defense of Ukraine’s sovereignty if they gave up their weapons, yet has now reneged on that agreement. This is a signal to other nations that if you do not have nuclear weapons, you cannot guarantee your protection, and may lead to an increased pursuit of nukes by states protected by treaties.

Another reason that nuclear disarmament is unlikely is the pursuit of weapons by warring states, such as in the case of India and Pakistan. Pakistan must have nuclear weapons, or else it is helpless to the pressures of India - and vice versa. This is also occurring as states like China rise, and begin to build massive arsenals to be on the same level as Russia or U.S. China is further motivated by its goal of reunifying with Taiwan. Not to mention North Korea’s whole existence is predicated on the fact they cannot be invaded, due to the asymmetric retaliation they can inflict. And none of these states will ever completely disarm, since it leaves them vulnerable to the rest. Mutually assured destruction has thus been a very effective mechanism for avoiding nuclear conflicts, but leaves the whole world more vulnerable to such conflicts.

There is a case to be made that given a long enough time horizon, nuclear disarmament is inevitable. The cynical approach would be to say that nuclear weapons will one day be rendered obsolete, given that they are not as powerful as the weaponry of the future. If a state has space lasers that can incinerate anything on the earth with targeted precision, or a bomb that can kill all life forms in a region without delivering nuclear fallout, then why would they use the brutish nuke? On the contrary, one could take an optimistic view. Globalization, in recent decades, is said to have died. Its optimism that the world will grow more peaceful through interconnection has waned, as more conflicts arise despite it. However, I think that this is still an ideal the world can work towards. As society advances, and people are able to travel and make global relationships with less friction, the Earth may reach a point where its borders don’t carry the weight they once did. Perhaps, in this enlightened society of abundance, humanity may no longer see the need for weaponry. While it seems very far off, this is a future that I think many could spend their lives striving for.

  

29. (a) In the introductory dialogue for the section on Technology and War, Mara gives us a view of a society that is warlike, but has banned all but hand-wielded striking weapons such as swords. Is this situation possible? enforceable? Stable?

This was a very interesting dialogue, and quite something to try and wrap my head around. It seems to resemble the system of our world’s European feudal age, with its distinct class structure, honour society, and weaponry. However, there are several critical differences that make this possible. First, the common people appear to have a great deal more power than ours did, enforcing the leadership's accountability. Additionally, given their high level of advancement, society has developed strongly. This situation seems theoretically possible, but I think highly unlikely. My intuition is that Ortho would be very unstable, as its people are continuously in conflict, with a very violent method of resolving conflict. Most advanced societies try to abstract themselves away from blood and death, as war and medicine become things done far away or behind closed doors. I don’t think it is realistic for a society to run towards this at scale, when a more peaceful alternative is readily available. It may also lead to a very unstable society, as cheating is highly incentivised, and readily available. Consider the aforementioned Congresspeople (8.44) who swear not to take advantage of their positions for selfish gain, yet do so, suspiciously finding cushy jobs after their terms. Even in this honour society, individuals would attempt to get away with fraud. It also wouldn’t get many of the benefits of scale that occur with modern globalization. 

  

(b) In the same dialogue, there is a discussion of equality between the sexes and the consequences for women in battle. Discuss further and comment.

Historically, societies sent men into battle for a few practical reasons. The most fundamental comes down to evolutionary biology and reproduction: men are simply less of a demographic bottleneck. If a man dies in combat, another can take his place without reducing the number of children the community can produce. If a woman dies, that loss directly impacts the next generation’s numbers. This was especially apparent in the ancient world, where sustaining the home population was critical while armies campaigned. Alexander the Great, for instance, occasionally sent Macedonian soldiers back home from Asia specifically to start families, ensuring his military machine would still have fuel a couple of decades down the road. Beyond reproduction, physical asymmetries between the sexes, the same differences that lead to gender divisions in modern sports, also made men the default choice for hand-to-hand warfare.

None of this suggests women cannot be capable or adept in battle. In Ortho’s society, female warriors are clearly an everyday reality. Some modern studies highlight areas where women excel, such as rapid task-switching and multitasking, which would be major assets in the chaos of combat. Furthermore, technique, conditioning, and martial precision can effectively bridge physical gaps, as demonstrated by Mara’s lethal knife throw and battlefield experience. A force that draws on both men and women naturally benefits from a wider, more versatile talent pool. What stands out most is how thoroughly meritocratic this system is. It is easy for a society to pay lip service to equality, but Ortho ties it directly to voluntary oaths and measurable responsibility. Once an individual takes up the sword, they share the exact same risks and respect as anyone else, creating a form of equality that is truly earned and proven on the battlefield.
