### 6.1 Introduction
- **Diplomacy** is above all **negotiation**, direct or mediated
    - Nicolson (1939): management of intl relations by negotiation; method by which ambassadors and envoys adjust and manage relations
    - de Felice (1778): negotiation isn’t limited to intl affairs, happens anywhere there are differences to conciliate. _All life is a continual negotiation_
- Negotiation continues during war too, needed to end it
- Reaches beyond state to state: down to domestic, up to global, across from law to war
- Includes conflict mgmt and prevention
- **Track-one** is official diplomacy (state reps)
- **Track-two** is unofficial (NGOs)
    - Official side finds unofficial efforts useful for context, or where official entry would feel intrusive
    - Unofficial side needs state auspices to conclude _binding_ agreements
- Spread of democracy means public opinion and interest groups matter more
- “Diplomacy” in the loose sense = smooth wording and good manners; chapter uses the state-oriented sense
### 6.2 Negotiation and Diplomacy
- States are sovereign, self-centred, morally responsible to citizens for security and welfare
- But sovereignty, self-centredness, and security aren’t the absolutes they were
    - States need cooperation from others, sovereignty not an indivisible shield anymore
#### 6.2.1 Normal Diplomacy
- **Normal diplomacy** is ongoing communication between formally equal parties to maintain relations, do business, and prevent/handle conflicts
- Defuses the countless conflicts that _never_ turned violent
- Handles day-to-day trade, tourism, culture, comms
- Done by permanent and occasional reps, bilateral and multilateral channels
- Ambassadors have two jobs
    1. Maintain good relations with host country
    2. Gain host support for home state’s policy positions
- **“Diplomacy as usual”** is the problem with #1
    - Ambassador adopts host’s position on a rebellion, ignores that rebellions signal a problem
    - Ex. US policy in Zaire (Mobutu) in the 1990s
#### 6.2.2 Ripeness and Stalemate
- **Negotiation** is the process of combining divergent positions into a joint decision
- Happens when parties are blocked from their preferred unilateral solution and see the stalemate as the worst outcome
    - That’s **ripeness**
- Key terms (Box 6-1)
    - **Mutually hurting stalemate (MHS)** is when both parties feel they can’t escalate to a unilateral win at acceptable cost, and the impasse is costly. _Pushes_ them to negotiate
    - **Mutually enticing opportunity (MEO)** is when both feel a cooperative solution is possible. _Pulls_ them to agreement. Also called a **way out (WO)**
    - **Ripeness** is the moment both MHS and WO are present in the parties’ perceptions
    - **Ripen** is making a conflict ready by increasing perception of MHS and WO
    - **Positive sum** is where each party gains something (not necessarily equally)
    - **Zero sum** is where one party’s gain = the other’s loss
    - **Relative gains** are measured against the other party; **absolute gains** are measured against your own goal
- _Ties to the Country A/B scenario: the border stand-off is basically the MHS_
#### 6.2.3 Zero-Sum vs. Positive-Sum
- Zero-sum outcomes are unstable, loser looks to improve relative gains next round or elsewhere
- Diplomacy = managing _ongoing_ relations, not a one-shot deal
    - Richelieu: aim for “solid and durable relations,” not opportunistic arrangements
- Positive-sum diplomacy gives the other party an incentive to keep the agreement
    - “Giving something to get something,” negotiation is **setting the price**
    - Matches absolute gains thinking (Powell 1991)
- Two ways to get positive sums
    1. **Compensation** is “giving to get,” exchange of concessions on different matters
        - **Homans’ maxim**: split stakes into two piles (more valuable to one side/less costly to the other, and vice versa) and trade the piles
        - Ex. Namibia/Angola 1988: South African withdrawal from SW Africa traded for Cuban withdrawal from Angola
        - Ex. South Africa early 1990s: National Party and ANC, stalemate perceived as unacceptable
        - Ex. P5+1 and Iran (2015): sanctions flipped who was the _demandeur_, Iran bargained over how much non-proliferation it’d take to buy off sanctions
        - Problem: not all stakes are Homans-divisible. Use **side payments** (items outside original stakes)
    2. **Construction (reframing)** is redefining the stakes so both parties find an interest, instead of dividing them
        - Can’t totally remove distributive concerns, but gives **superordinate goals** and a cooperative atmosphere
        - Ex. Peru and Ecuador 1998: moved off legalisms of competing claims to reach a settlement
- _Third route in Key Points: concession or division (just splitting it)_
- Box 6-2: women negotiators in the P5+1 (Mogherini, Schmid, Sherman)
    - Mogherini: many women at the table helped be “concrete and pragmatic”
**Key points (6.2)**
1. Normal diplomacy = daily exercise handling most interstate problems
2. Ripeness required before states turn to cooperating with the opponent
3. Positive-sum is preferable, more durable
4. Three means to agreement: concession/division, compensation/exchange, construction/reframing
### 6.3 Expanding the Scope of Diplomacy
- Globalization pushes subjects of negotiation _upward_ (intl security) and _downward_ (**human security**)
- **Human security** is the security of people, populations, lives
    - Rise of humanitarian conditions, human rights, emergencies, intervention
    - Holds the state responsible for its citizens’ plight
    - NGOs are direct interveners and advocates; states follow
    - Ex. Libya 2011 (NATO, debatable motives)
    - Some states make mediation a _vocation_, ex. Norway (El Salvador, Israel, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, South Sudan)
#### 6.3.1 Internal Conflict
- End of Cold War questioned state centrality
    - Democracy legitimized opposition, free enterprise encouraged competition, structural adjustment shattered centralized economies
    - Loss of foreign support for single-party regimes
    - **State-nation** (state creates new identity) vs. **nation-state** (state based on shared identity) tension, traditional groups reasserted themselves
- Result: rise in internal, often ethnic, conflicts (1990s and 2000s)
- Meanwhile **interstate war has become rare**, even in deep-seated cases (India-Pakistan, Korea, Russia-Ukraine etc.)
    - Nuclear MAD-style stalemates froze them
- Internal (asymmetric) conflicts challenge the assumption of formal equality and legitimacy
    - State is the stronger, legitimate sovereign
    - Rebels fight a _triple struggle_: attention, power, legitimacy, with violence as their only currency
    - **Commitment** is their equalizing power
    - Hard to move from zero-sum to positive-sum
- Outcomes differ too
    - Interstate peace = resolve the issue, return to daily business
    - Internal settlement = new political system, social readjustment, rebuilding economy, ethnic inclusion, military integration
- Successes: Mozambique, South Africa (early 1990s), UN in El Salvador, Cambodia, Guatemala
- **Conflict trap** is the tendency for such outcomes not to last
    - Needs continuing third-party attention: monitoring, reconstruction, peace dividends
    - Problem is donor fatigue and distraction
    - Ex. Haiti, also Palestine, Congo, Kosovo, Senegal, Somalia
#### 6.3.2 New Norms: Self-Determination
- After WWI: **national self-determination** (new states based on major nationalities), limited to Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires
- After WWII: **state self-determination** (new state from a former administrative unit)
    - Locked in by _uti possidetis_ (“possession is nine-tenths of the law”), sanctified badly drawn colonial borders
- Held after Cold War: Yugoslavia, USSR, Eritrea, Somaliland, Southern Sudan
    - Ignores ethnic criteria, many groups crammed into majority-dominated states
    - Claims rejected: Albanians in Macedonia, Serbs in Kosovo, Armenians in Azerbaijan, Chiapas, Tamils, Casamance
- Creative middle path = **autonomy** short of independence (Zanzibar 1964, Mindanao, Aceh 2005)
#### 6.3.3 New Norms: Sovereignty and R2P
- **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)** is sovereignty defined as a state’s responsibility to care for its people, help other states do the same, and intervene if another state fails
    - Meant to replace Westphalian (1648) _sovereignty as protection of the state_
- Both doctrines are dangerous
    - Old one: state can do what it wants to its people, shielded from interference
    - R2P: stronger states can intervene against weaker states
- Limits and thresholds not worked out, risks have made R2P less prominent since the 2005 UN Summit
**Should R2P replace sovereignty as protection?**
- **Yes**
    - Shields people from their own state
    - Sovereignty comes with responsibility
    - Human security is the point of the state anyway
- **No**
    - Gives strong states an excuse to intervene in weak ones
    - Thresholds unclear (_who decides?_)
    - Libya 2011 shows mixed motives
**Key points (6.3)**
1. Globalization expands subjects of negotiation
2. Human security expands state security to the inhabitants
3. Internal conflicts challenge diplomacy (one state interfering in another’s internal matters)
4. Self-determination shifted from nation to existing state unit, avoids drawing new lines but overlooks divided nationalities
5. Conflict trap, conflict perpetuates itself by weakening the state
6. R2P reinterprets sovereignty, legitimizes interference
### 6.4 Challenging the Processes of Negotiation: Mediation and Multilateral Diplomacy
- Defining characteristics of negotiation (Iklé 1964)
    1. Unanimity as the decision rule
    2. Formal equality of parties
    3. Mixed motives (common and conflicting interests)
    4. Exchange of offers/demands with a three-way choice (yes, no, or continue talking)
- All under strain in globalized diplomacy
- Internal conflict pulls in outside states and NGOs
- **Mediation** is any type of third-party involvement
- Post-Cold War superpower withdrawal freed Third World countries to pursue their own conflicts, which is unsettling, so outsiders get drawn in
    - US: Sudan, Somalia, Philippines, Sahel (terrorism), Congo, Korea, Burma (stability)
    - Other UNSC members have wider concepts of interests too
    - UN-justified humanitarian interventions: Iraqi Kurdistan 1991, Somalia 1992, Libya 2011, South Sudan 2011
#### 6.4.1 Mediation
- States mediate in their _own interest_ (Zartman and Touval 2007)
    - Usually to end a disturbing conflict or improve relations with a party
- Parties can’t perceive a ripe moment alone, so need a third party
- Mediator doesn’t have to be **neutral/unbiased**, just a **trustworthy message carrier** encouraging a stable solution
    - If the mediator has ties to one party, assumed it’ll _deliver that party’s assent_
- **Entry** is hard in internal conflicts
    - State resists (interference, implies it can’t handle its own problems)
    - Mediation boosts the weaker rebel party, gives recognition and equal position (rebels’ prime goal)
- Mediator’s first challenge is **ripening**
    - If not possible, then **positioning**, be available when the stalemate is felt
    - Ex. UN mediators in Syria (Annan, Brahimi, de Mistura) failed, couldn’t convince parties the stalemate hurt them. ~400,000 dead by end of 2016
- Parties also need help with _process_
    - Rebels are badly organized, need training to negotiate
    - Rebel movements **fractionate** as talks succeed (splinters) or fail (flexibility vs. violence debate)
    - Ex. Liberia, Darfur
    - Ex. RENAMO knew what it fought _against_, not _for_. Also UNITA, FARC, ELN, LTTE, Hamas, Polisario
- **Three types of mediator**, depending on the obstacle
    1. **Communicator/facilitator**: parties can’t hear or get messages to each other, mediator is a “clear telephone line”
        - Ex. Vatican in Beagle Channel (Argentina/Chile)
    2. **Formulator**: parties can’t think of a way out of opposing demands, mediator injects ideas and persuades
        - Ex. US in Middle East peace process 1974-91
    3. **Manipulator**: not enough payoffs, or not enough equality of strength
        - Ex. US aid promise to Egypt and Israel 1975 (second Sinai disengagement)
        - Raises cost of stalemate or gain of agreement
        - Most involvement, _most dangerous_ for the mediator
**Does a mediator have to be neutral?**
- **Yes**
    - Trust in the process
    - Matters in labour relations and domestic counselling
- **No** (Zartman’s position)
    - Intl mediators have own interests
    - Only need to be trustworthy
    - Ties to a party can help deliver their assent
#### 6.4.2 Multilateral Diplomacy
- Expanded hugely since WWII and the creation of the UN
- States negotiate **regimes** to resolve common problems and reduce transaction costs
- **Regimes** are rules, regulations, norms, principles governing expectations in specific issue areas
    - Universal: Law of the Sea (12 years of negotiating), Kyoto Protocol (1997)
    - Limited: OPEC oil pricing (only half the producers but sets the referent for all)
    - With an org (OPEC) or just norms, no secretariat (Law of the Sea, Kyoto)
    - Soft law (Kyoto guidelines, UN Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement) or political decisions (OPEC)
- Regimes aren’t hard law (comply/infraction), they’re **recursive negotiations** that correct course
    - Because of shifting power and interests, new info, interest groups, domestic implementation, changes in the problem itself
- Challenge of multilateral negotiation: manage multiple parties, issues, and roles to reach consensus
- Main tool is **coalitions** (of parties and issues)
    - Package, pair, or link issues to simplify decisions
    - Types: coalitions of the whole, minimum winning, blocking, single issue
    - Negotiators also mediate between or generate coalitions
- Regimes tied to redefining interests, **narrow** to **enlightened**
    - **Narrow national interests** are unilateral concern for independence, territorial integrity, way of life and standard of living (Morgenthau 1948). Linked to realism and “sovereignty as protection”
    - **Enlightened interests** are maintaining collective agreements and reciprocal security. Liberal/institutionalist, rests on **reciprocity** (rewarding a concession with a concession)
    - Security becomes a collective responsibility states negotiate
- Ex. Hill (2005) on Six-Party Talks: convince North Korea security is better assured by joining the intl community than by unilateral measures (nukes)
- _Matches the narrow vs. enlightened interests language in the scenario handout_
**Key points (6.4)**
1. Negotiation processes challenged by demands for mediation and multilateral diplomacy
2. Mediation = peaceful third-party involvement, necessary esp. in internal conflicts
3. States mediate for direct national _and_ enlightened/humanitarian interests
4. Mediator types: communicators, formulators, manipulators
5. Multilateral negotiation = many states, coalitions
6. Regimes = rules/norms made by recursive multilateral negotiation for problems no single state can handle
### 6.5 Facing the Future of Diplomatic Negotiation: Prevention
- Normal diplomacy resolves _and_ prevents conflicts, testified by the conflicts that never happened
- Preventive cases: Macedonia (1992, 2001, models), Russia in Nagorno-Karabakh 1994 (still holds), post-USSR cases (Baltics, Ukraine, Tatarstan, Tajikistan)
- **Prevention** is keeping conflict from escalating into violence, not eliminating conflict itself
    - Conflict of positions is normal, it’s the _ground for negotiation_
    - Conflict through violence is the _object of prevention_
- Aim of diplomacy: **attend** (before violence), **fend off** (violent stage), **mend** (after de-escalation)
- Terms
    - **Conflict management** is keeping conflict from escalating beyond the political level
    - **Conflict resolution** is removing the incompatibilities
    - **Conflict prevention** addresses both violent means and incompatible ends
- History: Hammarskjold pushed it, Boutros-Ghali revived it
- ICISS 2001: “prevention is the single most important dimension” of R2P
    - Structural/root-cause vs. direct/operational prevention
    - Adopted at 2005 World Summit, elaborated by Ban Ki-Moon
- Since then, prevention has run aground like R2P and human security
    - **Practical**: UN peacekeeping (“Chapter VI.5”) only keeps an _agreed_ peace. Congo stretched it, Congo-Brazzaville 1997 dodged it
    - **Conceptual**: preventing escalation = telling sovereign states not to pursue conflict with enemies
        - Targets resent it, governments demonize their enemies
        - Even more controversial for conflicts merely on the horizon
        - Big gap between structural prevention (dev aid vs. poverty) and operational prevention (relief to oppressed minorities)
**Key points (6.5)**
1. Prevention is the _hardest_ challenge since pressure of ripeness is weakest and efforts are most intrusive
2. Still, normal diplomacy and intense ad hoc efforts have kept violence exceptional
### 6.6 Conclusion
- 20th c.: great power concerns extended beyond their neighbourhood
- Now: complex global system, no clear structure to preserve world order
- Responsibility is spread around, small states play big roles, internal conflict outpaces interstate, immature norms (R2P) can’t yet replace old practice
- Regimes rest on **unenforceable, fragile consensus**
- Zartman’s globalization example: al-Qaeda as a multinational corporation (secret mobile HQ, diaspora/criminal funding, IT), followed by Islamic State
- Diplomatic negotiation’s big advantage is **flexibility**, a match for the system’s complexity
- Concepts are a _springboard for creativity_, not rigid restraints
- Normal diplomacy and diplomacy as usual both have constraints that hinder track-one and block track-two intersection
- Bottom line: managed conflicts, prevented conflicts, and negotiation make violent conflict the _exception_


### 1. Interests in the scenario

**Country A**

- **Economic:** keeping cheap access to Country B’s rare earth minerals, keeping Country B as a market for its oil and gas, protecting its resource exports and manufacturing base
- **Military:** keeping access to Country B’s strategic ports, keeping freedom of action (“unhampered options”), not being encircled by a rival alliance
- **Social/political:** status as a major power, being included in global decision-making, regime stability and elite wealth

**Country B**

- **Economic:** ag exports reaching new allies on time, diversifying energy imports, getting fair value for its rare earths, growing its tech sector in 10 to 15 years
- **Military:** sovereignty and territorial security, security guarantees, keeping its alliance partnerships and base arrangements
- **Social/political:** consolidating its young democracy, being treated as an equal despite its size, expanding ties with other democracies

**a. What threatens those interests?**

- Country A sees Country B drifting toward democratic allies as encirclement, and the lapsed trade deal as a loss of resource access and leverage. Its sense of isolation and irrelevance after the last global crisis makes this worse.
- Country B sees Country A’s overstay at the ports, the border exercises, and the refusal of security guarantees as threats to its sovereignty and economy. Its dependence on Country A for energy and its exposed location add to that.

### 2. Common ground

- Both depend on trade, resource exports, and the ports (A for access and projection, B for shipping), so a stable and open trade relationship benefits both
- Both want to avoid a full war. Country A is a nuclear power but faces reputational and economic costs, and Country B would be devastated.
- Both prioritize national interests first, so deals framed around security and economic gain should land better than appeals to values
- Both have expressed willingness to cooperate (A with the international community, B with partners), so third-party mediation is acceptable to each
- Both have a shared interest in clear, enforceable agreements, since trust broke down over unclear or violated terms

### 3. What the conflict is really about

On the surface it is about the border skirmishes, the ports, and the lapsed trade deal. Underneath, I think it comes down to:

- **Security and trust.** Each side reads the other’s moves as aggression (A’s “administrative mistake” and B’s alliance ties and exercises), and neither will move first without guarantees.
- **Strategic alignment.** Country B is shifting from A’s sphere toward democratic allies, and Country A fears losing influence, buffer territory, and resources.
- **Status and recognition.** Country A wants to be treated as a major power, and Country B wants to be treated as a sovereign equal and not a buffer state.

It is a classic security dilemma wrapped in an economic dependency.

### 4. Negotiable issues

1. **Trade:** renewing or restructuring the rare earth agreement (pricing, volume, duration), and energy supply terms
2. **Port access:** clearer rules on duration, notification, and penalties, plus protections for agricultural shipping
3. **Military posture:** buffer zones at the border, notification or limits on exercises near the border, de-escalation and troop withdrawal
4. **Security guarantees:** what A would commit to regarding B’s sovereignty, and whether B’s allied bases and exercises could be limited or made more transparent
5. **Verification and dispute resolution:** monitors, third-party mediation, and hotlines
6. **Sequencing:** who de-escalates first, and what gets linked to what (for example, trade restoration in exchange for border withdrawal)
7. **Responsibility for the skirmishes:** a joint inquiry or face-saving language to handle casualties without assigning blame
