In the last two months, Canada has substantially emphasized its commitments to its nuclear energy policy. On April 4, 2025, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) approved the first of four small modular reactors (SMRs) to be constructed in Darlington, Ontario. Costing a total of 20.9 billion CAD, these reactors are part of a larger plan to increase Canadian nuclear energy production. Around 13% of Canada’s energy is produced by nuclear plants in Ontario and New Brunswick. Furthermore, the Darlington project is being touted as establishing Canada “as the first G7 nation to have an operational SMR,” (privy-council) and positioning us to take a global leadership position on the technology. Saskatchewan currently mines and mills nearly 15% of the world's annual Uranium supply, with 90% of this resource being exported (natural-resources).

Through this project, we can gain a much deeper understanding of the relationship between Provincial and Federal jurisdictions, and how they interact. In this scenario, provinces like Saskatchewan have significant leverage, due to their control of non-renewable natural resources (Malcomson, p. 30). Additional provincially-owned groups like Ontario Power Generation (OPG) are the ones who own and operate energy plants. However, Federal groups such as the CNSC and the Canada Growth Fund take part in regulating and funding these projects, respectively (Malcolmson, p. 174-175). Here, the importance of managing Provincial-Federal relations in a federal system of government becomes highly evident. If the priorities are in misalignment, then nothing can be effectively completed.

There are many broader political implications of the event. One of the primary purposes of the head of government is to be a champion for national issues, and lead the charge towards progress. Prime Minister Mark Carney has certainly been doing that in this case. The point of being the first in the G7 to building SMRs was brought up in his recent speech to the Economic Club of New York. Here, he emphasized how Canada must become stronger in its energy sector, both for national security and the benefit of other countries. This trend reveals the Canadian state of Collaborative federalism, in which the government is occasionally interested in collaborating with provinces (Malcolmson, p. 171-172). Nevertheless, not all provinces are bound to be excited for this unification. Especially Alberta and Quebec, who are more likely to see the Federal nuclear energy strategy as federal government overreach. 

  

Event #2: 2026 Spring Economic Update

  

Last month, Finance Minister Francois-Phillipe Champagne presented the Spring Economic Update in the House. The Liberal cabinet was proud of the fact that it came in $11.4 billion under budget, leading to an annual deficit of $66.9B CAD. This gap was largely due to an increase in oil prices, and unusually strong performance in the face of economic turmoil. Furthermore, the Liberal government announced the $25B CAD Canada Strong Fund – marketed as a sovereign wealth fund for Canadians. Canada’s debt-servicing makes up $54B of this deficit, and projections say that this cost is expected to raise by $25B over the next five years. This high deficit will continue to be run throughout this forecast.

There are several significant connections to be made to course content. First of all, in relation to the powers of the Prime Minister. It may be stated that the budget is the clearest consensus of what the Cabinet’s consensus is, since it is where funds are being directed (Malcolmson, p.78). Furthermore, ideological differences in where this direction should be pointed are highlighted by how different political parties reacted to this update. The Liberals clearly frame spending in a deficit to be necessary, as this allows advancements such as meeting its NATO 2% GDP defence target ([Canada.ca](http://canada.ca), 2026). Mr. Pollievre and the Conservatives have hammered that these deficits are double what Trudeau had planned for this time, nullifying the Liberal’s statement of “fiscal discipline” (CBC News, 2026). The PBO’s relatively independent analysis (p. 94) suggests that this trend in debt and its servicing costs is concerning, and states that the government’s capital budgeting definitions are insufficiently transparent (PBO, 2026). If the Liberal’s newly majority government and/or budget loses support, this could lead to a lack of confidence and serious consequences for the party (Malcolmson, p. 101).

Each of the three parties in opposition to the Liberals have been vocally critical of the budget. As discussed, the Conservatives are ever-worried of “real human costs” (CBC News, 2026). The NDP has an opposing perspective, arguing that the update was another “missed opportunity,” since the government failed to raise taxes on oil and gas, and dropped social programs (Avi, 2026). The Bloc was unhappy, since Quebecois industries like aluminum were unsuitably addressed in regards to tariffs (Gowling WLG, 2026). My primary concern regarding this announcement is the Canada Strong Fund. It strikes me as confusing that the government would be funding this via debt, rather than trying to balance the budget. Furthermore, the messaging which calls it a “sovereign wealth fund” is a bit of mis-marketing, as it does not retain the characteristics of funds like the Norwegian Government Pension Fund or Saudi’s Public Wealth Fund. These countries frequently run budget surpluses
