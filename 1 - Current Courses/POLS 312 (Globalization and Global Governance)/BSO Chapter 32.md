#AISummary 
### 32.1 Introduction
- Intros to human rights differ based on **who tells the story** (victim, state, conqueror, colonial power, nationalist)
- Dominant narrative emerged post-WWII
    - UDHR claims to prevent barbarism, but colonialism and slavery didn't trigger the same sense of responsibility
    - _Why do some harms get "human rights" attention and others don't?_ Political issue? Policy failing? Intrinsic?
- **Liberal internationalist** story has 3 central claims
    1. Universal
    2. Based on liberal individualism
    3. Progressive (**teleological**, i.e. defined by end purpose, more rights = more freedom and equality)
### 32.2 The global human rights structure
- UN Charter (26 June 1945) names human rights as key objective
    - Commission on Human Rights drafted UDHR, replaced by **Human Rights Council** in 2006
    - **UDHR** adopted 10 Dec 1948
- Rights are **indivisible, interdependent, universal**
    - **ICCPR**: legal protections vs. abuse by state, political participation (equality before law, no arbitrary arrest, speech, assembly, life)
    - **ICESCR**: access to education, food, housing, health care
    - Both in force 1976
- Later binding treaties on racial discrimination, gender discrimination, torture, enforced disappearances, children, disabilities, migrants, minorities, indigenous peoples
#### 32.2.1 Accountability
- 5 mechanisms
    1. **Treaty bodies** + reporting
        - States submit periodic reports, reviewed publicly by expert committees (Geneva)
        - **Optional protocols** = individual complaint mechanism, few states sign on
        - No enforcement, state complies as it sees fit
        - HRC **universal periodic review**: states review states, not independent experts
    2. **Special procedures**
        - Special rapporteurs, working groups, thematic and country mandates (45 thematic, 13 country as of Oct 2021)
        - Country visits, individual cases, structural issues (systemic racism, economic injustice)
    3. **ICC**
        - Genocide, war crimes, crimes vs. humanity
        - Narrow scope but has **judicial enforcement** powers
    4. **Regional mechanisms**
        - European Court of HR, Inter-American Court, African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights
    5. **NGOs and individuals**
        - Range from big international groups to grassroots orgs
        - Intl groups mostly Western (HRW, 1978, NYC; Amnesty, 1961, London)
        - Constrained by funding, donor agendas, North/South and East/West divides
#### 32.2.2 What are human rights?
- Mainstream textbooks: formal, doctrinal, apolitical, "common sense," mark of a civilized, democratic society
- Reality: mean different things to different ppl, have political effects, contradictory (Dembour)
- 4 schools of thought
    1. **Natural law**: natural rights, held by being human, independent of social recognition, **negative obligations** on states (ex. no torture), absolute
    2. **Political values** a society chooses to adopt
        - Aspire to universality but don't assume it
    3. **Protest/resistance** tools for the excluded (poor, oppressed)
        - Perpetual struggle, suspicious of elite bias
    4. **Critical/discourse**: exist because ppl talk about them
        - Not natural, not _the_ solution
        - Powerful language for claims vs. injustice, but also used for imperial, civilizing agendas (past) and neoliberal market goals (present)
- Takeaway: human rights are about more than law, **not necessarily progressive**
### 32.3 The core assumptions
- 3 assumptions of dominant liberal internationalist view
    1. Transformative and progressive
    2. Universal
    3. Universal, common **subject**
#### 32.3.1 Human rights and progress
- Post-WWII: states can't hide behind **sovereignty** anymore
- Belief history has purpose and direction (backward/uncivilized to modern/civilized)
- Evidence for: slavery ended, women have more rights, kids better protected
- Critiques
    - Empirically flawed: more violations in the 20th c. than ever
    - Theoretically flawed: the **"dark side"** of human rights (D. Kennedy)
        - Permeated by imperial ambition, moral/racial/civilizational superiority, religious evangelicalism
        - Ex. **2001 Afghanistan**
            - Started as self-defence, mutated into women's rights ("saving" women from the burqa)
            - Two decades on: loss of life, Taliban still there, women still wearing burqa
    - Narrow, formalistic, individual approach ignores structural and material causes
        - Ex. **South Africa TRC**
            - Narrow defs of "victim" and "political"
            - Bodily integrity rights only (not subsistence), individuals only (not groups)
            - Excluded apartheid laws and policies themselves, so didn't link violations to apartheid
#### 32.3.2 Universality
- Claim: all humans entitled w/o distinction, based on objectivity, neutrality, inclusion
- Used by violators (states) and by those contesting violations
- Critics: universality exposes **particularity, contingency, malleability**
    - Enlightenment liberty/equality coexisted with colonialism and slavery
    - Legit subject of law within Europe = white, Christian, propertied men
    - Ideals falter when they meet difference
- Today
    - Liberal democracies closing doors on, deporting, or locking up refugees
    - Colonial civilized/uncivilized binary, outsider = threat to social cohesion
    - Ex. **Rohingya** in Myanmar (UN calls it genocide), cast as infiltrators outside the dominant ethnic order
- Key point: the "dark side" is **integral**, not an irregularity fixed by more inclusion
#### 32.3.3 The subject of human rights
- Core subject = **sovereign, autonomous** individual, pre-social, ahistorical
    - UDHR language: "all," "everybody," "no one"
- Postcolonial/Global South critique: this subject can't exist w/o its **"Other"**
    - Access only to the extent you resemble the familiar subject
- 3 ways the Other is handled
##### 32.3.3.1 Assimilation
- Difference can be erased
- Colonial subjects got rights only after being trained into "civilizational maturity"
- Ex. **Islamic veil bans** in Europe: choose between the veil and your rights (education, public space)
- Also: new citizenship/nationality requirements in Europe, South Asia, Australia

> **Case Study 32.1 Islamic headscarf ban**
> 
> - _Leyla Sahin v. Turkey_, Istanbul Univ. circular banned headscarves
> - Court upheld ban: **secularism** + **gender equality**
> - Barely any room to argue the ban itself was sex discrimination
> - Didn't address her argument that she _chose_ it
> - Dissent (Judge Tulkens): paternalistic and contradictory
> - Reinforces assumption that Muslim women are always coerced, lack agency, need rescue
> - Q1: Do all veiled Muslim women = oppressed? Q2: Is the decision enabling or liberating?
##### 32.3.3.2 Essentializing the difference
- Women, Black ppl, gay ppl seen as **just different**, biologically/naturally, incapable of change
    - Used to justify denial of education, work, speech, political participation
    - Slavery: Black ppl denied subjectivity, treated as objects
- Stereotypes persist even after rights secured, often displaced onto **Global North/South** divide
- Ex. anti-violence-against-women and **anti-trafficking** campaigns
    - Assume Global South women are more victimized by "primitive" culture
    - Leads to protectionist laws, protective detention, carceral/criminal justice focus
    - Denies the "other" woman agency
##### 32.3.3.3 Incarcerating difference
- Other is outside of / undeserving of rights, seen as threat, backward, dangerous
- Ex. post-9/11 treatment of young Muslim men
    - "Enemy combatant" category, indefinite detention (**Guantanamo**), **rendition**
    - Justified as saving liberal democracy from "illiberal" societies (Ignatieff)
- Ex. opposition to queer rights even where decriminalized (France, US, India), seen as destroying civilization, family, faith
- Ex. refusing to rescue migrants from capsized boats

> **Case Study 32.2 Queer relationships, LGBTQ rights, and colonial laws**
> 
> - 2016: HRC voted to create UN **Independent Expert on SOGI**
> - Polarized globally
>     - Increased criminalization (Nigeria, Uganda, Kenya, Ethiopia, DRC, Russia)
>     - Legal recognition (Angola, Botswana, Gabon, Mozambique, Nepal, Cambodia, India, South Africa, Europe, US)
> - **Botswana** 2019: High Court struck down colonial-era Penal Code provision, unanimous
>     - Court noted British colonial origins
> - Persistent belief that homophobia = "less civilized" non-West
> - Counter: homophobia is a **Western import** via colonialism
>     - Ex. **SMUG** (Uganda) says the civilizational argument deflects from US Christian evangelicals' role in producing anti-gay agenda
> - Q1: Is homophobia cultural? Straight? Political? Q2: What strategies avoid the Global South/North opposition?

> **Key Points**
> 
> - HR mean different things (liberation tool _and_ tool to prevent threats to liberal democratic values)
> - 3 assumptions need critical interrogation
> - Techniques of inclusion/exclusion: assimilation, essentializing, treating as threat
> - "Dark side" may be **constitutive**, not an aberration
> - Meanings shift based on encounters w/ difference
### 32.4 Doing human rights advocacy
- Core question: how to advocate w/o doing more harm or reproducing stereotypes?
- Critical approach = fine line
    - Don't reject HR outright (plays into populist and nationalist hands)
    - Don't despair when HR fail to deliver full emancipation
- Rights = **radical tools** for those who never had them, flawed but better than none
- 1st question to ask: **who is accountable when HR interventions harm more than help?**
- 4 considerations
    1. Move **beyond universality** w/o retreating to the local or getting stuck in historical particularity
        - HR = site of power, so _who wields it_ matters
    2. Be thoughtful in designing interventions
        - Ex. **sex trafficking** laws focused on rescue/abolishing sex work
            - Sex workers' groups (Netherlands, Thailand, India): denies subjectivity, doesn't give rights, doesn't cut reliance on criminal networks
            - Not all sex workers trafficked, not all trafficking is sex work
            - Migrants: ignores causes of migration (no safe legal routes), drives dependence on smugglers
        - Better questions: are minimum HR criteria met? What are structural causes? More empowering options?
        - Alternative: foreground migrants' rights, safer routes, push UN migrant workers convention, less criminal law
    3. Include awareness of **colonial trappings** and Global North hegemony in HR materials/education
        - Learn from postcolonial, feminist, racial critiques instead of rescuing or patronizing "Others"
    4. Watch **who tells the story** of HR
        - Poor ppl = constituency w/ knowledge, not just objects of rights
        - HR can create **hierarchies of suffering**, get tied to market-friendly paradigms that worsen poverty and inequality
- Avoid false dichotomies
    - Religious freedom and gender equality don't have to oppose each other
    - Ex. **India**: women in religious minority communities renegotiate equality, secularism, religious freedom while challenging discrimination in their own communities

> **Opposing Opinions 32.1** States should be free to choose whether or not to let in irregular migrants
> 
> **Yes?**
> 
> - State's first obligation = own citizens, limited resources (jobs, education, health care)
> - Citizenship criteria = sovereign power of the nation-state
> - No obligation to admit those threatening **social cohesion**
> - Irregular migrants = benefits reliance, less tax revenue
> 
> **No?**
> 
> - States must uphold **freedom of mobility**, esp. rich ones
> - No safe legal routes = more vulnerability (smugglers, risk of death)
> - Forced migration caused partly by outside states (climate change, corporate environmental harm, arms sales, colonial wealth extraction)
> - Migration = global HR problem, not law and order problem
> - Vulnerability is **produced** by discriminatory laws, xenophobic nationalism
> 
> 1. Is the right to mobility an important human right?
> 2. Do states owe any obligation to humans beyond their own borders? Why? Why not?
> 3. Is 'social cohesion' a valid criterion for admitting migrants? Why? Why not?

> **Key Points**
> 
> - HR = political, used by diff groups for competing agendas
> - Interventions not always progressive or emancipatory
> - Rights can liberate _and_ confine
> - Need to be **historicized and contextualized**
> - Rights are complex, contradictory, contingent, so **no permanent victories**, constant monitoring and questioning
### 32.5 Conclusion
- Covered formal structure (treaties, conventions, committees, reporting) and the politics of HR
- HR shaped by legacies of colonialism, slavery, apartheid, and engagements w/ sexual, religious, racial difference
- Question progressive/universal/common subject claims, they can hide **power relations**
- HR can't be steered by good intentions alone, can lead to harmful effects
- Goal: more mindful, reflective advocacy and policy work in IR

4. Can you think of situations in your own context or work in which human rights have been part of the problem in terms of either inflicting or aggravating pain and suffering or reinforcing or advancing colonial, racial agendas that are themselves extremely troubling?

9. If human rights did not exist in the world, would anyone notice? Are we better off with human rights or without them—how and why?
