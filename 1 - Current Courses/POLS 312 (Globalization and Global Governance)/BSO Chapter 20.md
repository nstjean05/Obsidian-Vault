### 20.1 Introduction
- **IOs** = bodies of 3+ govts
    - ex. WHO attacked by US over Covid response, UN stuck on Syria, IMF revived after Great Recession, WTO gridlocked
- Each formal IO has constitution, permanent location, bureaucracy, staff, funding, logo
- Can be classified by:
    1. issue-area
    2. mandate
    3. geographical representation
    4. limited vs open membership
    5. forum for inter-state decisions vs relatively autonomous service org
- Chapter covers: defn, rise, why states cooperate formally, 4 theories (liberalism, realism, constructivism, Marxism)
- Problems: gridlock from power politics, _organized hypocrisy_ (gap between IO talk, decisions, actions)
### 20.2 What are international organizations?
- **IO** is “an organization that has representatives from three or more states supporting a permanent secretariat to perform on-going tasks related to a common purpose” (Barnett and Finnemore)
    - chapter = intergovernmental orgs only, NGOs are Ch. 22
- Membership seen as sovereign right, boosts recognition and legitimacy
    - ex. South Sudan = 193rd UN member (2011)
- 37 IOs in 1909 to 7,853 in 2021
- First modern IO = Central Commission for the Navigation of the Rhine (1815)
- Newest big one = China-led AIIB (57 members, open Jan 2016), both competitor and collaborator with World Bank
#### Ways to create an IO
1. Treaty, signed then ratified domestically, IO “born” once enough states ratify
2. **Emanation**, members of existing IO approve a spin-off
    - ex. UNCTAD from the UN
    - easier to create and easier to dissolve
    - 958 emanation vs 289 traditional (2021)
#### Hybrids
- **ILO** = tripartite (states, workers, employers), started as civil society idea (1919)
- **ISO** = technically an NGO, but members are national standards bodies (public or private)
    - standards affect up to 80% of world commodity trade
    - de facto standards for the WTO
    - _public-private, multi-actor governance_
#### Case Study 20.1: African Development Bank (AfDB)
- Created 1964 by 30 African states, modelled on World Bank, excluded the West at first (decolonial push for independence)
- 1970s oil crises led to debt crisis, reliance on World Bank/IMF structural adjustment loans, “drop the debt” campaign in the 1990s, then HIPC Initiative
- Borrowers not credit-worthy, so couldn’t borrow from own bank
- 1982 opened to non-regional members (now 80: 54 regional, 26 non-regional), which doubled lending funds
- US = 2nd largest member after Nigeria
- Still has “least Western hegemonic involvement”
    1. weighted voting relatively egalitarian until 2019
    2. donors sit in constituencies with regional states, diluting economic weight
    3. mixed constituencies and shifting voting coalitions
- Donors froze concessional funds in 1994, restructure done 2004 (biggest MDB reform ever), record capital injection 2019
- “High 5” agenda: light up and power Africa, feed, industrialize, integrate, improve quality of life
#### History of IOs
- Mid-1800s: European states create IOs to manage trade and tech from Industrial Revolution
    - preconditions (Claude): sovereign states, substantial contact, awareness of problems, agreement on need to regulate
    - earlier example = Hanseatic League
- **PIUs** (Public International Unions) = “apolitical” technical orgs
    - ex. International Telegraph Union (1865, now ITU), Universal Postal Union (1874)
    - set the template: permanent secretariat, conferences, smaller council between conferences
- **Multilateralism** is coordinating national policies in groups of 3+ states (Keohane)
- Concert of Europe (from Congress of Vienna 1815), ~30 meetings over the next century
    - still balance of power, imperialism, alliances
- Hague System (1899, 1907)
    - all recognized states invited, not just great powers
    - legal equality of states (but not for colonized peoples)
    - procedural stuff: elected chairs, committees, roll calls
    - Permanent Court of Arbitration
- League of Nations = first overtly political IO, response to failure of balance of power
    - balance of power politics also killed it
    - lessons shaped UN founders, ICJ created
    - ICC took 50+ years more
- Most of the world excluded, under colonial tutelage
- _Links back to Ch. 3 on 19th c. origins_
### 20.3 Why are international organizations important?
1. **They endure**
    - 15 of the 1865 PIUs lasted 100 years
    - avg post-1945 IO lasts only 15 to 20 years
    - big shifts kill IOs, ex. 25% of PIUs abolished after WWII, Warsaw Pact dissolved after cold war
2. **They shape how states respond to complex issues**
    - authority is _technical_ (IMF economists, WHO doctors) or _moral_
    - ex. UNHCR (1951) expanded who counts as a refugee, created IDP category despite state opposition
    - still dependent on states accepting proposals
3. **They affect everyday life**
    - supply **global public goods** (peace, stable economy, stable climate) as forums and through operations
    - reach non-members too, ex. only 44 states at Bretton Woods
    - increasingly intrusive on sovereignty (WHO travel warnings, IMF reform conditions, WTO protectionism rulings)
    - EU = most intrusive, 27 members
### 20.4 Why do states create IOs?
- **Informal multilateralism** = states meet as groups with no permanent structure
    - ex. G7, G20 (no treaty, secretariat, HQ, rotating headship)
    - benefits: fast decisions, flexibility, not bound by pledges, no domestic ratification (Lipson)
- “Never an absolute need for IOs” (L. Martin), yet states keep creating them
- Four theory approaches:
    1. **Liberalism** / neoliberal institutionalism: IOs provide collective goods, _absolute gains_
    2. **Realism** / neorealism: IOs as diplomatic tools, can hide overt interests, set rules others follow
    3. **Social constructivism**: how IOs take up and spread norms, when org culture blocks intended function
    4. **Marxism** / Gramscian: powerful states and elites use IOs to keep privileged position
**Is multilateralism in crisis?** (Opposing Opinions 20.1)
- Yes
    - shifts in balance of power mean more conflict, no consensus between hegemons and rising powers
    - retreat to nationalism and populism, ex. Brexit, Trump leaving UNHRC, UNESCO, UPU, challenging NATO, NAFTA, WTO, ICC
    - UN stuck in North/South deadlock
- No
    - negotiations ebb and flow, IOs keep working, changing them needs more cooperation
    - secretariats provide info, expertise, ways past logjams
    - populist govts don’t oppose all multilateralism, look at specific deadlocks
    - IOs still produce new ideas, ex. SDGs

#### Principal-agent (P-A) model
- Extends neoliberal institutionalism (Hawkins et al.)
- **Principals** = member states, give mandate, resources, location, staff
- **Agent** = IO, given autonomy to act on their behalf
- Risks:
    - **agency slack** = IO shirks
    - **slippage** = IO pursues its own preferred activities
    - more principals = more slack, harder to agree on what IO should do
- Used to study _performance gaps_
- Critique: assumes bureaucrats just want power, autonomy, resources, overlooks moral drivers (Park)
#### Social constructivism
- Looks at how **organizational culture** shapes which norms get adopted or rejected
- ex. IMF staff pushed capital account liberalization
    - not in Articles of Agreement, Keynes believed the opposite
    - comes from staff culture (neoclassical economists from elite US and UK schools)
    - held on despite 1997 Asian financial crisis and 2009 Great Recession
- ex. Interpol turned down a terrorism mandate in the 1960s
    - member states wanted it, more power and resources on offer
    - didn’t fit org culture, threatened autonomy
### 20.6 Conclusion
- IOs grew from mid-1800s, are a _specific form_ of multilateralism (permanent machinery vs informal “Gs”)
- Can be regional (AfDB, SCO) or universal (UN)
- Equal membership doesn’t mean equal power
    - UNGA = one state, one vote
    - World Bank and IMF = votes tied to material contributions
- Theory recap
    - early liberals: peace and prosperity for individuals
    - functionalists: bureaucratic cooperation spills up to high diplomacy
    - classical realists: IOs just tools
    - neorealism and neoliberal institutionalism: both say IOs useful under anarchy, disagree on who benefits more, when, how long
    - P-A and constructivism: what IO staff want vs member state interests
    - Marxist/Gramscian: IOs serve economic elites in global capitalism
- Key Points flag Marxist/Gramscian accounts need more empirical work tracing how elites shape IO policy
#### Case Study 20.2: Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)
- Current form 2001, members China, India, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Russia, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan
- Focus on “three evils”: terrorism, extremism, separatism
- **Contested multilateralism** (Morse and Keohane), do rising illiberal powers challenge the liberal order?
- Problems: underdeveloped, non-intervention principle, internal distrust, unclear direction
- Seen as threat to West (backs autocracy) but avoids anti-Western talk since some members need Western investment
