### Preface to the Third Edition
- **Negotiation revolution** is the shift from hierarchical decision-making to networks of negotiation
    - Old view: people at top of the pyramid decide, people at bottom follow orders
    - Now: flatter orgs, faster innovation, Internet, so we rely on many people we don’t control
    - Can’t just give orders (even to employees or kids), so we have to negotiate
- “Like it or not, you are a negotiator” used to be an eye opener, now an acknowledged reality
- Shifts over 30 yrs
    - “Negotiation” used to mean labour talks, closing a sale, intl diplomacy. Now we see it informally everywhere
    - Used to be adversarial (_who wins, who loses?_), now **win-win** is recognized
    - Even w/o win-win, a _wise agreement_ can beat the alternative
    - Used to be few courses, now core competence (law, business, govt schools, even primary/high schools)

#### The Work Ahead

- Still a huge need for negotiation based on **mutual gains** and **legitimate standards**
- Negotiation revolution brought _more_ conflict, not less
    - Hierarchies bottle up conflict, networks let it out
    - Democracies surface conflict, why they seem quarrelsome vs. authoritarian societies
- Goal shouldn’t be to _eliminate_ conflict
    - Conflict is inevitable and useful (change, insight, addresses injustice, business competition, democratic process)
    - _The world needs more conflict, not less_
- Challenge is to **transform** conflict, from destructive adversarial battling to hard-headed, **side-by-side problem-solving**
- “First human family reunion”: communications revolution put all ~15,000 language communities in touch, with dissension and resentment of inequities
- Need to “change the basic game of conflict”

#### Ten Questions (listed in the front matter)

1. _Fairness and principled negotiation_
    1. Does positional bargaining ever make sense?
    2. What if the other side believes in a different standard of fairness?
    3. Should I be fair if I don’t have to be?
2. _Dealing with people_  
    4. What do I do if the people are the problem?  
    5. Should I negotiate even with terrorists or someone like Hitler? When does it make sense not to negotiate?  
    6. How should I adjust my approach for differences of personality, gender, culture, etc.?
3. _Tactics_  
    7. Where to meet, how to communicate, who makes the first offer, how high to start?  
    8. How do I move from inventing options to making commitments?  
    9. How do I try out these ideas without taking too much risk?
4. _Power_  
    10. Can the way I negotiate make a difference if the other side is more powerful? How do I enhance my negotiating power?

### Introduction

- Opening line: “Like it or not, you are a negotiator”
- Negotiation is a fact of life, everyone negotiates daily
    - Ex. raise with boss, house price, lawsuit settlement, oil co. joint venture, transit strike, US/Russia arms control
    - Like Molière’s Monsieur Jourdain speaking prose all his life without knowing it
- **Negotiation** is back-and-forth communication designed to reach an agreement when you and the other side have some interests shared, some opposed, some just different
- _Conflict is a growth industry_
    - Everyone wants a say, fewer accept dictated decisions
    - Even court cases usually settle
- Standard strategies leave people dissatisfied, worn out, or alienated (often all three)
- The dilemma: **soft** or **hard**
    - **Soft negotiator** avoids conflict, concedes easily, ends up exploited and bitter
    - **Hard negotiator** sees a contest of wills, takes extreme positions, ends up with an equally hard response, exhausted, relationship harmed
- The third way: **principled negotiation** (Harvard Negotiation Project)
    - Decide issues on their **merits**, not by haggling over what each side will/won’t do
    - Look for mutual gains
    - Where interests conflict, insist on **fair standards independent of the will of either side**
    - **Hard on the merits, soft on the people**
    - No tricks, no posturing. Lets you be fair while protecting you from those who’d exploit that
- Book layout
    - Ch. 1 problems of positional bargaining
    - Next four chapters = the four principles
    - Last three = what if the other side is more powerful, won’t play along, or uses dirty tricks
- Works for diplomats, bankers, couples, hostage negotiators
    - One issue or many, two parties or many, ritual (collective bargaining) or free-for-all (hijackers)
    - If the other side learns it, it gets _easier_ for you. If they read the book, all the better

### 1. Don’t Bargain Over Positions

- **Positional bargaining** is each side taking a position, arguing for it, and making concessions to reach a compromise
    - Ex. the brass dish haggling (customer/shopkeeper)
- Three criteria for judging any method
    1. Produces a **wise agreement** if one is possible
    2. Is **efficient**
    3. Improves or at least doesn’t damage the **relationship**
- **Wise agreement** is one that meets each side’s legitimate interests to the extent possible, resolves conflicting interests fairly, is durable, and takes community interests into account
- Positions do serve some purposes (tell the other side what you want, give an anchor, can eventually produce terms) but those can be served other ways
- Positional bargaining fails all three criteria

#### 1.1 Arguing Over Positions Produces Unwise Outcomes

- Negotiators lock into positions
    - The more you defend it, the more committed you get
    - Ego becomes identified with position, new interest in **saving face**
- Ex. **1961 nuclear test ban talks** (Kennedy)
    - Q: how many on-site inspections per year?
    - USSR agreed to 3, US insisted on 10, talks broke down
    - Nobody had defined an “inspection” (one person for a day or 100 people for a month?)
    - Never designed a procedure reconciling US verification interest with both sides’ desire for minimal intrusion
- Ex. **Iraq farmers vs. national oil co.**
    - Oil found under leased farmland, oil co. said “get off our land,” farmers said “we’re not leaving,” escalated to army threats
    - Official from a training program asked about _underlying concerns_
        - Oil co.: no production for ~3 yrs, only mapping and seismic surveying in the meantime
        - Farmers: harvest in 6 weeks, “everything we own”
    - Solution: farmers harvest, don’t impede prep work, oil co. may hire them later, can keep planting between derricks
- More attention on positions = less on **underlying concerns**
    - Agreement less likely, or just a mechanical split-the-difference

#### 1.2 Arguing Over Positions Is Inefficient

- Takes a lot of time (breakdown or agreement either way)
- Incentives stall settlement
    - Start extreme, hold stubbornly, deceive about true views, small concessions only as needed
    - More extreme opening + smaller concessions = more time to find out if agreement is possible
- Lots of individual decisions (offer, reject, how much to concede)
    - Each decision = yielding + pressure to yield more, so little incentive to move fast
    - Tactics: dragging feet, threatening to walk out, stonewalling

#### 1.3 Arguing Over Positions Endangers an Ongoing Relationship

- Becomes a **contest of will**
    - Ex. “If you want to go to the movies with me, it’s _Avatar_ or nothing”
- Anger and resentment, relationships strained or shattered (business partners, neighbours, lifelong bitterness)

#### 1.4 When There Are Many Parties, Positional Bargaining Is Even Worse

- Almost every negotiation involves more than two (constituents, higher-ups, boards)
- Ex. 150 countries at a UN conference, positional bargaining _next to impossible_
    - Takes all to say yes, only one to say no
    - Who do you make a concession to?
- Leads to **coalitions** with shared interests more symbolic than substantive (North/South, East/West)
    - Harder to develop a common position, then harder to change it
- Same for absent higher authorities who must approve
- _Ties to Zartman ch. 6 on coalitions in multilateral diplomacy_

#### 1.5 Being Nice Is No Answer

- **Soft positional bargaining**: friends, concede, trust, yield to avoid confrontation
    - Efficient, agreement likely, but may not be wise
    - Ex. O. Henry’s story (wife sells hair for watch chain, husband sells watch for hair combs)
    - Risk: sloppy agreement, relationship-focused
- **In positional bargaining, a hard game dominates a soft one**
    - Soft response to hard bargaining = “you will probably lose your shirt”

**Soft vs. hard positional bargaining (table)**

- **Soft**
    1. Participants are friends
    2. Goal is agreement
    3. Concede to cultivate the relationship
    4. Soft on people and problem
    5. Trust others
    6. Change position easily
    7. Make offers
    8. Disclose bottom line
    9. Accept one-sided losses
    10. Search for the single answer _they_ will accept
    11. Insist on agreement
    12. Try to avoid a contest of will
    13. Yield to pressure
- **Hard**
    1. Participants are adversaries
    2. Goal is victory
    3. Demand concessions as a condition of the relationship
    4. Hard on problem and people
    5. Distrust others
    6. Dig in
    7. Make threats
    8. Mislead about bottom line
    9. Demand one-sided gains
    10. Search for the single answer _you_ will accept
    11. Insist on your position
    12. Try to win a contest of will
    13. Apply pressure

#### 1.6 There Is an Alternative: Change the Game

- Negotiation happens at **two levels**
    - Substance (salary, lease, price)
    - Procedure for dealing with substance (soft, hard, or other)
    - The second is a **meta-game** (a game about a game)
- Every move helps set the rules, even substantive ones
    - Usually unnoticed, except across cultures
- Answer to soft vs. hard is **“neither,” change the game**

**The four points of principled negotiation (negotiation on the merits)**

1. **People**: separate the people from the problem
    - Humans aren’t computers: strong emotions, different perceptions, communication trouble
    - Emotions get tangled with the merits, positions make it worse
    - Concessions “for the relationship” reward stubbornness and breed resentment
    - Work **side by side**, attacking the problem, not each other
2. **Interests**: focus on interests, not positions
    - Position often obscures what you really want
    - Compromising between positions won’t meet the human needs behind them
3. **Options**: invent multiple options for mutual gain before deciding
    - Deciding under pressure narrows vision, high stakes inhibit creativity, searching for _the_ one answer too
    - Set aside designated time to brainstorm
4. **Criteria**: insist on objective criteria
    - Stubbornness rewards intransigence and gives arbitrary results
    - Insist the agreement reflect a fair standard independent of either side’s will (market value, expert opinion, custom, law)
    - Neither side gives in to the other, both defer to a fair solution

**Principled column of the table**

- Participants are **problem-solvers**
- Goal is a **wise outcome reached efficiently and amicably**
- Be soft on people, hard on problem
- Proceed _independent of trust_
- Explore interests, avoid having a bottom line
- Develop multiple options, decide later
- Insist on objective criteria
- Reason and be open to reason, **yield to principle, not pressure**

**Three stages of negotiation** (all four points apply throughout)

1. **Analysis**: diagnose the situation, gather and organize info
    - Note people problems (partisan perceptions, hostile emotions, unclear comms)
    - Identify your interests and theirs
    - Note options already on the table and criteria already suggested
2. **Planning**: deal with the four elements again, generate ideas and decide
    - How to handle people problems? Which interests matter most? Realistic objectives?
    - Generate more options and criteria
3. **Discussion**: communicate back and forth
    - Acknowledge perception differences, frustration, anger, comms problems
    - Understand each other’s interests
    - Jointly generate options, seek agreement on objective standards

- Result: wise agreement, gradual consensus, no **transactional costs** of digging into positions and then out of them
- Separating people from problem = deal with the other negotiator as a human regardless of substantive differences
