3. Suppose a government were to tax information access (so much per byte of information or word of text transferred on the net). What effect would this have on the progress of the information society? Consider both economic and control implications.

Let us imagine a significant region of the world in which the government had complete control over the internet, and thereby all information on it. There would be many positive effects of this. For people coming from the current internet, as known via search engines like Google, this could be a fantastic adjustment. The recently dubbed trend of “encrappification” has taken a wide variety of modern business by storm, in which a company with sufficient audience capture degrades its product. Websites are a prime example of this, as they struggle to stay afloat by pushing worse content, hidden behind an increasing facade of advertisements. One growing solution to this is that of paid browsers. Like paying for a service like YouTube Premium, ads are eliminated, and the service pays sites instead. A government would be able to do this at scale, simultaneously lowering costs (from operating at scale) and increasing utility. Ideally, this government would not exhibit the traits of a traditional monopoly, since it is accountable to the people. We have seen how other public information databases, like the National Center for Biotechnology Information, have been massively influential in scientific democratization.

Economic implications are certainly vast for this adjustment. Aforementioned impacts on advertising would be significant, especially given the huge scale at which ads have transitioned to the digital landscape. If this information includes entertainment media, companies would either have to return to traditional advertising models, or cooperate with the government in a greater capacity. However, these losses would be more than made up by the greater ease of access of information. Consider the raw societal time that would be saved by an all-encompassing, streamlined search engine! With newfound funding, new APIs and standards could be made to further interconnect webpages, databases, legislation, education systems, and other institutions. At the scale of all a country’s information, massive funds would be raised, allowing the government to invest even more into social issues. There are, however, potential drawbacks to taxing information. It would be another cost heaped on individuals, being especially impactful on low-income families. Those without education may be less economically well-off, and accessing the information required would be a cost that some would not be able to afford. Government programs could adjust for this, just as taxes are adjusted by income, but government objectives often fall short with people slipping through cracks.

The greatest concerns about this program would likely be in the realm of control. George Orwell famously described a society in which all information was government controlled, and he didn’t paint a pretty picture. Many people don’t trust the current governments to do the moral thing, and how can a government with even more power be trusted? If the people’s only method of getting information and holding the government accountable is through the government, then it creates a strong incentive for government officials to manipulate information. There are other disincentives as well. One can imagine that underground collections of information would expand, as happens in any market suppressed by the government. From a more authoritarian perspective, the government would also have the ability to control the quality of information like never before. This could be used for good, as strong centralized fact-checking could be made prevalent. Many new jobs would be created to help sustain the monolithic government enterprise, and said government would be able to do much good. All in all, there are many advantages and drawbacks to this system, some of which we are already seeing. In the future, hopefully we can make a transition to a hybrid system - one in which information can be readily accessible, but where major players can compete to have the best product.

As Europe transitions into the 5th stage of demographic transition, their place in the future economy is changing distinctively. (b) What do you see as the probable business, trade, and general economic consequences of Brexit for England, Ireland, Northern Ireland, and the EU?

There have been significant impacts of Brexit. Most notably, the U.K. has not reached their potential economic output, as smaller firms have struggled with the increased cost of entry. There is a great deal more red tape to enter EU markets. While the U.K. has been able to broker independent trade deals, this hasn’t been taken full advantage of. The additional deals add up to a maximum of +0.38% of UK GDP, whereas Brexit lowered the GDP by 4%. There are a number of other critical places where there have been impacts, such as immigration, freedom to travel, legal sovereignty, and EU membership fees. While these were all major talking points leading up to Brexit, there is not a lot of evidence that the U.K. has been able to sufficiently capitalize on their newfound independence.

Smaller government entities, like England and Northern Ireland, also paid a price. Northern Ireland faced a “Brexit trilemma,” three objectives of which only two could be chosen: leave the EU customs union and single market, have no hard border on the island of Ireland, or have no checks between Northern Ireland and the UK. The Northern Ireland protocol resolved this, giving its namesake a unique EU-UK dual membership. Trade between Ireland and Northern Ireland jumped dramatically, as Ireland imported 80% more from its Northern neighbour. Furthermore, NI’s Irish exports to the UK doubled, as it works a favourable trade position. England saw many of the same effects as the UK, given that most of the UK’s economic output is centered there. It has been noted that while economists' initial predictions of costing 4% GDP were shown to be correct in the first 5 years, yet that estimate has underestimated the long-term impacts. The largest share of impacts was on investments at minus 12-18%, much of which was centered on England, and especially London.

Outside of the UK, Ireland and the EU also had major impacts. Ireland imports from the UK fell by 49%, while keeping exports the same, leading to a surplus since the UK only dropped imports by 15%. One major loss was that of fisheries, as Irish fishermen lost access to 1/6th of their fishing grounds that were in UK waters. This is one of many sustained impacts as two close economies decouple. To ease this process, Ireland received 1B Euros in compensation from the EU, as part of its Brexit adjustment reserve. The EU also received extensive impacts. As the second largest EU member,  EU revenues were short by 2.4T Euros (balancing out to around -5% of EU potential GDP), and the population dropped by 13%. There were also large political changes, as the balance of power shifted more in favour of other major EU powers like France and Germany. This was noticed in the Council of the European Union as a weakening of the liberal block, who had prominently advocated for an economically liberal Europe.

One final note to make is that there is considerable support within the UK for potentially rejoining the EU. As of October 2026, more Brits support than oppose rejoining. Many view it as having been a hasty decision, and having placed the UK in an even more troubled position. It would certainly be a challenging and extended process, but with current global instability, it is not completely out of the question.

  

29. A chemical company makes a herbicide that is particularly effective on a plant from which a black-market drug is derived. The government orders a large quantity of the herbicide intending to spray it indiscriminately over remote areas of several South American nations, with their governments’ cooperation, in an attempt to reduce trafficking in the drug. You, as president of the company, are concerned that there may eventually be damaging lawsuits against your firm if this program goes ahead. Should you place the potential interests of your shareholders first and refuse to sell the chemical, or should you place their immediate interests in maximizing profit and the declared interests of the government first? What do you do, and why?

This question is phrased in an interesting way, placing shareholders' interests on the side of refusing sale. I would suggest that the shareholders and government are absolutely in alignment - even if this is unethical, many modern companies are relatively protected by the government in unethical operations. Surely the shareholders would be on the side of a government contract, which would increase company revenue. In the long-term there may be negative impacts on the company from lawsuits, but most modern investors are focused on the near term. Additionally, the government is the one using the product, so they would probably take much of the fall. However, I don’t think that this alignment means that it is ethical to spray indiscriminately. From DuPont’s cattle incidents in the 1990s to Roundup being thought to cause cancer (and banned in some places in the 2010s), there are many examples of chemicals being used by companies for a profit, in the face of them being bad for humans. Without highly extensive testing, spraying a herbicide over even remote areas could lead to major maleffects to humans. Aside from the potential impacts on native peoples in rural regions, there are a multitude of potentially risky knock-on consequences. Perhaps a better option would be to accept the shipment, but add additional terms. The new herbicide is probably still a trade secret, giving the company major bargaining power in the government. Limits on where the herbicide could be applied, perhaps only to be used in the highly specific locations where farming is occurring. Even small scale dispersion can have major impacts, where local peoples likely don’t have a voice. As the president, I would go to every reasonable end to make sure that the herbicide has either hyper-targeted use, or is not used at all.

  

44. Pick one of the characters in the opening debate and argue either for or against her or his views on business-related matters, OR choose a topic they do not mention there, and extend their dialogue to cover that topic from their various viewpoints in a fashion consistent with their character.

“Nellie: Right. Non-disclosure is a name for lying about your business deals to gain an advantage.”

This statement from the opening debate raised my eyebrows. I think Nellie is really making a straw-man statement here - one where she portrays the side in the weakest way possible. Non-disclosure, and non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) are one of the most important tools for businesses to mitigate risk and protect themselves. While not always used for good, they play a critical part in the modern world. Take a company like Apple, for instance. A sufficiently competent engineer who works for them could probably hop to another company, and that company would pay him or her handsomely for the technical details they know from working at Apple. Imagine if Samsung, for the price of an annual salary, could know Apple’s plans for the next several generations of iPhone! This would be a huge advantage, but probably an unethical one. NDAs allow Apple to deter individuals from stealing their information and give it to competitors. There are many other uses for NDAs; if a government is monitoring a merger or acquisition, then it is crucial that no parties in the proceedings can leak to markets, without consequences. However, as Ellen and Nellie mentioned, there are many unethical uses of non-disclosure. It is well known that many government officials disclose what they shouldn’t - how else could Nancy Pelosi have reached 16,930% on her stock investments during her congressional tenure? She’s made more than double Warren Buffett’s returns, during a time the S&P 500 increased by only 2,300%. Furthermore, NDAs can be used to trap people in positions, making it hard to whistleblow without major punishments. Non-disclosure is certainly a two-sided coin, and it’s important to acknowledge both sides.
