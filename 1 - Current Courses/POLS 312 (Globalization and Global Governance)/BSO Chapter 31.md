### 31.1 Introduction
- Standard view of nationalism, nation-states, and global politics
    1. Westphalian system of sovereign territorial states develops in Europe from mid-17th c. (see Ch. 3)
    2. **Nationalism** turns monarchies into nation-states, spreads worldwide, so IR = relations between nation-states
    3. Globalization threatens this order (erodes sovereignty and identity)
- Chapter tests propositions 2 and 3, but first defines key concepts
### 31.2 Nationalism, nation-states, and global politics
#### 31.2.1 Definitions
- **Nationalism** is the idea that membership in a nation should be the overriding focus of political identity and loyalty, justifying **NSD** (national self-determination)
    - nation usually = “whole society” on a specific territory
    - same society can be claimed by competing nationalists
        - ex. Turkish nationalists claim Kurds in Turkey, Kurdish nationalists reject this
- Defining _nation_
    1. “objective” criteria (language, common descent)
    2. subjective, imagined
    3. sceptical of any definition
    - Box 31.2: Anderson (imagined political community, limited and sovereign), Hroch (3 irreplaceable ties: memory of common past, density of linguistic/cultural ties, equality of members), Hobsbawm (agnostic, no a priori definition)
- Nationalism studied as **ideology**, **politics**, **sentiments**
    - significant, not necessarily intellectually coherent
##### Types
- **Civic nationalism** is commitment to a state and its values
    - state membership determines nationality, ex. US
- **Ethnic nationalism** is commitment to people of (imagined) common descent
    - nation precedes state, ex. ethno-national states in Europe after 1918
- Problems: culture changes fast, religion and language fit neither box, distinction often _moral_ (civic good, ethnic bad) not analytical
    - still useful for analyzing justifications for NSD
- Other distinctions
    1. elite vs popular
    2. **state-supporting** (further nationalize the nation-state, “purify” internally or reclaim territory externally)
    3. **state-opposing** (seeks new state by separation and/or unification)
- Combos matter, ex. elite + civic claims to secede ≠ popular + ethnic claims to further nationalize existing state
##### Origins debate (Table 31.1)
- 4 questions
    1. Does nationalism need prior nations?
    2. Are nations modern or old?
    3. Culture, economy, or politics?
    4. Internal factors vs external factors (threats or support from powerful states)?
- **Modernism**: nationalism tied to modernization
    - Gellner (industry), Anderson (communication/print capitalism), Kedourie (intellectuals), Brass, Tilly, Mann (elite politics, modern states, warfare)
- Critics: long-lasting identities are necessary
    - **Primordialism** (socio-biological or sociological): van der Berghe, Connor
    - **Perennialism** (pre-modern nationalism): Hastings
    - **Ethno-symbolism** (myths and symbols over generations): Smith
##### “Nation-state”
- Term is unclear, UN = “Divided States” would be more accurate
- Not worth counting nation-states (fuzzy criteria, flawed data, implies accepting nationalist claims)
- Chapter treats as **national states** those that
    1. claim to be national (however defined)
    2. aren’t challenged by powerful state-opposing nationalism
    3. are recognized internationally
- Box 31.1: rough # of nation-states
    - 1500 = 2, 1800 = 6, 1900 = 30, 1923 = 45 (League), 1945 = 51 (UN), 1960 = 99, 1970 = 127, 2006 = 192, 2021 = 193
#### 31.2.2 A brief history of nationalism in global politics
- Nationalism as popular politics and sentiment matters after ~1750
##### Pre-1914
- **Seven Years’ War** (1756-63) = arguably first “world war”, Britain vs France (Europe, Asia, Americas)
    - Europeans justified power via superior culture, religion, sometimes race
    - early nationalism = state-supporting, civic, elite
    - civic nation linked to expanding middle class shaped by commercial globalization
    - French Revolution declared the nation the source of sovereignty (echoes US Declaration of Independence)
    - rulers answered with nationalist rhetoric too
- Nationalism stayed a _minority idea_ for a long time
    - Americas: creole elites used popular sovereignty, not national identity
- After Napoleon, Britain = major power
    - naval supremacy, informal collaboration with local rulers
    - proclaimed separation of coercive and economic power (free trade, gold standard)
    - tied to industrialization (telegraph, steam), mass migration
- British power depended on rivals being weak and divided
    - 1860s wars won by modernizing, nationalizing states
    - tech-power link led to state intervention, imperialist conflict over overseas resources
- Early nationalists imitated French/British civic forms
    - “historic” nations demanded “non-historic” ones assimilate
    - this stimulated _counter-nationalisms_ (folk culture, popular religion, spoken language)
- British indirect power outside Europe gave little stimulus to nationalism
- Contradictions of British-led globalization produced new nationalisms
    - imperialist conflict = popular state-supporting nationalism in challenger states
    - racial supremacy replaced civilizational and religious claims (also used in Europe, ex. modern anti-Semitism)
    - tighter direct imperial administration stimulated counter-nationalism
- Challenger states (Germany, Japan) = bureaucracies, militaries, state intervention (mass education, tariffs, subsidies)
    - “As political conflict globalized, it nationalized”
    - contradiction: universalist justifications of empire vs real subordination
    - counter-nationalism often regional (Pan-Africanism, Pan-Asianism, Pan-Arabism, Pan-Slavism)
- Ethnic, state-opposing nationalism had limited success vs declining multinational states
    - Balkans: Russian support against Ottomans mattered more than movement strength
- Pre-1914 nationalism mattered but was **unbalanced**
    - imperial states dominated, strongest nationalisms at the core of empires
    - empires couldn’t accept NSD (threatened their existence)
    - no international body or law for creating or recognizing new states before 1918
##### World wars and interwar
- WWI went global, huge rise in state control
- Western Allies claimed national + liberal democracy cause
    - undermined by alliance with Russia and failure to universalize after victory
    - Wilson’s Fourteen Points = NSD, but unexpected beneficiaries were ethno-nationalists
    - US and USSR both turned inward
- WWII more global and total
    - military globalization + economic “deglobalization” (free trade, fixed exchange rates gone, less migration)
    - new tech brought under state control
    - global processes became _components_ of state-supporting nationalism, not threats to it
- **Fascism**: not insular
    - hated communism and liberalism, rejected conservative elites
    - nation as supra-individual, classless collective, needs strong state, mass mobilization, genius leader
    - imperialist, anti-universalist, vision = power blocs w/ master nation/race over “slave” nations
- Colonial world: mobilization increased subordination and exploitation, but also exposed fragility
    - nationalists invoked liberal, communist, or fascist models
    - nationalism _alone_ couldn’t form nation-states, imperial power actually grew after 1918
    - League dominated by France and Britain (no US), pioneered intl law and administration but couldn’t create peaceful order
- Communism and fascism envisioned super-empires, not a world of nation-states
    - communist states recognized limits (survived), Third Reich = escalating self-destructive radicalism
- Nationalists who welcomed fascists as a way out of imperial rule got “a bad master for a worse one”
- Nationalism could only succeed if old empires dismantled **but not replaced by new ones**
##### Post-1945
- 1941-2: USSR and US forced out of isolationism
- Stalin = expansion as defensive bulwark, but plus Chinese revolution made communism a global force
- US: sole control of nukes at first = _coordinating_ rather than direct power
    - liberal world order (national sovereignty, low tariffs, managed exchange rates, reconstruction)
- 1st wave of decolonization 1947-9 (mainly Asia)
- USSR got nukes, mutual threat perception, US retreated from anti-imperialism
    - nuclear umbrella handed initiative to local states (clients of one superpower)
    - nationalism flourished in contested zones (Middle East, Asia, Africa)
- Europe: stabilizing nation-states in supranational framework (see Ch. 23)
    - ethnic homogenization made ethno-nationalism redundant, civic nationalism acceptable
- Decolonization made the **colonial territory**, not the ethnic nation, the basis of NSD
    - anti-colonial nationalism preferred intl legitimacy to violent liberation
    - explains postcolonial problems (coups, corruption, ethnic politics)
    - national solidarity non-existent under empire
    - bipolar order + sacrosanct state sovereignty meant “system preferred dysfunctional states to new states”
        - Bangladesh from Pakistan = exception that proves the rule
    - changed w/ end of cold war
- _Caution from author_: no linear direction (rise of nationalism then challenge of globalization), judge for yourself if history supports the patterns
- Key Points
    1. no dominant form of nationalism
    2. no simple sequence nationalism → nation-state → world order change
    3. leading states’ ideology matters most (Britain/France, then Germany/Japan by 1900, then US/USSR after 1918 and esp. 1945)
    4. imitation, great power conflict, and nationalist assertion in peripheries produced a world of nation-states
    5. cold war stabilized this order via end of overseas empires
    6. Soviet collapse and Western capitalist crises = rise of nationalist movements
        - W. Europe: “civic” separatism (Scotland, Catalonia), “ethnic” state-supporting (UKIP, Rassemblement National)
        - Central/E. Europe: popular ethnic nationalism
        - beyond Europe: state breakdown sometimes stimulates nationalism, sometimes undermines all political movements or lets radical Islamism overshadow it
#### Case Study 31.1: Germany
- After 1815, weak zone under Habsburg and Hohenzollern
- Liberal nationalists (elite, civic) shifted to **state-supporting** Prussia, mobilized popular support
    - industrialization (rail, coal, iron, steel) = wins over Austria (1866) and France (1870-1)
    - national state proclaimed, not democratic, excluded many German speakers, included many non-German speakers
- Industrialization challenged British hegemony, populist illiberal imperialist nationalism, then WWI
- 1919: ethnic NSD applied to successor states, and to Germany too
    - only “non-German” territories taken (Alsace-Lorraine, Schleswig, Polish Prussia)
- Defeat radicalized ethnic nationalism + mass unemployment = Hitler
    - Nazi aim: race empire in Europe, parity w/ British Empire and US
- After WWII: occupation ended sovereignty, de facto partition
    - supranational liberalism and communism replaced nationalism as official ideology
- Reunification ≠ nationalist revival
    - no strong demand before Soviet collapse
    - East Germans wanted Western affluence and freedom
    - W. Germany’s liberal commitment made rejecting unity impossible
    - Germany strongly pro-EU, populist nationalism after 2008 questioned it, but held in check in 2021 election
- Takeaway: “German” states since 1871 changed hugely in territory, institutions, ideology, position
- Q1: Is German nationalism necessarily a threat to European stability?
- Q2: Did Germany as a whole only become part of the ‘West’ after 1991?

### 31.3 The changing meanings of NSD since 1918

- 3 waves of nation-state formation: after **1918**, **1945**, **1989**
    - each preceded by imperial collapse, each justified by NSD
    - meaning of NSD changes w/ global context (Fig. 31.1, Wimmer)

#### 31.3.1 General outline

- New state needs 3 things
    1. power
    2. legitimacy at home
    3. recognition abroad
- Old Europe: monarchical and religious terms
- Clean break = **US Declaration of Independence** (1776)
    - “decent Respect to the Opinions of Mankind”
    - practical aim = French support, but made case for non-confessional republic
- 19th c. = **national-imperialism**, not anti-imperial nationalism
- 1918 change
    - all major WWI powers multi-ethnic, formal empires (except US)
    - war boosted nationalism in imperial cores, small states (Serbia), “shatter-zones” in Central Europe, colonies
    - post-war peace couldn’t just be an adjustment (unlike 1714-15, 1814-15, Berlin 1878 and 1884)
- 2 newcomers in 1917-18
    1. **Bolsheviks**: published secret treaties, backed national liberation movements
    2. **Wilson**: new open politics, states founded on NSD

#### Case Study 31.4: Nationalisms in South Africa, not South African nationalism

- 4 movements: British imperialism, Afrikaner nationalism, Zulu nationalism, African nationalism
- **British**
    - Cape c. 1800 to defend sea route to Asia
    - diamonds and gold = British immigration
    - English speakers = elites when Union of South Africa formed 1910
- **Afrikaner**
    - boere moved beyond British control (slave emancipation), founded states, ruled Black Africans, lost traumatic war to Britain
    - Depression pushed them into English-dominated cities, separate identity, Afrikaans recognized
    - Blood River centenary 1938
    - own schools, universities, churches
    - mass party, urban intelligentsia (Broederbond), won 1948, apartheid
        - segregation already established, apartheid pushed it to new levels (racial categorizing, outlawed interracial marriage and sex)
- **Zulu**
    - Shaka’s centralized military kingdom, defeated by British after one big success
    - British indirect rule preserved Zulu culture
    - Inkatha used apartheid “homelands” to propose Zulu kingdom
- **African (ANC)**
    - founded 1912 as SANNC, influenced by Indian National Congress (Gandhi)
    - moderate, relied on white sympathy, post-1945 hopes dashed
    - weak, dispersed, suppressed, but made intl connections (SACP and Soviet bloc, frontline states, Western circles)
- Apartheid crisis
    - 63% white vote in 1977 but Black pop growth, ANC resistance, sanctions, need for skilled Black workers, gold price drop
    - failed divide-and-rule (enfranchising Coloured and Asian communities, Inkatha vs ANC violence)
    - Soviet collapse killed “bulwark against communism” argument
    - de Klerk thought ANC was weakened, underestimated it
    - credit to leaders, esp. Mandela
- 1994: ANC 63%, concessions to white minority (civil servants, property rights)
    - “Rainbow Nation” constitution celebrates difference
    - Mandela’s 1995 Rugby World Cup = symbolic, but no unifying patriotism
    - haves vs have-nots gulf deepening
- Q1: Can the ANC be described as nationalist?
- Q2: What made it possible for a white minority to implement the extreme policies of apartheid?

#### Post-1989 wave (section number not in my pages, text starts mid-sentence on p. 500)

- Interventions (UN, NATO, states, NGOs) justified in **universalist** terms (human rights, democracy), not protecting ethnic minorities or state sovereignty
- Nationalists adapt: intl community disapproves of ethno-nationalism, so frame cause as democracy or human rights
    - demand devolution or multiculturalism, not independence
    - often sub-state + transnational (ex. using EU to promote autonomy)
- Nationalism reframed as cultural recognition or affirmative action, so it _appears_ to decline as central legitimation
    - this can stimulate state-supporting ethnic nationalism (defence of dominant group)
    - ex. **Brexit** (23 June 2016), popular, state-supporting, ethnic
        - elite (Conservatives) + populist (UKIP) leadership, media support, refugee crisis, austerity discontent, social media over “experts”
        - “Us against Them” beat the more complex case from a distrusted establishment
- New nationalisms can use transnational networks (diaspora communities from E. Europe and Middle East)
- Contrast of justifications for intervention
    1. world wars era: ethno-national rights
    2. cold war: state sovereignty _blocked_ intervention
    3. now: universalist rights
- New interventions undermine nation-states (culturally, politically, economically, militarily), hits weakest states hardest
- **Nationalism ≠ nation-state**
    - strongest when nation-states feel most threatened
    - globalization can stimulate new subnational and transnational nationalisms
- Key Points
    1. state sovereignty weakened by end of cold war, new nation-state formation, new globalization
    2. provoked wave of state-opposing ethno-nationalisms, sometimes violence and ethnic cleansing
    3. intl recognition favouring civic, territorial legitimations + new interventions pressure nationalism away from ethnic and state-opposing language
    4. state-supporting ethno-nationalism grows in response to globalization threats, can get _stronger_ as the nation-state is weakened
    5. nationalism shifts from state focus to devolution, cultural recognition, transnational links

### 31.4 Conclusion

- Nationalism and global politics have shaped each other since mid-18th c.
    - Anglo-French domination → British hegemony → imperialist conflict and world war → cold war → US hegemony after 1990 → erosion
    - more change coming (ex. rise of China), may not mean more nation-states
- Nationalism often seen as a _passing phase_
    - liberalism and socialism assumed global ties = cosmopolitan world
    - missed that the “**power container**” managing globalization would be the territorial sovereign state
        - new tech for military power, guided development, mass schooling, conscription, media control, social services
        - mobile participatory society swept away legitimations based on privilege, heredity, religion
- Nationalism strongest in imperial cores, then shifts to NSD after 1918, 1945, 1990
    - nation replaced privilege or religion as source of authority
    - creates emotional solidarity among strangers in large societies, which liberalism and socialism couldn’t
- Why did it manage this?
    1. extreme A: expression of pre-existing solidarity (nations, ethnicities, races)
        - honour and prestige matter in IR, makes them unstable
    2. extreme B: manipulated by elites for power
        - fits the view of states as rational actors w/ clear interests
- **Author’s view**: nationalism is a _political idea and practice_ that mirrors the emergence of sovereign territorial states and changes as that order moves through phases
    - shared values get exploited (ex. making Hinduism “Indian”) but only works in context of modern state-formation and global conflict
    - now “common sense”, repetitive and imitative, uniform assumptions enable intl coordination
    - NSD justification alternated between ethnic and civic rhetoric after 1918, mirroring global IR patterns
- World order of nation-states is now questioned, but that’s distinct from nationalism
    - state-supporting: defend threatened nation-state
    - state-opposing: exploit powerful states’ readiness to intervene
    - new forms where the sovereign nation-state isn’t central (devolution, cultural recognition)
- “It is too early to write the obituary of nationalism.”

**Does NSD threaten stable international relations?** (Opposing Opinions 31.1)

- Yes
    - constant challenges to existing states, hardly any state truly “national” (ethnic homogeneity, shared identity)
    - elites claiming to represent repressed minorities can always demand a state
    - external actors amplify demands, ex. Russia in Georgia and Moldova, annexed Crimea, supports “Russian” opponents of Ukraine in the east, fears about other states w/ Russian-speaking minorities
    - globalization erodes territorial sovereignty and national identity
- No
    - states need popular consent, national identity creates the belief the state “belongs” to people
    - Ottoman, Habsburg, Romanov empires failed in 1914-18 partly because they lacked national underpinning (unlike Britain, France, Germany)
    - order of nation-states essential for stability, empires unsustainable once force couldn’t prop them up and people demanded “own” govts
    - globalization exaggerated, territorial states still key power units, national solidarity still top loyalty
        - ex. EU’s lack of solidarity weakens political integration
