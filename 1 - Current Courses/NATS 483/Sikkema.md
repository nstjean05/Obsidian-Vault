## Genesis 1 & the Big Bang
- Opinion 1: Genesis 1 forces us to **reject** the Big Bang
	- 'Young Earth creationism'
	- Anti-realism, instrumentalism, or thorough revision
		- God created the universe with the light in transit
		- Need revisions of current theories
- Opinion 2: Genesis 1 must be **concordant** with Big Bang
	- Hugh Ross, *Reasons to Believe*
		- When God says 'let there be light,' light was actually created in some scientific sense
	- Scripture contains advanced science
- Opinion 3: Genesis 1 tells a **complementary** story
	- Walton, Collins
	- Context: ANE, Exodus
	- The science doesn't need to match precisely
	- Understand the historical context of the first hearers of Gen 1

## Lecture Q&A
1. Genesis uses the language and cosmology of the ancient world (ex. firmament or vault). By what principles should Christians distinguish between culturally-conditioned descriptions and timeless theological claims in a biblical text?
	- As individual Christians, you aren't going to solve the big questions. Rather, look to the conclusions reached by the greater Christian community.


2. Many Christians do not read the "firmament" literally today. Why, then, do the six days of creation generate much more controversy? Are we being consistent in the way we interpret Genesis 1?
	- Some things are problematic, like light from the sun, when you are viewing it as a sequential process.
	- Its easier to ignore things like the firmament that are less convenient.
	- Ask whether plants or humans came first, and the creation accounts will differ

3. When science and traditional interpretation of Scripture appear to conflict, how can we decide whether the scientific understanding should be reconsidered, or the biblical understanding, or both?
	- It is more likely that a scientific understanding which has reached a consensus it true rather than a Biblical understanding which has reached one. Religious consensus are much more informed by their tradition and view as compared to science.
	- At different points in history, we have had different understandings of biblical authority. Copernicus got in trouble because at the time people gave scientific authority to the Bible. But 400 or 1000 years earlier, Copernicus would've been fine.

4. The claim that "God is the author of both Scripture and creation" is often used to reconcile faith and science. In practical terms, how does this principle help us resolve disagreements about origins and cosmology?
	- We should expect there to be agreement in the conclusions we reach, and if we don't, then we should reassess.

5. What questions about the universe can science investigate, and which questions seem to lie beyond scientific methods? Where should we draw the boundary between scientific explanation, philosophy, and theology.
	- Science studies things about the universe, but not really how it came to be.
		- Sometimes this mixes, such as currently not understanding the origin of life, but being able to see a future in which we will.

6. Scientists and Christians alike bring assumptions, commitments, and biases to their interpretation of evidence. What habits or practices can help us evaluate evidence fairly when the conclusions matter personally or spiritually?
	- Often we have biases in how to interpret evidence.
	- In community, with good trusted advisors, and in conversation with Christians in the sciences and other fields.
